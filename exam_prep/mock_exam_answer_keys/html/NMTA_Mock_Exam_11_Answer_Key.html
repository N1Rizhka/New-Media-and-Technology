Part B: Reference answers and marking guidance

B1: How fast the rule search grows (6 marks) Week 3 · Finding rules is a search problem

Reference answer. For d = 5, R = 243 − 64 + 1 = 180. For d = 10, R = 59,049 − 2,048 + 1 = 57,002, more than 300 times as many after doubling the catalogue. The sum picks an antecedent of k items out of d in C(d, k) ways, then a consequent that is any non-empty subset of the d − k items left; the −1 removes the empty subset, and the sides stay disjoint. Step 1, frequent itemset generation, suffers: there are 2d possible itemsets (1,024 for 10 products) to check against minsup. Step 2, rule generation, is cheap because every rule split from a frequent itemset shares that itemset's support, so only confidence is checked, from counts already found.
Marking. 1 mark for 180. 1 mark for 57,002. 1 mark for the antecedent choice C(d, k). 1 mark for the consequent as a non-empty subset of the remaining items (the −1). 1 mark for naming frequent itemset generation as the expensive step and linking it to the exponential number of itemsets. 1 mark for explaining why rule generation is cheap (shared support, confidence from known counts). A correct formula with one arithmetic slip earns the method marks; accept 3d reasoning (left, right or out) as an equivalent explanation of the count.

B2: Ship one setting and defend it (6 marks) Week 4 · Strict, balanced, sweep: one filter, three postures

Reference answer. (i) Spam missed: STRICT 600 − 520 = 80, BALANCED 24, SWEEP 16. BALANCED to STRICT saves 10 − 6 = 4 customers but lets 576 − 520 = 56 more spam stay up. BALANCED to SWEEP catches 584 − 576 = 8 more spam but deletes 62 − 10 = 52 more customers. (ii) Ship BALANCED: it misses 24 spam and deletes 10 genuine comments out of 2,000. Moving to STRICT gives up 56 spam to save 4 customers; moving to SWEEP gives up 52 customers to catch 8 spam. With no reason to price one error far above the other, both steps give up a lot to gain a little, so the dial stays at 0.5. STRICT would be worth it only if one deleted customer cost more than 14 visible spam comments; SWEEP only if 8 more caught spam outweighed 52 lost customers.
Marking. 1 mark for the three missed-spam counts (80, 24, 16). 1 mark for the STRICT step (4 saved, 56 more missed). 1 mark for the SWEEP step (8 more caught, 52 more deleted). 1 mark for naming a setting. 2 marks for the defence: 1 for quoting both error counts of the chosen setting, 1 for arguing from the asymmetric steps or from the relative price of the errors. Another setting can earn full marks if defended with both counts and a stated pricing reason; a defence using accuracy alone earns 0 of the 2 defence marks.

B3: Doubling the ad budget: significant but does it pay? (6 marks) Week 5 · From a number to a decision: three discussion questions

Reference answer. Spend rises by 10 thousand yuan, so the line predicts 1.8 × 10 = 18 more units a week; the interval allows 1.2 × 10 = 12 up to 2.4 × 10 = 24. Extra margin at the point estimate: 18 × 300 = 5,400 yuan, against 10,000 yuan of extra spend; even the top of the interval gives 24 × 300 = 7,200 yuan, still below 10,000. So the association is significant, but on these numbers doubling does not pay. What could go wrong: 20 thousand yuan lies outside the 5-15 range, where the line is only a local approximation; and the coefficient is an association, not a lever (spend may have been high in busy weeks), so the real gain could be smaller still. Recommendation: do not double; if the team wants to learn more, test a modest increase in an experiment first.
Marking. 1 mark for 18 units. 1 mark for the range 12 to 24. 1 mark for the payback comparison: 5,400 versus 10,000, and even 7,200 at the upper end falls short. 2 marks for two explained risks, 1 each (data range / local approximation; association not a lever). 1 mark for a recommendation consistent with the arithmetic. Accept a recommendation to test a smaller increase. Do not accept "p = 0.000 so the spend is guaranteed to pay".

B4: Why Etsy groups products, not people (6 marks) Week 6 · Case: Etsy and a buyer's lasting interests

Reference answer. Etsy follows the products first, then the buyer: product features (images and descriptions of each listing) are clustered with K-means into interest groups (about 1,000 groups of similar listings); a buyer's history (purchases over time) is then read through those groups; and the result feeds recommendations of items linked to the buyer's lasting interests. Etsy needs many fine groups because the job is recommendation, matching buyers to similar listings, whereas a campaign plan needs only as many segments as it can run campaigns for; 'similar' is a business choice that depends on the job. A single purchase alone suggests 'more of the same item', which is often useless, but several purchases from one interest group reveal a lasting interest, so the marketplace can recommend different listings from that interest and give the buyer reasons to return.
Marking. Award 2 marks for the four stages in order (product features, interest groups, buyer history, recommendations); 1 mark if two or three are given. Award 2 marks for explaining why about 1,000 groups suit recommendation while campaigns need few, 1 mark for the recommendation job and 1 for linking the number of groups to the business purpose. Award 2 marks for the after-first-purchase logic: 1 for 'lasting interest, not one-time need' from repeated purchases in a group, 1 for recommending different items instead of more of the same. Do not credit answers that say Etsy clusters buyers by demographics.

B5: A billing topic is not a billing complaint (6 marks) Week 7 · Topic models and latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)

Reference answer. (1) P(charge) = 0.5 × 0.02 + 0.3 × 0.30 + 0.2 × 0.05 = 0.01 + 0.09 + 0.01 = 0.11 (11%). Topic 2 contributes 0.09 of the 0.11, although it is only 30% of the review, because "charge" is far more likely within it. (2) LDA only produces word probabilities; the label is added by a person, and a topic groups words, not attitudes. Topic 2 tells her that people talk about billing, but it can mix praise ("the annual plan is good value"), complaints ("I was charged twice") and questions ("how do I renew my plan?"). A large topic is not evidence of a price problem. (3) She should inspect topic 2's top words and read original reviews with a high share of it, check their sentiment, and compare fits with different K or preprocessing before acting; the action could be fixing double charges or clearer renewal information rather than a price cut.
Marking. Award 1 mark for the setup and 1 mark for 0.11. Award 1 mark for identifying topic 2 as the main contributor with the reason. Award 1 mark for explaining that the model does not name topics or measure sentiment. Award 1 mark for showing that a topic can contain praise, complaints and questions (at least two of the three with examples). Award 1 mark for a concrete check before acting: reading original reviews and checking sentiment (comparing K or repeated fits is acceptable as an addition, not alone). Error carried forward applies to the dominant-topic statement.

Part C: Case analysis

Case 1: Brushwood Art Supplies Week 3 · Real baskets: the bakery and the doilies decoy, Lift: the number that compares against chance, A rule and its two strength numbers

C1.1 (8 marks).

Model answer. Minimum count = .04 × 3,200 = 128 orders. gold_leaf → gilding_size: support = 150/3,200 ≈ .047 (clears .04, 150 ≥ 128); confidence = 150/200 = .75; s(gilding_size) = 240/3,200 = .075, so lift = .75/.075 = 10.00. canvas_board → acrylic_set: confidence = 240/480 = .50; s(acrylic_set) = 800/3,200 = .25, so lift = .50/.25 = 2.00. Ranking by lift: gold_leaf → gilding_size (10.00), watercolour_pad → round_brush (3.50), canvas_board → acrylic_set (2.00). Gilding size is rare overall (7.5% of orders) because almost nobody needs it without gold leaf, yet three in four gold-leaf orders contain it; like matcha powder and its whisk, a niche item paired with its tool gives a large gap over chance.
Marking. 1 mark for 128. 1 mark for gold-leaf support .047. 1 mark for its confidence .75. 1 mark for its lift 10.00. 1 mark for canvas confidence .50. 1 mark for canvas lift 2.00. 1 mark for the correct ranking. 1 mark for explaining the top lift through the low base rate of gilding size combined with high confidence. Error carried forward into the ranking.

C1.2 (6 marks).

Model answer. Confidence = 180/576 = .3125 ≈ .31; s(masking_tape) = 960/3,200 = .30, so lift = .3125/.30 ≈ 1.04. Masking tape is in 30% of orders, so it appears alongside almost everything and any rule pointing at it has a sizeable confidence. But lift divides by that same high base rate, and every lift lies between .87 and 1.08, at the chance level: buying a brush, or anything else, does not make tape more likely. Masking tape is a decoy: it rides along with everything and predicts nothing, so it should not anchor the bundles.
Marking. 1 mark for confidence .31. 1 mark for lift 1.04. 1 mark for explaining that high support raises confidence but lift divides by the base rate. 1 mark for interpreting the .87 to 1.08 range as chance level. 2 marks for rejecting the suggestion as a decoy argument (1 for the verdict, 1 for linking it to the numbers). A rejection based only on "tape is cheap" earns 0 for the verdict marks.

C1.3 (6 marks).

Model answer. Bundle 1: gold leaf with gilding size, the strongest association (lift 10.00, confidence .75), with support .047, thin but real at 150 orders. Bundle 2: watercolour pad with round brush (lift 3.50, confidence .63) and the highest support of the real rules (.085), so it reaches more customers. Canvas board with acrylic set (lift 2.00) is a reasonable placement idea but weaker. Masking tape should not be included. Limitation: these are associations in six months of past orders, not causes, so the bundles may not raise sales; the store should test them, for example against the current home page.
Marking. 2 marks for the gold-leaf bundle justified by lift (1) with support or confidence noted (1). 1 mark for a justified second bundle (watercolour-brush preferred; canvas-acrylic acceptable if justified by lift above 1). 1 mark for excluding masking tape. 2 marks for a limitation with its consequence (a bare limitation earns 1). Do not award bundle marks for choices justified by support alone.

Case 2: Petalpost Florist Week 5 · Three yardsticks, the split of the total miss, and R squared, Residual plots, and residuals on new weeks, The wiggly line and the tailor's suit, Training fit versus prediction error, A complexity budget for your team: three discussion questions

C2.1 (8 marks).

Model answer. R2: line 1 − 1,200/4,000 = 0.70; quadratic 1 − 560/4,000 = 0.86; degree-8 1 − 40/4,000 = 0.99. Line SSR = 4,000 − 1,200 = 2,800 (2,800/4,000 = 0.70). The line explains about 70 percent of the week-to-week variation in orders; 30 percent is left in the residuals. Its residuals are positive at low and high fitted values and negative in the middle: a curve remains, so the line is systematically too low at the ends and too high in the middle. Something systematic is left behind; the relationship bends, and the course's advice is "if the truth curves, add curves" (polynomial regression), which the quadratic does. But on the same training weeks, lower SSE always means higher R2, and more flexibility always lowers training SSE, so R2 rising to 0.99 is guaranteed and may be fitting noise; the choice must be made on new weeks.
Marking. 3 marks for the three R2 values, 1 each. 1 mark for SSR = 2,800. 1 mark for reading 0.70 as share of variation explained. 1 mark for reading the residual pattern as a curve / systematic structure left (line too low at ends, too high in middle). 1 mark for the response: add curves (polynomial term). 1 mark for explaining that training R2 always rises with flexibility, so it cannot pick the model. Do not accept "the residual curve means the errors are random".

C2.2 (6 marks).

Model answer. Line: 150 − 160 = −10, 180 − 175 = +5, 210 − 200 = +10, 240 − 250 = −10; total (−10)2 + 52 + 102 + (−10)2 = 100 + 25 + 100 + 100 = 325. Quadratic: +5, −5, +5, −5; total 25 × 4 = 100. Degree-8: −20, +30, −30, +35; total 400 + 900 + 900 + 1,225 = 3,425. The quadratic's residuals sum to zero, but that is cancelling of over- and under-estimates, not zero error: it still missed every week by 5 orders, which the squared total of 100 shows.
Marking. 2 marks per model (line, quadratic, degree-8): 1 for the four correctly signed residuals, 1 for the total (325, 100, 3,425). Within these, award the quadratic's total mark only if the answer does not claim zero error from the zero residual sum; a comment on cancelling is expected. Reversed signs throughout with correct totals: lose the residual marks for that model only. Error carried forward into totals.

C2.3 (6 marks).

Model answer. Ship the quadratic: its total squared prediction error on the four later weeks is 100, against 325 for the line and 3,425 for the degree-8 model. The degree-8 model's R2 of 0.99 is training fit on the same weeks it was built from; like a suit cut to one customer's every bump, it followed the noise of those 20 weeks and predicts new weeks badly. That is high variance, an unstable fit: overfitting. The straight line is too simple: its curved residuals show a missed pattern (high bias), so it underfits and errs more than the quadratic on new weeks. The quadratic sits nearest the sweet spot where bias2 + variance is lowest. Limitation: four later weeks are a small sample, and seasonal peaks such as holidays may favour a different model, so the choice should be re-checked on more new weeks.
Marking. 1 mark for choosing the quadratic. 1 mark for supporting it with the comparison 100 versus 325 and 3,425 on the same later weeks. 2 marks for the degree-8 model: 1 for distinguishing training R2 from prediction on new weeks, 1 for naming overfitting / variance (or the tailor's suit) with a correct explanation. 1 mark for the line as underfitting / bias, linked to its curved residuals. 1 mark for one explained limitation. Carry forward: a choice that follows correctly from the student's own totals earns the choice marks.
