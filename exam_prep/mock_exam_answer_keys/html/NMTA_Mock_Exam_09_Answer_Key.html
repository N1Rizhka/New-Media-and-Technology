Part B: Reference answers and marking guidance

B1: Chi-square: new versus returning viewers (6 marks) Week 1 · The chi-square test: are two attributes linked?

Reference answer. Expected counts: new and bought 240 × 100 / 400 = 60; new and not 240 × 300 / 400 = 180; returning and bought 160 × 100 / 400 = 40; returning and not 160 × 300 / 400 = 120. Each gap is 6 in size, so χ2 = 36/60 + 36/180 + 36/40 + 36/120 = 0.6 + 0.2 + 0.9 + 0.3 = 2.0. df = (2 − 1) × (2 − 1) = 1. Since 2.0 < 2.71 there is no evidence of a link between viewer type and buying at α = 0.1. Returning viewers bought a little more often (46/160 = 28.75% versus 54/240 = 22.5%), but this sample does not show that difference clearly; it does not prove that viewer type is unrelated to buying, and a larger sample could reach a different verdict.
Marking. 1 mark for all four expected counts (60, 180, 40, 120). 2 marks for χ2: 1 for the correct (f − e)2/e terms and 1 for 2.0. 1 mark for df = 1. 1 mark for comparing 2.0 with 2.71 and concluding no evidence of a link. 1 mark for stating this is not proof of no relationship (or that a larger sample might differ). Do not award the last mark for “new and returning viewers buy the same”. Error carried forward: a wrong χ2 followed by a correct comparison earns the comparison mark.

B2: Share the leader's slot or dig out a new one? (6 marks) Week 2 · Five ways to position the brand

Reference answer. Plan A is share: Nookbrew coexists with the leader's slot as the try-harder number two. Plan B is excavate: it finds an attribute buyers care about that nobody owns and takes it. Plan A can reach 200,000 × 45% × (1 − 80%) = 18,000 buyers, while Plan B can reach 200,000 × 15% = 30,000 buyers with no owner to fight. Plan B is the better recommendation for a start-up, and the leader will probably consolidate, defending and deepening its "strongest" slot. A limitation is that stated importance in a survey is not purchase behaviour: some of the 15% may not switch or pay more for low acid.
Marking. Award 1 mark for share for Plan A. Award 1 mark for excavate for Plan B. Award 2 marks for the two reachable-buyer figures, 18,000 and 30,000, 1 mark each. Award 1 mark for a recommendation supported by the figures, together with consolidate as the leader's strategy. Award 1 mark for one valid limitation of the survey figures. Accept first place for Plan B only if the answer argues that the low-acid slot is empty and Nookbrew is first to claim it, and still contrasts it with Plan A. Carry arithmetic errors forward into the recommendation.

B3: A best-seller as a bundle anchor (6 marks) Week 3 · Real baskets: the bakery and the doilies decoy

Reference answer. s(potting soil) = 960/3,000 = .32. c(orchid → soil) = 85/250 = .34, so lift = .34/.32 ≈ 1.06. s(bark mix) = 150/3,000 = .05. c(orchid → bark mix) = 60/250 = .24, so lift = .24/.05 = 4.80. Potting soil is a decoy: it is in about a third of all orders, so it co-occurs with almost everything, but all its lifts sit near 1, so no item makes customers more likely to buy it. It rides along with everything and predicts nothing, so it should not anchor bundles. Bundles should be built on pairs with lift well above 1, such as orchid with bark mix (4.80), even though their support is lower.
Marking. 1 mark for c(orchid → soil) = .34. 1 mark for lift ≈ 1.06. 1 mark for c(orchid → bark mix) = .24 and lift 4.80 (both needed). 1 mark for identifying potting soil as popular but not associated (lift near 1 in every pair). 1 mark for explaining why: high base rate raises confidence but lift divides by it. 1 mark for a recommendation: do not anchor on soil; build bundles on high-lift pairs such as orchid and bark mix. An answer that keeps soil as anchor because "everyone buys it" earns 0 for the last two marks.

B4: Same brand, two posts, two dials (6 marks) Week 4 · Two errors, two prices

Reference answer. Post 1: the miss is the expensive error. Buyers ready to click and pay are an easy target for scam links posing as the sale; a scam left up costs followers money, and they blame Hearthly, while engagement starts to look fake. Lower the threshold (for example toward 0.3): recall rises, at the cost of more genuine comments deleted. Post 2: the false alarm is the expensive error. A genuine buyer asking about a late order who watches the question vanish feels silenced, files a support ticket or complains in public, and may not buy again. Raise the threshold (for example toward 0.8): precision rises, at the cost of more spam left visible. The same model therefore runs two settings: price the two errors separately, then set the dial.
Marking. 2 marks for Post 1: 1 for identifying the miss as the expensive error, 1 for who pays (followers who click, the brand's trust or campaign). 2 marks for Post 2: 1 for identifying the false alarm as the expensive error, 1 for who pays (the silenced buyer, support desk, repeat purchases). 1 mark for moving the dials in the matching directions (lower for Post 1, raise for Post 2). 1 mark for one stated cost for each post (more genuine deleted on Post 1; more spam visible on Post 2); both are needed. A different pricing earns the first marks only if it is argued from who reads each post; moving a dial without a stated pricing earns no direction mark.

B5: Three statements about p = 0.03 (6 marks) Week 5 · The p-value and the decision rule

Reference answer. The p-value is the probability, computed assuming H0 (true coefficient zero) is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, on either side for a two-tail p. Colleague 1 is wrong: p assumes H0 is true, so it cannot be the probability that H0 is true. Colleague 2 is wrong: p says only that the association is unlikely to be pure noise; whether it pays for a studio needs the coefficient in its units, its interval and the costs. Colleague 3 is right: 0.03 is below 0.05 but above 0.01, so the decision depends on the false-alarm budget fixed before the test.
Marking. 1 mark for the definition including "assuming H0 is true" and "at least this extreme". 2 marks for colleague 1: 1 for "wrong", 1 for the reason. 2 marks for colleague 2: 1 for "wrong", 1 for explaining that p does not measure business size (needs coefficient, units or costs). 1 mark for colleague 3 right with 0.01 < 0.03 < 0.05. A verdict without reason earns only the verdict mark.

Part C: Case analysis

Case 1: Pebblefit Week 1 · Entropy: how much information is in an answer, Preprocessing: aggregate, discretize, binarize, Data quality: platform data is not neutral

C1.1 (7 marks).

Model answer. Plan: Free 3, Basic 3, Premium 3, each share 1/3. H = 3 × (1/3 × 1.585) = 1.585, about 1.58 bits. Device: phone 6, tablet 3, shares 2/3 and 1/3. H = 2/3 × 0.585 + 1/3 × 1.585 = 0.390 + 0.528 = 0.918, about 0.92 bits. Plan is harder to guess: it has three equally likely values, while for device you would guess phone and be right two times out of three, so an answer about plan tells you more. Member ID would indeed have H = log2 9 = 3.17 bits, because every member is unique, but entropy measures how unpredictable an attribute is, not whether it explains an outcome; an ID carries no reason why a member stays.
Marking. 2 marks for Plan: 1 for the shares and setup, 1 for 1.58 (accept 1.58 or 1.59). 2 marks for Device: 1 for the setup, 1 for 0.92. 1 mark for naming Plan as harder to guess with the reason (more, equally likely values). 1 mark for Member ID's high entropy coming from uniqueness (3.17 bits not required). 1 mark for stating that high entropy does not mean explanatory or business value. Error carried forward on arithmetic.

C1.2 (7 marks).

Model answer. (i) Equal width: range 300 − 0 = 300, width 100. Low (0 to under 100): M1 to M7; Mid (100 to under 200): M8; High (200 to 300): M9. (ii) Equal frequency: nine members, three per grade in sorted order. Low: M1, M2, M3 (0, 15, 30); Mid: M4, M5, M6 (45, 60, 75); High: M7, M8, M9 (90, 120, 300). The single extreme value of 300 stretches the range, so equal width puts seven of nine members in Low and leaves High with one member. Equal frequency (or a k-means cut in the natural gap below 300) gives more useful grades for targeting, so I would use equal frequency here.
Marking. 1 mark for width 100. 2 marks for the equal-width grades (all three correct 2; one grade wrong 1). 2 marks for the equal-frequency grades (same rule). 2 marks for the choice: 1 for preferring equal frequency or k-means, 1 for explaining that the extreme value 300 stretches equal-width intervals. Accept equal width only with an argument that M9 is first corrected.

C1.3 (6 marks).

Model answer. M9's 300 minutes is measurement error on the collection side: the recorded value (classes playing overnight) differs from the actual workout time. M4's age is a missing value, also on the collection side. Age is not needed for the activity grade, so I would ignore the gap and keep M4 in the sample; the cost is that any age summary uses only eight members (dropping M4 would lose one of only nine members). Recommendation: correct or exclude M9's overnight minutes first, then send the coach session to the members in the equal-frequency High grade after correction (currently M7, M8 and M9), checking that M9 really belongs there. Limitation: nine members is a very small sample, and the export predates the schedule redesign, so the grades may already be out of date (timeliness).
Marking. 1 mark for measurement error for M9; 1 mark for collection side (for both M9 and M4). 1 mark for missing values with a chosen option (drop, estimate or ignore) and 1 mark for its correct cost. 1 mark for a recommendation that handles M9 before targeting. 1 mark for one limitation (small sample, timeliness after the redesign, minutes do not show intent to stay). Accept “noise” for M9 only if the answer argues the error is random; the reference is measurement error.

Case 2: Larkspur Meal Kits Week 7 · Topic models and latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA), Sentiment analysis at scale

C2.1 (8 marks).

Model answer. Each topic is a probability distribution over the same words, so each column sums to 1. T3 without recipe: 0.10 + 0.05 + 0.05 + 0.02 + 0.30 = 0.52, so P(recipe | T3) = 1 − 0.52 = 0.48. P(late | R7) = 0.6 × 0.40 + 0.1 × 0.05 + 0.3 × 0.05 = 0.240 + 0.005 + 0.015 = 0.26 (26%). T1 contributes 0.24 of the 0.26: it is the largest share of R7 and "late" is very likely within it. Candidate labels: T1 (late, driver) = Delivery timing; T2 (box, fresh) = Packaging and freshness; T3 (recipe, easy) = Cooking and recipes. The labels are added by people after inspecting top words and original reviews; the model does not name topics.
Marking. Award 1 mark for 0.48 and 1 mark for the reason that each topic's probabilities sum to 1. Award 1 mark for the correct setup of the sum and 1 mark for 0.26. Award 1 mark for T1 as the main contributor with the 0.24 figure or equivalent reason. Award 2 marks for three sensible labels supported by top words (1 mark for two correct). Award 1 mark for noting that labels are a human judgement after inspection. Error carried forward applies from a wrong missing value.

C2.2 (6 marks).

Model answer. S1 is praise (a product strength: reliable, polite drivers). S2 and S4 are complaints (reports to investigate: late boxes, warm chicken, a box left in the rain). S3 and S5 are questions (information needs about arrival times and changing slots). A topic groups words that appear together, not attitudes: praise, complaints and questions about delivery all use words such as driver, late and box, so they fall in the same topic. A large T1 shows that customers talk about delivery a lot, not how they feel or what they want; sentiment and reading of the reviews are needed before acting.
Marking. Award 3 marks for the classification: 1 for S1 as praise, 1 for S2 and S4 as complaints, 1 for S3 and S5 as questions. Award 1 mark for stating the business meaning of the three types (strength, report to investigate, information need). Award 2 marks for explaining why topic size is not a problem measure: 1 for topics grouping words not attitudes, 1 for needing sentiment or reading reviews before acting.

C2.3 (6 marks).

Model answer. Do not switch couriers on the basis of topic size. Next steps: run sentiment analysis on reviews with a high T1 share, ideally at aspect level, to measure how many are complaints about lateness or handling versus praise and questions; read a random sample of these reviews; then decide. The questions in S3 and S5 suggest a cheaper action as well: publish clear delivery-time windows and slot-change rules. On K: lower perplexity need not produce clearer topics, so K = 8 should not be adopted just for its fit; compare K values, preprocessing and repeated fits and keep the version whose topics staff can interpret and use (here K = 3). Limitation: topic mixtures are estimates that can change with K, preprocessing and repeated fits, and Exhibit 2 shows only five reviews, so they illustrate meanings but do not measure how common each is.
Marking. Award 1 mark for rejecting the immediate courier switch with the topic-size reason. Award 2 marks for specific next steps: 1 for sentiment or aspect analysis of T1 reviews, 1 for a concrete action or check (reading a sample, publishing delivery windows, investigating late deliveries). Award 1 mark for the K decision with the perplexity point. Award 1 mark for mentioning comparison across K, preprocessing or repeated fits. Award 1 mark for one limitation explained (unstable topics, five illustrative reviews, labels are human judgements). A recommendation to "improve delivery" without a concrete step earns at most 1 of the 2 next-step marks.
