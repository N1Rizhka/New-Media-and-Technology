Part A: Answers and explanations

Part B: Reference answers and marking guidance

B1: One rule, two decisions, two threshold settings (6 marks) Week 3 · Two thresholds and the four readings

Reference answer. Support = 12/3,000 = .004 and confidence = 12/15 = .80. For the marketing team the rule fails, because .004 is far below minsup .05, even though .80 clears .60. For the product team it passes both thresholds (.004 ≥ .002 and .80 ≥ .75). The thresholds come from the decision, not the data: a home-page banner must touch many customers, so it needs common patterns, while a note shown only to stove viewers can accept low support but must usually be right. Caution: the confidence rests on only 15 stove orders, so it is a low-support, high-confidence rule; if just two fewer of those orders had a spork, confidence would fall to 10/15 ≈ .67.
Marking. 1 mark for support .004. 1 mark for confidence .80. 1 mark for marketing: fails because of support (must name support as the reason). 1 mark for product team: passes both. 1 mark for explaining that thresholds follow the decision (reach versus reliability), not the data. 1 mark for one valid caution tied to thin support, such as the small σ(X) of 15 making confidence unstable, or the rule being "seductive but thin". Accept a caution that lift should also be checked if it is explained. Do not award the last mark for a generic "more data is better".

B2: A 96 percent filter that misses most spam (6 marks) Week 4 · The accuracy trap

Reference answer. (i) Deleting nothing is right on all 4,750 genuine messages: 4,750 / 5,000 = 0.95. (ii) Scam messages = 250; TP = 100, FN = 150, so recall = 100 / 250 = 0.40. (Check of the claim: (100 + 4,700) / 5,000 = 0.96.) (iii) The stream is lopsided, so the do-nothing filter already scores 0.95; the vendor's 0.96 is only 0.01 above doing nothing and hides that 150 of 250 scam messages, 60 percent, stay up. Accuracy gives every verdict the same weight and does not say which mistakes were made. LoopTalk should ask for the two errors separately on held-out messages: scam messages missed (150, recall 0.40) and genuine messages deleted (50), and judge them against what each error costs.
Marking. 1 mark for 0.95 with working. 1 mark for recall 0.40 (accept 40 percent). 2 marks for explaining the trap: 1 for comparing 0.96 with the 0.95 baseline (lopsided stream), 1 for stating that accuracy hides that most scam messages are missed. 2 marks for the request: 1 for asking for both error counts (or precision and recall) rather than accuracy, 1 for measuring them on held-out data or pricing each error. Do not award the trap marks for saying only "accuracy is not enough" without the baseline or the missed scams.

B3: Changing both rulers, and a negative intercept (6 marks) Week 5 · Units, and the intercept as an anchor

Reference answer. One hour is 60 minutes, so one more hour comes with 50 × 60 = 3,000 yuan, which is 3.0 thousand yuan: new slope 3.0 thousand yuan per hour. The intercept is in target units only, so −800 yuan becomes −0.8 thousand yuan. Old units: −800 + 50 × 100 = 4,200 yuan. New units: −0.8 + 3.0 × (100/60) = −0.8 + 5.0 = 4.2 thousand yuan, the same 4,200 yuan: the number moves with the unit; the shop does not. The intercept is the fitted revenue at zero minutes. Zero minutes lies outside the 30-180 minute range, so it is an anchor that places a locally fitted line, not a forecast of a loss.
Marking. 1 mark for the slope conversion with working; 1 mark for 3.0 thousand yuan per hour. 1 mark for −0.8 thousand yuan. 1 mark for both predictions equal to 4,200 yuan / 4.2 thousand yuan, with the remark that only the ruler changed. 1 mark for the intercept as the fitted value at zero minutes; 1 mark for anchor / local approximation with zero outside the 30-180 range. Do not accept 0.05 or 50,000 for the slope. Error carried forward: a wrong slope used consistently in the prediction loses only the slope marks.

B4: Three viewers, three silhouette zones (6 marks) Week 6 · The silhouette score

Reference answer. P: s = (4.8 − 1.2) / 4.8 = 3.6 / 4.8 = 0.75, well inside its own cluster, a confident member. Q: s = (3.2 − 3.0) / 3.2 = 0.2 / 3.2 ≈ 0.06, on the boundary between two clusters, a coin flip. R: the larger value is a = 4.5, so s = (3.6 − 4.5) / 4.5 = −0.9 / 4.5 = −0.20; R sits closer to the neighbouring cluster and was probably filed in the wrong one. R deserves the closest look, since a campaign aimed at its cluster may not fit it; Q is also uncertain. Many such learners would suggest the clusters themselves are weak.
Marking. Award 1 mark each for the three silhouettes: 0.75, 0.06 (accept 0.0625) and −0.20. Award 2 marks for the three readings (confident / boundary / misfiled): all three correct earns 2, two correct earns 1. Award 1 mark for identifying R (or R and then Q) as needing a second look, with the reason that it is closer to another cluster. A value of −0.25 for R (dividing by b) earns no calculation mark for R, but the reading mark is available if the negative sign is interpreted correctly.

B5: A split brand term and dictionary maintenance (6 marks) Week 7 · English and Chinese tokenization, and the dictionary

Reference answer. (1) With the default dictionary the product name is split into 星芒 and 环, and 环 is glued to the particle 的 to form 环的, a wrong token. So the product is never counted as itself (the count of 0 is a tokenization artefact, not an absence of mentions), and 的 joins the wrong token. (2) Add 星芒环 to the dictionary, one entry per line, before counting; the term then stays one token and 的 returns to marking possession. (3) The analytics team maintaining the dictionary must add the new hashtag and product code before the campaign's reviews are counted. Common words such as 续航 already segment correctly, but coined terms do not; an outdated dictionary causes segmentation errors that flow into every later count, weight and sentiment score.
Marking. Award 1 mark for the split product name and 1 mark for 的 attached to the wrong token (环的). Award 1 mark for explaining that the count of 0 understates real mentions. Award 1 mark for adding the term to the dictionary before counting. Award 1 mark for naming a responsibility to update the dictionary for new campaign terms (hashtags, product codes). Award 1 mark for the consequence: segmentation errors affect later counts, weights or sentiment. Answers that propose switching to the English tokenizer earn no fix mark.

Part C: Case analysis

Case 1: Wickford Candles Week 3 · The Apriori principle and candidate pruning, Lift: the number that compares against chance

C1.1 (8 marks).

Model answer. Minimum count = .12 × 250 = 30 orders. Matchboxes (E, 26 orders) are infrequent, so every set containing E is pruned; the four pairs AE, BE, CE and DE are never counted. Frequent pairs: AB (48), AC (33), AD (41) and BC (36); BD (22) and CD (27) are below 30. Discard ABD because BD is infrequent; discard ACD because CD is infrequent; discard BCD because BD (and CD) are infrequent. ABC survives, since AB, AC and BC all reach 30, and remains a candidate to be counted. Principle: support is anti-monotone. Every order holding a larger set also holds each of its subsets, so a set can never appear in more orders than any subset; if one subset is infrequent, the set and all its supersets are infrequent.
Marking. 1 mark for 30. 1 mark for pruning E with the four E-pairs never counted (accept "4 pairs saved"). 1 mark for the four frequent pairs, with BD and CD identified as infrequent. 1 mark for ABD with BD. 1 mark for ACD with CD. 1 mark for BCD with BD or CD (naming both earns no extra mark). 1 mark for ABC kept as a candidate. 1 mark for stating the anti-monotone principle. Each discard needs both the set and a correct infrequent pair. Carry forward an incorrect minimum count if the classification follows from it.

C1.2 (6 marks).

Model answer. ABC appears in 28 orders, below the minimum of 30, so it is not frequent and the colleague's claim was wrong. Passing the pruning only shows that ABC could be frequent: frequent pairs are a necessary condition, not a guarantee that all three items occur together often enough. For {B} → {C}: support = 36/250 = .144; confidence = 36/70 ≈ .51; s(C) = 64/250 = .256, so lift = .514/.256 ≈ 2.01 (or 36 × 250 / (70 × 64) = 9,000/4,480). Wick-trimmer buyers take a snuffer about twice as often as a random customer: the two go together.
Marking. 1 mark for ABC not frequent (28 < 30). 1 mark for explaining that surviving pruning is necessary but not sufficient. 1 mark for support .144. 1 mark for confidence .51 (accept .514). 1 mark for lift 2.01 (accept 2.0). 1 mark for reading lift above 1 as going together, about twice chance. Error carried forward for the lift if the confidence was miscalculated but the method is right.

C1.3 (6 marks).

Model answer. c(A → D) = 41/120 ≈ .342 and s(D) = 95/250 = .38, so lift = .342/.38 ≈ .90 (41 × 250 / (120 × 95) = 10,250/11,400). A lift below 1 means candle buyers take wax melts slightly less often than a random customer: the two substitute, which makes sense because both scent a room. Recommendation: test a "candle care" bundle or side-by-side placement of the trimmer and snuffer (lift about 2.01, support .144), and do not bundle candles with wax melts; show them as alternatives instead. Limitation: the rules show association, not causation, from one quarter's 250 orders, so a bundle test should measure whether it actually lifts sales.
Marking. 1 mark for the setup and 1 mark for lift .90 (accept .899). 1 mark for reading lift below 1 as substitution. 1 mark for a recommendation on B-C supported by its lift above 1. 1 mark for not bundling A-D, with alternatives as an acceptable use. 1 mark for one explained limitation (association is not causation; one quarter; small sample; thresholds chosen by the team). Do not award the A-D mark for "bundle because 41 orders already buy both".

Case 2: Tidewave Audio Week 7 · TF-IDF: weighting words by frequency and rarity, N-grams: keeping local word order, Stop words: what to remove and what to keep

C2.1 (8 marks).

Model answer. Document frequencies (N = 5): speaker 5, loud 3 (r1, r2, r4), app 2 (r3, r5), crackle 1 (r3). IDF: speaker = ln(5/5) = 0; loud = ln(5/3) = 0.51; app = ln(5/2) = 0.92; crackle = ln(5/1) = 1.61. Weights in r3: speaker = 1 × 0 = 0; app = 2 × 0.92 = 1.84; crackle = 2 × 1.61 = 3.22. "crackle" is the most distinctive term in r3: it is repeated in r3 and appears in no other review. "speaker" appears in every review, so its IDF is ln(1) = 0 and it distinguishes no review, whatever its count. "app" is second: it is repeated but also occurs in r5.
Marking. Award 1 mark for the four document frequencies. Award 3 marks for the four IDF values (1 mark for speaker = 0; 1 mark for loud = 0.51; 1 mark for app = 0.92 and crackle = 1.61 together). Award 2 marks for the three r3 weights (all three correct = 2; two correct = 1; accept 1.83 for app). Award 1 mark for identifying crackle as most distinctive with the joint TF and IDF reason. Award 1 mark for explaining speaker's zero weight through df = N. Apply error carried forward: a wrong IDF used correctly loses only the IDF mark. Log base 10 throughout loses the IDF marks but can earn the remaining marks if applied consistently.

C2.2 (6 marks).

Model answer. A unigram count shows "loud" in three reviews but cannot tell how it is used. The bigrams of r2 are speaker not, not loud, loud enough, enough outdoors. "not loud" shows that r2 complains about loudness, so only r1 and r4 praise it: 2 of the 5 reviews, with one explicit complaint. The proposal overstates the evidence. If "not" had been removed as a stop word, r2 would read as "speaker loud enough outdoors", turning a complaint into apparent praise, and even with "not" kept, a unigram count records only that a negator appeared somewhere in the review. Bigrams keep local word order, so "not loud" becomes its own feature. The cost is a larger, sparser vocabulary: more columns, most of them zero for any one review.
Marking. Award 1 mark for the four bigrams of r2. Award 1 mark for identifying "not loud" as the bigram that reverses the meaning. Award 1 mark for concluding that only two reviews praise loudness, so the proposal overstates the evidence. Award 1 mark for explaining that removing "not" would turn the complaint into apparent praise. Award 1 mark for explaining that unigram counts lose word order while bigrams keep it. Award 1 mark for the larger, sparser vocabulary cost. Do not award the conclusion mark for simply repeating "loud appears three times".

C2.3 (6 marks).

Model answer. Do not fund the redesign yet. A high TF-IDF weight means "crackle" is distinctive in r3, not that crackling is common or that the hardware is the cause. Reading r3 shows the crackling started after the app update and the app froze; r5 also says the app is slow. A supported next step is to have the app team investigate the recent update (for example, test playback with the previous and current app version) and to search all recent reviews for crackle and app terms to see how often they occur together. Limitation: TF-IDF measures distinctiveness within one review relative to the collection, not frequency across customers or sentiment; with five reviews, one mention cannot show how widespread the problem is.
Marking. Award 1 mark for a clear decision not to fund the redesign now (or to defer it), and 1 mark for justifying it with distinctiveness not being frequency or cause. Award 2 marks for a specific next step tied to Exhibit 1: 1 for linking crackling to the app update in r3 (or app problems in r5), 1 for a concrete investigation (test the update, check how many reviews mention it). Award 2 marks for a limitation with its consequence, such as a small sample, weights not measuring frequency, or weights not measuring sentiment. A recommendation to "improve the speaker" without a concrete step earns no next-step marks.
