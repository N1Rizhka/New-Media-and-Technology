What the field is: one definition, three branches

On the exam: slides 23-27

The lecture gives a working definition you should be able to write from memory: new media technology and analytics studies how digital media and data analytics reshape business, information systems, and marketing. The definition has a cause and three things it affects. The cause is two forces together: digital media (feeds, livestreams, AI content, extended reality) and data analytics (the methods that turn platform logs into decisions). Those forces change three fields, and the slides call them the three branches.

The business branch asks how firms create and capture value. On slide 25 it is made concrete as platform business models, and pricing, channels and monetisation. A typical business question is "how does a livestream platform make money, and who keeps the margin?" The information systems branch covers the technologies and data infrastructures the field is built on. Slide 26 lists platforms and recommendation, data pipelines, and AI and AI-generated content (AIGC). A typical question is "what does the recommendation system log, and how does that data reach an analyst?" The marketing branch is about how organisations reach, understand, and measure people. Slide 27 lists consumers, engagement, targeting, and campaign measurement. A typical question is "did this campaign bring buyers, or only views?"

The debrief on slide 24 makes one point: "each field is an entry point, not a fence." A business student starts from the cases and the four pillars, an information systems student from data types, pipelines and the method weeks, and a marketing student from the Week 2 frameworks and the campaign cases. The course connects all three, and that connection is the definition. Slide 28, which places course topics on the branches, is a timetable view and is not examined.

Exam tip

If a question gives you a scenario and asks which branch it belongs to, look at the verb. "Capture value, price, monetise" points to business. "Store, log, recommend, build a pipeline" points to information systems. "Reach, target, engage, measure a campaign" points to marketing. Real problems usually touch all three, so name the main branch and say why.

Four technologies, two tracks, and the data each one leaves

On the exam: slides 37-50

Slide 37 names four technologies that are rewriting marketing, each with a short tag: social media is "scaled attention", livestreaming is "shows with checkout", AI and AIGC is "the invisible curator", and XR is "the next interface". For each one the lecture gives three things: what it is, a China track and an international track of example platforms, and the data it generates. Learn them as a grid, because the exam can ask about any cell.

Social media (slides 38 to 40): users make the content, feeds rank it by engagement, and there are 5.7 billion users (2026). A social platform generates four layers of data: profiles (who the user is), content and engagement (every action, timestamped), transactions (orders and refunds), and network ties (follows and mentions).

Livestreaming (slides 41 to 43) is real-time video selling with in-stream checkout: you watch a host and buy without leaving the stream. The China track is worth "about ¥5 trillion a year". The data trail logs traffic and engagement second by second, commerce per product per minute, and host performance as conversion and gross merchandise value (GMV) per hour. GMV is the total value of the orders placed.

AI and AIGC (slides 44 to 46) plays two roles: as a curator it decides what you see in the feed, and as a producer it makes the content. Slide 46 explains what this changes for analytics: generated content floods the data stream, so authenticity becomes an analytic problem, and detecting generated content is a classification problem (Week 4).

XR, or extended reality (slides 47 and 48), covers augmented, virtual and mixed reality (AR, VR, MR). Marketing uses include AR try-on and virtual stores, and the new data are gaze and dwell: where you look and how long you stay. The table gives the tracks for all four.

The five data types and the platform shift

On the exam: slides 51-53

Under the four technologies, slide 51 finds a common core of five data types. Behavioural events are the event log: every view, click and like with a time stamp. Text means comments, reviews and danmaku (bullet comments that fly across the video). Networks are follows, mentions and shares. Transactions are orders and refunds. Profiles describe who the user is. Every method week works on one or more of these five, so when you read a case, a good first move is to name which of the five the case data belongs to.

Slide 52 is a discussion: think of an app you use daily that did not exist five years ago, decide which shift explains it (mobile, feeds, or creators), and say what it logs that its predecessor could not. The debrief on slide 53 gives a test, which the slide calls "the tell", for each shift. The mobile shift shows when sessions last minutes and happen anywhere, and the log carries location and motion. The feeds shift shows when you watch what the algorithm picks, not whom you subscribed to. The creators shift shows when the platform itself produces nothing you consume; all the content comes from users and creators.

The debrief gives a decision rule. Most apps born in the last five years carry all three shifts, so "all three" is not an answer. The primary shift is the one whose removal kills the app. As my own illustration: remove the creators from a short-video app and nothing is left to watch; remove the algorithmic feed and it becomes a list of subscriptions. Argue which removal is fatal, then say what new data that shift logs.

Two words carry the vocabulary (slide 72). A dataset is objects (rows) plus attributes (columns). An object is one row, here one streamer. Other books call it a record, case, sample or observation; they all mean the row. An attribute is one column, such as followers or category. Do not confuse the object with a value or with a grouping column. In a table with one row per video and columns for creator, length and shares, the object is one video. The share count in a cell is a value, and "creator category" is an attribute you might group by. This is exactly the shape of mock exam question A1.

Four measurement scales and the operations ladder

On the exam: slides 74-78

Slide 74 names four scales. Each adds one property to the one before it. Nominal values are names: they only tell you whether two things are the same or different (room ID, gender, category). Ordinal values are ranks: they have an order, but the gaps between them have no fixed size (popularity High, Mid, Low; tier Master, Elite, Warrior). Interval values have equal gaps but no true zero (the heat index). Ratio values have equal gaps and a true zero, where zero means "none of it" (followers).

Slide 74 labels every column of the extended streamer table, and you should know these labels because they show the reasoning. Room, gender and category are nominal. Popularity, recommended (Front page, Trending, Not listed) and tier are ordinal. Last live (a date), heat and rating are interval. Followers, resolution and hours live are ratio. The 1 to 5 rating is labelled interval: the slide treats each step between stars as equal in size. A date is interval because gaps between dates are meaningful but there is no "zero date". Resolution is ratio if you read it as a count of pixels.

Slides 75 and 76 show what each scale carries. Nominal carries labels only, so you may count and take the mode; "a mean of room IDs is nonsense". Ordinal carries order but not gaps, so you may use the median and percentiles. Interval carries gaps but not ratios: 90℃ − 80℃ is the same gap as 60℃ − 50℃, but 0℃ is not "no heat", and a heat of −5 is possible. So you cannot say a room at 90 is "twice as hot" as a room at 45. Ratio carries both gaps and ratios: 120K followers really is 2 × 60K followers, zero means none, and there are no negatives. Money and counts, such as GMV, orders and views, are ratio data.

Slide 78 puts this into one table, the operations ladder. Each scale inherits everything above it: ratio can do all four operations, nominal only the first.

Slide 77 adds a second, separate split. A discrete attribute takes finite or countable values, usually stored as integers: room ID, or the number of words in a comment. A binary attribute is the special discrete case with only two values, 0 and 1. A continuous attribute takes real values with finite precision, stored as floats: the heat value in degrees. Discrete versus continuous is about how many values are possible. Nominal to ratio is about what the values mean. Every attribute has one answer to each question, and the two answers are independent. Room ID is discrete and nominal; followers is discrete and ratio; heat is continuous and interval.

Trap

Storage type does not decide the scale. Room IDs are stored as integers, but they are nominal, so the computer will happily give you a "mean room ID" that means nothing. Always ask what the number means before you compute with it. To justify a "twice as many" claim, point to the true zero: 80 shares is twice 40 shares because zero shares means no shares (mock question A2).

One summary per scale, and the superstar problem

On the exam: slide 79

Because each scale allows different operations, each scale has its own summary. For nominal data you use the mode, the most frequent value. In the six streamers, the categories tie: Lifestyle and Gaming appear 2 of 6 times each, so there are two modes. For ordinal data you use the median, the middle of the sorted ranks, and percentiles. The slide's percentile example: a streamer who received 85 gifts ranks at the 95th percentile of 100 streamers, which means 95% received fewer. For interval and ratio data you may also use the mean and the standard deviation, because gaps are meaningful, so adding and averaging make sense.

The slide's warning is "one superstar doubles the mean". Creator data is heavily skewed: a few accounts are huge and most are small. The mean adds everything and divides by the count, so a single huge value pulls it up. The median only looks at the middle position, so it is "robust to extremes".

Worked example (my own illustration): one superstar joins five small creators

Five small creators have 10K, 12K, 15K, 18K and 20K followers. The mean is (10 + 12 + 15 + 18 + 20) / 5 = 75 / 5 = 15K, and the median (the third value) is also 15K. Now one superstar with 110K followers joins. The total becomes 185K, and the mean is 185 / 6 = 30.8K, about double. The median of six values is the average of the third and fourth sorted values, (15 + 18) / 2 = 16.5K, which barely moved.

Business reading: if an agency tells a brand "our average creator has 30.8K followers", the brand will expect a typical creator far bigger than it will get. Five of the six creators have 20K or fewer. For skewed creator data, report the median for the typical case, and use the mean only when you really need the total divided by the count.

On the exam: slide 80

Entropy measures how much you learn from an answer. The slide's principle is "information = uncertainty: the harder to guess, the more an answer tells". If you can almost always guess the answer before you hear it, hearing it tells you little. If every answer is equally likely, hearing it tells you a lot.

H(U) = −Σ pi log2 pi

pi is the probability (here, the share of rows) of each value the attribute can take. You multiply each share by its log base 2, add the results, and put a minus sign in front so the answer is positive (logs of numbers below 1 are negative). H is measured in bits. A value with probability 1 adds nothing, because log2 1 = 0. H is largest when all values are equally likely.

A useful shortcut: −p log2 p is the same as p × log2(1/p). The values you need are log2 6 = 2.585, log2 3 = 1.585, and log2(6/5) = 0.263.

Worked example: three questions about the six streamers (slide 80)

The table has six rooms. Gender is Female for rooms 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, and Male for room 5. Category is Film & TV, Gaming, Lifestyle, Lifestyle, Gaming, Music.

Which room? Six different rooms, each with probability 1/6. H = 6 × (1/6 × log2 6) = log2 6 = 2.585, which the slide shows as 2.58.

Gender? The split is 5/6 Female and 1/6 Male. The Female term is 5/6 × log2(6/5) = 0.833 × 0.263 = 0.219. The Male term is 1/6 × log2 6 = 0.167 × 2.585 = 0.431. H = 0.219 + 0.431 = 0.650, shown as 0.65.

Which category? Gaming 2/6, Lifestyle 2/6, Film & TV 1/6, Music 1/6. Each 2/6 term is 1/3 × log2 3 = 0.528, and there are two, giving 1.057. Each 1/6 term is 0.431, and there are two, giving 0.862. H = 1.057 + 0.862 = 1.918, shown as 1.92.

Reading: the room number has the largest H, so it is the hardest to guess and carries the most information. Gender has the smallest H because you would guess "Female" and be right five times out of six. Category sits in between. Note that entropy depends only on the shares, never on the labels: renaming "Gaming" to anything else does not change H.

Trap

"Most information" here does not mean "most useful for a business decision". The room number has the highest entropy because every row is unique, but an ID tells you nothing about why a stream sells. Entropy measures how unpredictable an attribute is, not whether it explains an outcome. In Week 4, decision trees use the reduction of entropy to choose splits, which is a different question.

The chi-square test: are two attributes linked?

On the exam: slide 81

The chi-square (χ2) test asks whether two categorical (nominal) attributes are related. The slide asks: is a streamer's category related to gender, among 420 streamers? The logic has three steps. First, assume there is no link. If category and gender were unrelated, every category would have the same female share as the whole sample, and that assumption tells you the count you would expect in each cell. Second, measure how far the observed counts are from the expected counts. Third, compare that distance with a threshold, the critical value.

e = row total × column total / n

χ2 = Σ (f − e)2 / e

f is the observed count in a cell, e is the expected count if there were no link, and n is the total (420). Squaring makes all gaps positive. Dividing by e scales each gap to its cell: a gap of 5 matters more when you expected 30 than when you expected 300. Degrees of freedom (df) = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1).

Rank correlation: do two orderings move together?

On the exam: slide 82

Chi-square is for two nominal attributes. For two ordinal attributes you want to know whether the two rankings move together. Because ordinal gaps are not meaningful, you correlate the ranks, not the raw values. The tool is Spearman's rank correlation.

rs = 1 − 6 Σ di2 / ( n (n2 − 1) )

For each object i, di = xi − yi is the gap between its rank on the first attribute and its rank on the second. You square each gap, add them up, multiply by 6, and divide by n(n2 − 1), where n is the number of objects. Then subtract that fraction from 1.

Read the result on a scale from −1 to +1. At +1 the two rankings move together perfectly: every di is 0, the fraction is 0, and rs = 1. At 0 there is no relation: knowing one ranking tells you nothing about the other. At −1 the rankings move in opposite directions: the top on one attribute is the bottom on the other. As my own illustration of −1, take three streamers ranked 1, 2, 3 on one attribute and 3, 2, 1 on the other. The gaps are −2, 0, 2, so Σd2 = 8, and rs = 1 − 6 × 8 / (3 × 8) = 1 − 2 = −1. The slide says Week 1 shows only "the question shape" and the mechanics return in Weeks 4 and 5, but be ready to plug ranks into the formula if the paper prints it.

Preprocessing: aggregate, discretize, binarize

On the exam: slide 85

Preprocessing shapes raw data before modelling. The slide names three operations.

Aggregate: roll single events up into per-stream totals. The raw log has one row per click or order; aggregation turns it into one row per stream (total viewers, total orders, total GMV). This decides what one object means, so it is a modelling choice: totals per stream and totals per creator answer different questions.

Discretize: turn a continuous value into a grade, such as a popularity value into High, Mid or Low. The slide names three ways to set the cut points. Equal width splits the range into intervals of the same size. Equal frequency splits so that each grade holds the same number of objects. K-means places the boundaries where the data naturally separates, in the gaps between groups. Discretizing loses detail, but the result is easier to read and less sensitive to small changes.

Worked example (my own illustration): three grades for the heat index

The six heat values are 90, 80, 50, 45, 60 and −5. Equal width: the range is 90 − (−5) = 95, so each of three bins is 95 / 3 = 31.67 wide. Low is −5 up to 26.67, Mid is 26.67 up to 58.33, High is 58.33 to 90. Low gets only −5; Mid gets 45 and 50; High gets 60, 80 and 90. Equal frequency: sort the values (−5, 45, 50, 60, 80, 90) and put two in each grade: Low is −5 and 45, Mid is 50 and 60, High is 80 and 90. Notice that room 5 (heat 60) is High under equal width but Mid under equal frequency. A k-means cut would look for natural gaps, and the obvious one is the large gap between −5 and 45.

The lesson: one extreme value (−5) stretched the range, so equal width left a Low grade with a single streamer. Equal width is the easiest to explain but is distorted most by skewed data, which platform data usually is. The method you choose changes who lands in which grade.

Similarity and distance between streamers

On the exam: slide 86

Similarity and distance are two views of the same idea, scaled in opposite directions. A similarity of 1 means identical; higher means more alike. A distance of 0 means identical; higher means more different. Slide 86 places four streamers on two attributes, a popularity score (x) and a tier (y), and reports that d(S2, S3) = 1.41 is the closest pair. The slide does not print the formula, but the number 1.41 only comes out of the Euclidean (straight-line) distance, which the course notes also use.

d(p, q) = √( Σk (pk − qk)2 )

For each attribute k, take the difference between the two objects, square it, add over all attributes, and take the square root. With two attributes this is Pythagoras: the length of the straight line between two points on a plot.

Euclidean distance adds the columns as it finds them, so a column in big units dominates. The analytics ladder: describe, diagnose, predict, prescribe

On the exam: slides 87-90

Slides 87 to 90 give each rung of the ladder a definition and an example. Learn the question each rung asks, because exam questions often give you a manager's request and ask which rung it is.

Describe asks "what happened?" It summarizes what the data says happened, with dashboards, totals and trends. The slide's example: 12 streams, ¥2.1M GMV, 3.4% conversion. Nothing here explains anything; it just reports.

Diagnose asks "why did it happen?" It breaks the description apart to find the driver. The slide's example: views fell only on paid streams, so the creative changed. Breaking total views into paid and unpaid streams located the problem.

Predict asks "what will happen?" It uses patterns in past data to score future cases. The slide's examples are classification (will this viewer buy?) and regression (how much will they spend?), taught in Weeks 4 and 5. Predicting needs past cases where you already know the outcome.

Prescribe asks "what should we do?" It turns predictions into actions and compares options. The slide's example: shift 20% of budget to retargeting the flagged 8%. Here a prediction (which 8% of viewers are flagged as likely buyers) is turned into a budget decision.

The important break is between the second and third rungs: describe and diagnose look backward at data you hold, while predict and prescribe look forward and need a model. Each rung builds on the one below it.

Trap

The word "why" signals diagnose, and "should" or "which option" signals prescribe. A request like "which streamer should get next month's budget?" is prescribe, even though it sounds simple. A request like "which viewers will buy next month?" is predict, not prescribe, because it scores cases without choosing an action.

The metric taxonomy and the toolkit

On the exam: slides 91-92 (the legal rule on slide 92 is not examined)

Slide 91 groups marketing metrics into three families. Engagement metrics measure attention: views, likes, comments, shares. Conversion metrics measure whether attention turns into money: click-through rate (CTR), GMV, and refunds. Retention metrics measure whether customers come back: repeat purchase and churn (customers who leave). The families follow the customer from watching to buying to returning. A campaign can be strong in one family and weak in another: high engagement with low conversion means people watch but do not buy.

conversion rate = orders / viewers

The numerator is the outcome you want (orders); the denominator is the chances to produce it (viewers). Most rate metrics have this shape.

The hard part is the denominator. Does a viewer who joined twice count once or twice? Two teams that report different conversion rates for the same stream usually disagree about viewers, not orders, so check that both streams define viewers the same way before you compare them.

Slide 92 lists the toolkit: Python every week (pandas for tables, sklearn for models, mlxtend for association rules, nltk for text) and Stata in Week 5 only (regress and logit). The slide also states a data-protection rule; legal topics are not examined.

The field studies how digital media and analytics reshape business (creating and capturing value), information systems (technologies and data infrastructures) and marketing (reaching, understanding and measuring people). P1.1 A fictional short-video app gives each post a "buzz score" that can be negative, and the app says a score of 0 is just a reference point, not "no buzz". Post A scored 30 last week and 60 this week. Which statement is correct? (a) The buzz doubled, because 60 = 2 × 30. Show the answer

(b). A score that can be negative and whose zero is only a reference point is an interval scale, like the heat index on slide 74. Interval data allows adding and subtracting, so "rose by 30 points" is valid. It does not allow multiplying or dividing, so "doubled" is not valid. (a) would need a ratio scale with a true zero. P1.3 A fictional electronics brand studies 200 viewers of its livestream. 120 watched on a phone and 80 on a PC. 50 bought and 150 did not. Observed counts: phone and bought 36, phone and did not buy 84, PC and bought 14, PC and did not buy 66. Compute the expected counts and χ2 = Σ(f − e)2/e. The critical value printed on the paper is 2.71 at α = 0.1 with df = 1. What do you conclude?

Show the answer

Expected counts use e = row total × column total / 200. Phone and bought: 120 × 50 / 200 = 30. Phone and not: 120 × 150 / 200 = 90. PC and bought: 80 × 50 / 200 = 20. PC and not: 80 × 150 / 200 = 60. Each gap is 6 in size (36 − 30 = 6, 84 − 90 = −6, 14 − 20 = −6, 66 − 60 = 6), so each squared gap is 36. χ2 = 36/30 + 36/90 + 36/20 + 36/60 = 1.2 + 0.4 + 1.8 + 0.6 = 4.0. The degrees of freedom are (2 − 1) × (2 − 1) = 1. Since 4.0 > 2.71, there is evidence of a link between device and buying at α = 0.1: phone viewers bought more often (36 of 120, 30%) than PC viewers (14 of 80, 17.5%). This is evidence of an association, not proof that the device causes buying; for example, the people who watch on phones may simply be different customers.

P1.4 A fictional brand ranks five creators by audience fit (1 = best) and, separately, by last month's sales (1 = highest). Creator A: fit 1, sales 2. B: fit 2, sales 1. C: fit 3, sales 3. D: fit 4, sales 5. E: fit 5, sales 4. Compute Spearman's rs = 1 − 6Σdi2 / (n(n2 − 1)) and interpret it.

Show the answer

The rank gaps d = fit rank − sales rank are A: −1, B: 1, C: 0, D: −1, E: 1. The squares are 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, so Σd2 = 4. With n = 5, n(n2 − 1) = 5 × 24 = 120. rs = 1 − 6 × 4 / 120 = 1 − 24/120 = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8. This is close to +1, so the two rankings move together strongly: creators with better audience fit tended to rank higher in sales. Porter's five forces, platform edition

On the exam: slide 7

The five forces model, from Porter (1980), asks a simple question: who else wants your customer's money, and who can squeeze your profit? It looks at an industry, not at one firm. In the middle sits industry rivalry, the fight among firms that already sell the same thing. Four other forces push into that middle box from outside. When the forces are strong, profit leaks away from every firm in the industry. When they are weak, firms keep more of what they earn. You use the model to judge how attractive a market is and to see where the pressure on your margin comes from.

The slide gives each force a platform example, and you should be able to explain each one in your own words.

Cost leadership, differentiation and focus

On the exam: slides 8-9

Porter's answer to the five forces is to pick one way to win. Slide 8 sets out two broad strategies and adds focus as a third.

Cost leadership

Cost leadership means your total cost is below every rival's, so your profit is above the industry average. The slide's key line is "win the margin game, not the price tag". A cost leader does not have to be the cheapest seller. It can sell at the normal market price and keep a bigger margin, because each unit costs it less to make and deliver. A firm that only cuts its price without cutting its cost is not a cost leader; it is just giving away margin.

The slide lists five routes to a lower cost: a simplified product (fewer features, fewer parts), improved design (a design that is cheaper to make), material saving (using less or cheaper material), lower labor cost, and production innovation and automation. The slide's example is the factory-direct livestream, which uses several routes at once: a simplified product, material saving and lower labor cost. Later in the deck (slide 38) the same idea appears as "a factory-direct stream with the tiers cut", meaning the layers of middlemen between the factory and the buyer are removed.

Differentiation

Differentiation means buyers see your product as unique, so they will pay for it or choose it over cheaper rivals. The slide's phrase is "different where buyers look". Being different only counts if buyers notice it and care about it. A unique feature that buyers ignore just adds cost. The slide's examples are dermatologist hosts (a skin doctor presenting the product gives expert trust) and exclusive shades (colours no rival sells). Both lead to the same result on the slide: "where buyers look, meaningfully different".

Differentiation does not fit every market. The slide says it fits when there are many valued ways to differ, buyers' needs differ, few rivals are already differentiating, the firm has a quality or technology reputation, and the firm has strong marketing. Read this as a checklist. If buyers all want the same thing and every rival already claims to be special, differentiation will be expensive and weak.

Focus

Focus means you do not try to serve the whole market. Slide 9 defines it as concentrating on one buyer group, one product use or one region, and winning it completely. Inside that narrow target you still have to choose how you win, and that gives two kinds of focus. Cost focus means you serve the niche cheaper than anyone else. Differentiation focus means you serve the niche better than anyone else. So the generic strategies are really a choice on two axes: how wide your target is (whole market or a niche), and how you win (lower cost or uniqueness).

Slide 8 lists four forms of focus: product line, customer, region and low share. The first three match slide 9's buyer group, product use and region: you can focus on one line of products, on one type of customer, or on one place. The fourth, low share, is given only as two words. The reasonable reading is a firm that accepts a small share of the total market on purpose, in exchange for being strong in its part of it.

The new media fit, says slide 9, is that "a niche audience is one hashtag away". On a platform, a narrow group of buyers can find you through one tag, so a niche is cheaper to reach than it was in mass media. The slide's example is a streamer who reviews only sunscreen for sensitive skin. That streamer serves one product use for one buyer group and wins by going deeper than general beauty hosts, which makes it differentiation focus.

Trap

Do not confuse cost leadership with low price. The definition is about cost below every rival and profit above the average. A seller that wins on price while its cost is the same as everyone else's is losing the margin game, which is exactly what the slide warns against.

Leader, challenger, follower, nicher

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

Slides 10 to 13 describe four roles a firm can hold in its market. A market leader can grow the pie (new users, new uses, more usage) or guard its slice by improving the product, widening the mix and keeping up advertising. A challenger picks a target and one of five attacks (frontal, flanking, encirclement, bypass, guerrilla). A follower imitates closely, distantly or selectively. A nicher is a small firm that specializes in a segment the big players overlook; a good niche is small, profitable, defendable and ignored by big rivals. The nicher is the market-position version of the focus strategy you just read about, and it sets up the STP pages that follow.

Segmentation: from mass market to a segment of one

On the exam: slide 15

The next block is STP: segmentation, targeting and positioning. Slide 72 gives the one-line meaning: "who you serve, which word you own". It runs in that order. You first cut the market, then choose which pieces to serve, then decide how you want those buyers to see you.

Segmentation is defined on slide 15 as cutting a market into sub-markets whose needs differ, where each sub-market is a group of buyers who want much the same thing. The two halves of the definition matter. Between segments, needs must differ, or there is no reason to treat them differently. Inside a segment, buyers must be similar, or one offer cannot serve them all.

The slide shows four levels, from widest to narrowest. In the mass market there is one offer for everyone, like a classic soda sold to everyone. At the segment level the firm serves a few large groups, like skincare for teens versus skincare for mothers. A niche is narrow, clearly defined and served deep, like sunscreen only for sensitive skin. Custom means one buyer and one offer, like print-on-demand merchandise where one buyer prints one shirt. As you move right, the group gets smaller and the fit to each buyer gets closer; data and platforms make small groups cheap to find and serve.

Disruption enters from below

On the exam: slide 16

Slide 16 brings in Christensen (1997) and his idea of disruption, told in three steps. First, overshoot: the incumbents (the established firms) keep improving their products until they offer more than most buyers need, and they charge for it. Second, low end: new entrants serve the low end of the market cheaply, with a simpler product that is good enough for those buyers. The incumbents tend to leave this end alone, because it looks unattractive to them. Third, climb: the entrants improve step by step and move up into the incumbents' own segments.

The slide's case is factory-direct livestreams, which undercut mid-market beauty brands and then climb. This connects to two ideas you have already seen. The factory-direct stream is a cost leader (slide 8), and the low end is a segment the mid-market firms overlooked. The lesson for segmentation is that a segment the leaders ignore is not always a small one forever. It can be the door through which a future rival enters.

Three targeting strategies

On the exam: slide 18

Once the market is cut, targeting decides which segments to serve and how. Slide 18 gives three ways.

Undifferentiated targeting uses one product and one marketing mix for the whole market. It ignores the differences between segments. The slide's example is commodity traffic goods: cheap everyday items that buyers treat as the same whatever the label. It is the cheapest way to serve a market, but it only works when buyers really do want the same thing.

Differentiated targeting serves several segments, each with a different mix and sometimes a different product. The slide's example is separate streams for students and for professionals. It fits buyers better but costs more, because each segment needs its own effort.

Concentrated targeting puts all the firm's strength behind one or a few segments, aiming for a high share of a small market. This is the natural choice for a small firm, and it is the targeting version of the focus strategy on slide 9.

Undifferentiated works when a strong firm sells something everyone sees as the same, to buyers who want the same thing, with few rivals around: one offer is enough and is the cheapest. Worked example: why Lumen Beauty is concentrated (slide 20)

Read Lumen's row against the three strategy rows, one factor at a time. Firm strength is limited: Lumen is a startup with a junior team. Only the concentrated row has "limited"; both other strategies need a strong firm. This one factor already rules out undifferentiated and differentiated.

Product and market homogeneity are both low: skincare buyers differ in skin type and in what they want. Low homogeneity rules out undifferentiated again, and fits either differentiated or concentrated. Rival count is many, which also fits differentiated or concentrated.

The business reading: Lumen should put all its streams, budget and messages behind one segment it can win, and only spread to more segments when it is stronger. That matches the coverage note on slide 19.

Five ways to position the brand

On the exam: slide 21

Positioning is giving the firm and its product a distinct standing in the buyer's mind inside the target market. Think of the buyer's mind as having a few "slots" for each category, such as "the gentle one" or "the cheap one". Positioning is the fight for a slot. Slide 21 gives five moves.

STP in the feed: the herbal tea example, and STP before the 4Ps

On the exam: slide 22

Slide 22 wraps the block with a three-step rule: segment by behavior, target one, position sharp. Its example is a Douyin herbal tea, and you should be able to walk through it in three sentences.

Worked example: the three-in-the-morning tea (slide 22)

Segment: the brand cuts the market by behavior and finds late-night workers who buy calming tea. This is a behavioral variable (a usage occasion), the kind the platform records directly.

Target: it serves this group alone. That is concentrated targeting, and on the coverage grid it is single-segment concentration.

The rest of the marketing toolkit: value, satisfaction, brand, product and price

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

Slides 23 to 50 work through the marketing mix one lever at a time. They are not on the new checklist, but two of these ideas are defined on the examined key-terms slide (PLC and DREK), and the mock exam, which was written against an older list, asked about brand equity, pricing bases and customer delivered value. So you should recognise them.

customer delivered value = total customer value − total customer cost

Slide 27. Total value has four parts: product, service, personnel and image. Total cost has four parts: money, time, energy and psychic cost (the worry of regret or of a fake). Price is only one of the eight levers. A livestream raises personnel and image value and cuts search time, so delivered value can rise even when the price is the same.

The mock exam's B1 used this idea: a creator whose buyers face six checkout screens and a one-day wait wants to cut the price. The price cut touches only the money cost, while the real problem is time and energy. A shorter checkout fixes the actual cost, and you would check it with a measure such as the share of buyers who complete checkout.

Satisfaction (slides 24-26) is performance compared with expectations: above them the buyer is delighted, at them satisfied, below them lost. The promise is the only source of expectations you control, so a host who overpromises converts today and gets refunds next week.

Brand equity (slide 30) is the added value a brand carries. The course reads it with DREK: differentiation, relevance, esteem and knowledge. Differentiation plus relevance gives brand strength; esteem plus knowledge gives brand stature. Leaders are high on both; brands with "niche potential" have strength but not yet stature; declining brands keep stature but have lost strength. The mock exam's A3 described customers seeing a service as more distinctive and relevant: that is strength.

The product lifecycle (PLC) (slides 36-37) has four stages, each with its own job. In introduction, sales are low, profit is negative and the goal is awareness and trial. In growth, sales rise fast, rivals enter and the goal is maximum share. In maturity, sales peak and the goal is to defend share and maximize profit. In decline, sales fall and the firm cuts spending and milks the product. You need the stage names for the six-factor table above.

Pricing (slides 40-42) has three bases. The cost base adds a markup to the unit cost; it gives the floor but says nothing about buyers. The demand base asks what the target segment will bear. The competition base sets the price against rivals' prices. The mock exam's A4, a seller who adjusts its price every month to stay near three rivals, is the competition base. Within the cost base, a markup on cost gives price = cost × (1 + markup rate), while a margin on price gives price = cost / (1 − margin rate); the slides reach a retail price of about 3580 from a factory price of 3150 by dividing by (1 − 12%). Channels and promotion (slides 45-50) complete the mix.

Variable types and three traps

On the exam: slide 65

Slide 65 asks "what kind of number is it?" The question matters because the kind of variable decides which summaries are valid. A number stored in a computer is not always a quantity.

Among numeric variables, continuous variables can take any value on a scale, like GMV in yuan or average watch time in minutes. Discrete variables are counts, like units sold, likes and comments. The slide's test is "means make sense?" For both continuous and discrete variables an average is meaningful: Lumen's mean of about 2,502 comments per session is a fair summary, even though no single session can have a fraction of a comment. Among categorical variables, nominal variables are labels with no order, like a session ID or a product name. Ordinal variables are labels with an order, like ranks or a 1-to-5 rating.

The slide's poll asks whether satisfaction scored 1 to 5 is numeric or categorical. The course's answer (practice set 2) is that it is ordinal: the levels are ordered, but the step from 4 to 5 is not a fixed amount of satisfaction. So "average satisfaction rose from 4.0 to 4.5" is a weaker claim than "average order value rose from 100 to 150 yuan", where every yuan is the same size. For a rating, the median or the share of 4s and 5s is safer.

The slide then names three traps.

Identifiers (IDs): a session or product code may be stored as a number, but it is a name. If sessions S01 to S12 were stored as 1 to 12, their average (6.5) is easy to compute and means nothing. Any report that shows an average ID has a bug.

Small-base ratios: a rate computed on a small denominator can look impressive and carry almost no information. One viewer and one order is a 100% conversion. The notebook shows a milder version with Lumen's own data. It computes conversion as units sold divided by peak viewers. Session S03 had 844 peak viewers and sold 139 units, a conversion of 139 / 844 = 16.5%. Session S01 had 1,419 peak viewers and sold 218 units, a conversion of 218 / 1,419 = 15.4%. S03 wins on the rate but sold fewer units. Always report the denominator beside the rate.

Bots: automated or paid accounts inflate views, likes and comments. These counts often sit in the denominator or numerator of engagement rates, so bots distort the rates, and they do not announce themselves.

From export to a dataset you trust: the cleaning order

On the exam: slide 66 (the scraping rule on this slide is not examined)

A platform export is not evidence yet. Slide 66 gives five steps, in a fixed order.

StepWhat you do (slide 66)
Tidyone row per observation, one column per variable
Duplicatesdedupe before judging missingness
Missing valuesdrop the row or flag it, never silently fill
Units and codingfix units before judging outliers
Outlierskeep it or flag it, never silently delete

Tidy data means one row per observation and one column per variable. An observation is one thing you measured, such as one stream, or one product in one week. If a variable such as "week" is hidden in the column names (gmv_w1, gmv_w2 and so on), the table is not tidy, because you cannot group or filter by week.

Duplicates before missing values. A duplicated row copies everything in it, including its gaps. If you count missing values first, the same gap is counted twice and the problem looks bigger than it is. So remove duplicates, then count what is missing.

Missing values: drop or flag, never silently fill. You may drop the row from an analysis that needs the value, or keep it and flag it. What you must not do is quietly put in a plausible number, such as the average of the other rows. That creates data nobody can tell apart from real data. Practice set 2 shows why it is so dangerous: filling a gap with the mean leaves the mean unchanged, so the headline number gives no sign that anything was invented.

Units and coding before outliers. A value recorded in the wrong unit looks exactly like an extreme value. If you judge outliers first, you may delete a correct observation that was only mislabelled. Fix units first, then see what is still extreme.

Outliers: keep or flag, never silently delete. Slide 72 defines an outlier as "an extreme but real value". Errors get fixed; real extremes stay in the data and get a flag in the report. Deleting a real event to make a chart look neat hides the most interesting thing that happened.

Behind every step sits one habit from slide 68: log every fix, so that any number can be traced back to the export it came from.

Trap

The two order rules are the ones examiners test: dedupe before judging missingness, and fix units before judging outliers. If a question asks why the order matters, give the reason for each: duplicates double-count gaps, and wrong units look like outliers.

The short version

Platforms make every one of Porter's five forces stronger, so a firm needs a clear way to win: lower total cost than every rival (cost leadership, which is a margin game, not a price game), being different where buyers look (differentiation), or owning a niche, either cheaper or better (cost focus or differentiation focus). Week 3 Association and rules

Every shop and every platform keeps the same question in mind: when someone takes this item, what else will they take? A bakery wants to know which tools to place next to which flour. A live channel wants to know which category to show after Gaming. This week gives you a method for answering that question from records of what people actually bought or watched together. You turn each order into a basket, you write down candidate rules of the form "if X, then Y", and you score each rule with three numbers: support, confidence and lift. In Week 1 you met correlation, which links two variables measured on a scale. This week has no variables, only sets of items, so you count co-occurrence instead. The output is a description of baskets. Next week, classification moves from "what goes together" to "what decides", and predicts a label such as churn or stay.

From co-occurrence to baskets

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

The lecture opens by setting co-occurrence against correlation. Correlation needs two variables on a scale, such as watch time and spend, and gives you r between −1 and 1. Co-occurrence needs no scale at all. It only asks which items land in the same basket, and you get the answer by counting transactions. The course uses the textbook definition of association analysis: given a set of transactions, find rules that predict the occurrence of an item from the occurrences of other items in the same transaction. The left side of a rule is called the antecedent and the right side the consequent, as in {bread flour} → {instant yeast}.

A basket is the set of distinct items in one order or one session. "Distinct" matters. If a customer buys three print packs and two frames, the basket simply contains "print pack" and "frame". In a basket table you write 1 for present and 0 for absent, never the quantity, so that order is the row 1, 1, 0 if the third column is stickers. Support counts orders, not units. The mock exam tests exactly this point (A5), and the practice solutions warn that adding up a quantity column is a common slip.

The lecture names four families of rules. Rules by value are about specific items (game streamer 1 → game streamer 2). Rules by category are about whole categories (Gaming → Film). Rules by attribute value are about properties of a session (watched: yes → liked: yes). Rules by time link periods (year one: Gaming → year two: Lifestyle). A rule by time only works if your data keeps each person's items separate for each period. If you merge January and February into one list per viewer, you can no longer ask whether January travel viewing goes with February language viewing. That is the reasoning behind mock question A6. The lecture also lists four business uses: placement (put associated items side by side), advertising (target by what was just consumed), bundling (sell the basket as one product, like flight plus hotel plus car) and design (lay out the shelf, the site or the stream). One line on slide 9 sets the tone for the whole week: association is not causation.

Itemsets and support

On the exam: slide 15

An itemset is any set of items you want to count across baskets, for example {Comedy, Gaming, Music}. Slide 15 gives you three ideas, and they build on each other.

The first is the support count, written σ (the Greek letter sigma). It is the number of transactions that contain the whole itemset. "Contain" means the basket has every item of the itemset; it may have other items too. The second is the support, written s. It is the support count divided by the total number of transactions, N. So σ is a count of baskets and s is a share between 0 and 1. The third is the frequent itemset: an itemset whose support is at least minsup, the minimum support. The slide says plainly that minsup is the threshold you set. The data does not tell you what it should be.

s(I) = σ(I) / N     and     I is frequent if s(I) ≥ minsup

σ(I) is how many baskets hold every item of I. N is how many baskets there are in total. Support is therefore "what share of all baskets show this whole pattern".

Worked example: the support of {Comedy, Gaming, Music} (slide 15)

Go down the table row by row and ask: does this viewer have all three dots? Viewer 1 has Gaming and Comedy but no Music, so no. Viewer 2 has Gaming and Music but no Comedy, so no. Viewer 3 has Comedy and Music but no Gaming, so no. Viewer 4 has all three, yes. Viewer 5 has all three, yes. So σ({Comedy, Gaming, Music}) = 2.

There are N = 5 viewers, so s = 2/5 = .40. In words: two of the five viewers watched all three categories. If you had set minsup at .40 or lower, this itemset would be frequent. At minsup .60 it would not.

Support has no direction. It describes a set, and a set has no left or right side. {Comedy, Gaming, Music} and {Music, Gaming, Comedy} are the same set with the same support. Direction only arrives with rules, in the next section.

A rule and its two strength numbers

On the exam: slides 16-17

An association rule is written X → Y. X and Y are itemsets, and they must be disjoint, which means they share no item. The rule claims that Y tends to appear when X appears. You cannot write {Comedy, Music} → {Music}, because Music would sit on both sides and the rule would say nothing.

A rule gets two strength numbers. Both use the same top number: the count of baskets that contain everything in the rule, σ(X ∪ Y). The symbol ∪ means "union", so X ∪ Y is all the items of X and Y together. The two numbers differ only in what they divide by.

support: s(X → Y) = σ(X ∪ Y) / N

confidence: c(X → Y) = σ(X ∪ Y) / σ(X)

Support divides by all baskets, so it asks how common the whole pattern is. Confidence divides only by the baskets that contain X, so it asks: among the cases where the rule applies, how often is it right? Support tells you whether the rule is worth attention. Confidence tells you whether the arrow can be trusted.

Worked example: {Comedy, Music} → {Film} (slide 16)

Step 1, find the baskets that contain X = {Comedy, Music}. Viewer 3 has both, viewer 4 has both, viewer 5 has both. Viewers 1 and 2 do not. So σ(X) = 3.

Step 2, among those three, find the ones that also contain Film. Viewer 3 has Film, viewer 4 has Film, viewer 5 does not. So σ(X ∪ Y) = σ({Comedy, Music, Film}) = 2.

Step 3, support = 2/5 = .40: two of five viewers watch all three. Confidence = 2/3 ≈ .67: two of the three Comedy-Music viewers also watch Film.

Worked example: {Gaming} → {Comedy} (slide 17)

Gaming viewers are 1, 2, 4 and 5, so σ({Gaming}) = 4. Of these, viewers 1, 4 and 5 also watch Comedy; viewer 2 does not. So σ({Gaming, Comedy}) = 3.

Support = 3/5 = .60, read as "60% of all viewers watch both Gaming and Comedy". Confidence = 3/4 = .75, read as "after Gaming, 75% also watch Comedy". Notice how the reading follows the denominator: support talks about all viewers, confidence talks about Gaming viewers only.

Because confidence divides by σ(X), it changes when you flip the arrow. For {Comedy} → {Gaming} the top number is still 3, but Comedy viewers are 1, 3, 4 and 5, so the bottom is also 4 and confidence happens to be .75 again. That is a coincidence of this small table, not a rule. In general the two directions give different confidences, while support stays the same in both directions. The practice set shows this on real data: bread flour → bread improver has confidence .470, but bread improver → bread flour has .689.

Exam tip

When a question gives you counts, write down three numbers before anything else: N, σ(X) and σ(X ∪ Y). Support and confidence each take one division from there. If a question asks you to "read" a number, say what the denominator is: "of all viewers" for support, "of the viewers who already watch X" for confidence.

Two thresholds and the four readings

On the exam: slide 18

You need two, and both are yours. minsup answers "how common must the pattern be?" minconf, the minimum confidence, answers "how reliable must the rule be?" The slide says both come from the decision, not the data. A shop that wants to redesign its whole home page needs patterns that touch many customers, so it sets a high minsup. A shop that only wants a small "you may also like" note under one niche product can accept a lower minsup but wants the note to be right often, so it cares more about minconf. The numbers in the data stay the same; the thresholds change with what you plan to do.

The base rate trap

On the exam: slides 19-20

So far it looks as if support and confidence together are enough: demand a pattern that is common and an arrow that is reliable. Slides 19 and 20 show that even this can fool you. The notes call it the most important idea of the week.

Worked example: one platform, 5,000 viewers (slides 19-20)

A platform has N = 5,000 viewers. 3,000 watch Gaming, 3,750 watch Comedy, and 2,000 watch both. Score the rule {Gaming} → {Comedy}.

Support = 2,000 / 5,000 = .40. That is a big share of the platform. Confidence = 2,000 / 3,000 = .667. Two out of three Gaming viewers watch Comedy. With both numbers looking healthy, you might pin a Comedy recommendation under every Gaming stream.

Now the catch. How many viewers watch Comedy anyway, whatever else they watch? 3,750 / 5,000 = .75. This is the base rate, P(Comedy) = .75. A random viewer watches Comedy with probability .75. A Gaming viewer watches Comedy with probability .667. So knowing that someone watches Gaming makes Comedy less likely, not more. The rule's confidence sits below the base rate.

Slide 20 then scores the opposite rule, {Gaming} → {not Comedy}. Gaming viewers who do not watch Comedy number 3,000 − 2,000 = 1,000. Support = 1,000 / 5,000 = .20. Confidence = 1,000 / 3,000 = .333. The slide says this rule "fits the data better". Why, when its numbers are smaller? Because only 1,250 of 5,000 viewers (.25) skip Comedy in general, and among Gaming viewers the share is .333, which is higher than .25. As my own check: .333 / .25 ≈ 1.33, a value above 1 in the lift measure of the next section.

Trap

A rule with high confidence can still be worse than guessing the base rate. Confidence has no idea how common the right-hand side is. Any rule that points at a very popular item will look confident, simply because that item is in most baskets. Before you believe a confident rule, ask: what share of all baskets contain Y anyway? If confidence is not above that share, X adds nothing.

Lift: the number that compares against chance

On the exam: slide 21

The base rate trap shows what is missing: a comparison against chance. Lift supplies it. It divides the confidence of the rule by the base rate of the right-hand side.

lift(X → Y) = P(Y | X) / P(Y) = c(X → Y) / s(Y)

P(Y | X) is the probability of Y given X, which is exactly the confidence. P(Y) is the probability of Y in any basket, which is the support of Y alone, the base rate. Lift asks: how many times more likely is Y when X is present, compared with a random basket?

Worked example: lift of {Gaming} → {Comedy} (slide 21)

Confidence = .667 and s(Comedy) = .75, so lift = .667 / .75 = .89. The slide's reading: below 1, Gaming viewers watch Comedy less than a random viewer does.

You can get the same answer straight from the counts, which is handy when an exam gives counts rather than shares: lift = (σ(X ∪ Y) × N) / (σ(X) × σ(Y)) = (2,000 × 5,000) / (3,000 × 3,750) = 10,000,000 / 11,250,000 ≈ .89.

The slide gives three readings. A lift below 1 means the items substitute: having X makes Y less likely than usual, so people tend to choose one or the other. A lift equal to 1 means the items are independent: X tells you nothing about Y. A lift above 1 means they go together more often than chance would predict. A lift below 1 is still a finding. The practice solutions make this point with a tea shop: if two teas have lift .55, do not bundle them, but you can show them side by side as alternatives.

One more property is useful. If you write lift out as P(X and Y) / (P(X) × P(Y)), you can see that swapping X and Y gives the same value. Lift is symmetric. It scores how strongly two sides go together, not which way the arrow points. Confidence is the number that differs between the two directions, so you rank by lift to find the association and then read confidence to decide which way to point a recommendation.

Exam tip

If the paper gives you only support and confidence, you can still find s(Y) when you know the base-rate count. If it gives you lift and confidence, you can recover the base rate: s(Y) = c / lift. Practise moving between the three numbers in every direction.

Finding rules is a search problem

On the exam: slides 26-28

Slide 26 restates the goal as a filter. Keep rules with s ≥ minsup, which are common enough, and with c ≥ minconf, which are reliable enough. Both thresholds are yours and come from the decision, not the data. The slide names a typical starting point: minsup .5 and minconf .5. The simplest way to find every rule that passes is brute force: list every possible rule, score each one, and keep the ones that pass. The question is how many rules that means.

Slide 27 counts them exactly for a catalogue of d items, in three steps.

Step 1: R = Σk C(d, k) · Σj C(d − k, j)

First pick the antecedent: choose k items out of d, which can be done in C(d, k) ways ("d choose k"). Then pick the consequent: choose j items out of the d − k that are left, in C(d − k, j) ways. The consequent must come from the leftover items because the two sides are disjoint. Add over every possible k and j, with neither side empty.

Step 2: R = Σk C(d, k) · (2d−k − 1)

The inner sum counts every non-empty subset of the d − k remaining items. A set of size d − k has 2d−k subsets in total, and one of them is empty, so there are 2d−k − 1 choices for the consequent. Here k runs from 1 to d − 1, since the antecedent cannot be empty and cannot use up every item.

Step 3: R = 3d − 2d+1 + 1

The binomial theorem collapses the sum. Split it into Σ C(d, k) 2d−k minus Σ C(d, k). The full sum of C(d, k) 2d−k over k = 0 to d is (1 + 2)d = 3d; drop the end terms k = 0 (which is 2d) and k = d (which is 1) to get 3d − 2d − 1. The full sum of C(d, k) is 2d; drop the same two end terms, each equal to 1, to get 2d − 2. Subtract: (3d − 2d − 1) − (2d − 2) = 3d − 2d+1 + 1.

The slide's check: d = 6 gives 36 − 27 + 1 = 729 − 128 + 1 = 602 rules. Six live categories already produce 602 possible rules.

If the binomial step feels heavy, here is my own way to see the same formula, which is not on the slide. For each item, a rule puts it in one of three places: on the left, on the right, or not in the rule at all. That gives 3d ways. Some of these leave the left side empty: then each item has only two places (right or out), so 2d ways. The same number leave the right side empty. Subtract both, 3d − 2 × 2d. But the case where both sides are empty was subtracted twice, so add 1 back. The result is 3d − 2d+1 + 1. You can also check the smallest case by hand. With d = 3 items A, B, C, a one-item antecedent (3 choices) can take any non-empty subset of the other two (3 choices), giving 9 rules, and a two-item antecedent (3 choices) can only take the last item, giving 3 rules. Total 12, which matches the formula.

The Apriori principle and candidate pruning

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

Slides 29 to 45 are no longer on the exam list, but the mock exam (Case 1, C1.1) asks a question about them, and they finish the story that slide 28 starts. Read this section so you can answer such a question if it appears.

Brute force on itemsets scores all 2d nodes of the itemset lattice, the diagram of every possible itemset from the empty set down to the full set. For six categories that is 26 = 64 nodes. The Apriori principle (Agrawal and Srikant, 1994) lets you skip most of them. It says: if an itemset is frequent, every one of its subsets is frequent too. Turned around: if an itemset is infrequent, every superset of it is infrequent, so you can prune them all and never count them.

The reason is simple. Adding an item to an itemset can never add baskets. A basket that holds Gaming, Comedy and Music also holds Gaming and Comedy, so the count of the bigger set can only be the same or smaller. In the five-viewer table, count({Gaming}) = 4, count({Gaming, Comedy}) = 3 and count({Gaming, Comedy, Music}) = 2. This property is called anti-monotone support: a set never has higher support than any of its subsets. The bound is not always strict. {Film} and {Film, Music} both count 3, because every Film viewer also watches Music.

Apriori uses this level by level, in a loop called generate and test. Count the single items and keep the frequent ones. Build candidate pairs only from frequent singles. Then, before counting any candidate of size k, check that every subset of size k − 1 is frequent; if even one is not, discard the candidate without counting. Count the survivors, keep the frequent ones, and move up one level. Stop when a level produces nothing frequent. A threshold given as a share must first become a count: minimum count = minimum support × number of baskets.

Worked example: Apriori on the five viewers at minimum support 60% (slides 36-39)

Minimum count = 0.60 × 5 = 3 baskets. Level 1 counts: Gaming 4, Comedy 4, Music 4, Film 3, Food 2, Lifestyle 1. Food and Lifestyle fail, so no set containing either is ever built.

Level 2 builds pairs from the four survivors only: C(4, 2) = 6 candidates instead of C(6, 2) = 15. The counts are {Gaming, Comedy} 3, {Gaming, Film} 2, {Gaming, Music} 3, {Comedy, Film} 2, {Comedy, Music} 3, {Film, Music} 3. Four pairs are frequent: {Gaming, Comedy}, {Gaming, Music}, {Comedy, Music} and {Film, Music}.

Level 3 builds triples from the frequent pairs and prunes. {Gaming, Music, Film} contains the pair {Gaming, Film}, which counted only 2, so it is discarded without counting. {Comedy, Music, Film} contains {Comedy, Film}, also 2, so it is discarded too. {Gaming, Comedy, Music} has all three of its pairs frequent, so it survives pruning. Now it must be counted: viewers 4 and 5 only, a count of 2, below 3. It is not frequent, and the search stops.

Total scored: 6 singles + 6 pairs + 1 triple = 13 candidates, against 64 for brute force.

Trap

Pruning only tells you which candidates cannot be frequent. It never proves that a survivor is frequent. Frequent subsets are a necessary condition, not a guarantee. {Gaming, Comedy, Music} had three frequent pairs and still failed when it was counted. If a colleague says "all its pairs are frequent, so the triple is a confirmed bundle", the answer is: it is only a candidate, and you must count it against the data before you know.

The mock exam case works the same way. With 100 baskets and minimum support 20%, the minimum count is 20. Any triple that contains a pair below 20 is discarded, and you name the failing pair as the reason. The one triple whose pairs all clear 20 is the only one left to count, and it is still unconfirmed until you count it.

Two further ideas from these slides are worth one sentence each. A frequent itemset of k items can be split into at most 2k − 2 rules (both sides non-empty), so a 3-itemset gives 6 rules and a 4-itemset gives 14. And confidence has its own pruning: for rules from the same itemset, moving items from the left side to the right side can only lower confidence, because the top number stays the same and the bottom number can only grow. So c(ABC → D) ≥ c(AB → CD) ≥ c(A → BCD), and if the first fails minconf, the others fail too. The cost of Apriori is one pass over the data per level, kmax + 1 passes in all, where kmax is the size of the longest frequent itemset. FP-growth is a faster algorithm that compresses the transactions into a tree, but it finds the same frequent itemsets and the same rules; it is an engineering optimization, not a new idea about rules.

The BakeBox case: one shop, two channels

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

The case behind the bakery numbers is BakeBox, a fictional Taobao store with 25 products and 2,800 orders over four weeks, sold through two channels. The storefront (search and browse, planned baskets) has 1,984 orders, 71% of the total. The livestream (host-led evening shows, impulse bundles) has 816 orders, 29%. Mined separately, the livestream slice shows spatula_set → apron with support .271, confidence 1.000 and lift 3.69. In the storefront slice the rule does not exist at all at the same threshold.

The explanation is not that live shoppers love aprons. Every show opens with the host demonstrating the starter kit, spatula in one hand and apron in the other, with the bundle link pinned under the stream. The host manufactures the rule. Association here is merchandising, not discovered preference, and the data describes the show as much as the shoppers. A confidence of exactly 1.000 with no exceptions is a hint to look for the mechanism before you act. The case's recommendations bring the week together: keep the starter bundle as the show opener, build a bread kit page in the shop from flour, yeast and improver (lift 6.96), and stop featuring doilies as an anchor (support .280, lift inside 0.850 to 1.073). The lab rebuilds all of this with the mlxtend library, which returns 40 frequent itemsets at min_support 0.05.

The short version

A basket records which items are present, not how many. Support is the share of all baskets that contain the whole pattern, and an itemset is frequent when its support reaches the minsup you chose. A rule X → Y has disjoint sides; its support divides the count of X and Y together by all baskets, and its confidence divides the same count by the baskets that contain X. You need both thresholds, minsup and minconf, and both come from the decision: high support with high confidence is worth acting on, while perfect confidence on one rare viewer is seductive but thin. Even then, confidence can fool you when Y is common anyway, as in the 5,000-viewer case where .667 sits below the base rate of .75. Lift fixes this by dividing confidence by the support of Y: below 1 the items substitute, at 1 they are independent, above 1 they go together. Sort by lift: R1 is killed at lift .89, R2 ignored at 1.02, and matcha → bamboo whisk kept at 7.80, while doilies at support .280 are a decoy with every lift between 0.850 and 1.073. Brute force must score R = 3d − 2d+1 + 1 rules (602 for six items), so the search is split into frequent itemset generation, which is the expensive step, and rule generation, which is cheap. Apriori cuts the first step: an infrequent set condemns every superset, but a candidate that survives pruning must still be counted.

P3.1 (Multiple choice) A fictional podcast app has 8,000 listeners. 2,000 listen to True Crime, 4,800 listen to News, and 1,200 listen to both. For the rule {True Crime} → {News}, which statement is correct? (a) Confidence is .60, so True Crime listeners are strongly drawn to News. (b) Support is .25, because a quarter of listeners hear True Crime. (c) Lift is 1.00, so True Crime tells you nothing about News. (d) Lift is .60, so the two shows substitute for each other.

Show the answer

(c). Support = 1,200 / 8,000 = .15, so (b) is wrong; .25 is the support of True Crime alone, not of the rule. Confidence = 1,200 / 2,000 = .60. The base rate of News is 4,800 / 8,000 = .60, so lift = .60 / .60 = 1.00. True Crime listeners take News at exactly the rate every listener does, so the two are independent. Option (a) falls into the base rate trap: a confidence of .60 only looks strong until you see that 60% of everyone listens to News. Option (d) confuses confidence with lift.

P3.2 (Multiple choice) A fictional stationery shop finds the rule {gold fountain pen} → {leather pen case} with confidence 1.00 and support .0004. Which reading from the course fits best? (a) Act on it. (b) Weak rule. (c) Seductive but thin. (d) Ignore.

Show the answer

(c). Confidence is perfect, but support is tiny: only about 4 orders in 10,000 contain both items. This is the low-support, high-confidence box, the same shape as the streamer 1 → streamer 99 trap. When σ(X) is very small, a handful of baskets can push confidence to 1.00, so the rule looks reliable without enough evidence behind it. Whether to use it depends on the minsup the decision needs, but the reading is "seductive but thin".

P3.3 (Short answer) A fictional gift shop sells wrapping paper in 35% of all orders, more than any other item. Its lift with each of the other products lies between 0.92 and 1.06. The manager wants to show wrapping paper as "frequently bought together" on every product page. In three to five sentences, explain whether the data supports this.

Show the answer

The data does not support it. Wrapping paper has high support, so it appears in many baskets with almost everything, and any rule pointing at it will show a fairly high confidence. But every lift lies close to 1, which is the value for independence: buying any particular product does not make a customer more likely to take wrapping paper than a random customer. Like the doilies in the BakeBox case, it rides along with everything and predicts nothing. A "frequently bought together" prompt should be built on rules with lift clearly above 1, where the first item really raises the chance of the second.

P3.4 (Calculation) A fictional phone-accessory brand sells 7 products and wants to score every possible rule by brute force. How many rules is that? It then adds one more product. How many rules now? In one sentence, say which step of the search becomes the problem.

Show the answer

With d = 7, R = 37 − 28 + 1 = 2,187 − 256 + 1 = 1,932 rules. With d = 8, R = 38 − 29 + 1 = 6,561 − 512 + 1 = 6,050 rules, more than three times as many from one extra product. The expensive step is frequent itemset generation, step 1, because the number of possible itemsets is 2d and doubles with every product; rule generation, step 2, is cheap once the frequent itemsets and their counts are known.

P3.5 (Case) A fictional craft store, Loomly, has 200 baskets and four products: yarn (P), knitting needles (Q), pattern books (R) and stitch markers (S). Minimum support is 15%. All four single items are frequent. Pair counts are: PQ 45, PR 38, PS 22, QR 41, QS 33, RS 29. Yarn appears in 90 baskets and needles in 80. (a) Convert the minimum support to a count and apply the Apriori principle to the triples PQR, PQS, PRS and QRS. (b) A colleague says PQR is "a confirmed bundle". Respond. (c) Compute support, confidence and lift of {yarn} → {needles} and say what the lift means.

Show the answer

(a) Minimum count = 0.15 × 200 = 30 baskets. Frequent pairs are PQ (45), PR (38), QR (41) and QS (33). PS (22) and RS (29) are below 30. Discard PQS because PS is infrequent. Discard PRS because PS and RS are infrequent. Discard QRS because RS is infrequent. A larger itemset can never appear in more baskets than any of its subsets, so these three triples cannot reach 30 and need no counting. Only PQR remains, because PQ, PR and QR all clear 30.

(b) PQR is only a candidate. Frequent pairs are a necessary condition for a frequent triple, not a guarantee: the three pairs could each appear in 30 or more baskets without all three items appearing together that often. The store must count PQR against the 200 baskets. If, for example, it appears in 26 baskets, it fails the minimum of 30 and is not frequent.

(c) Support = 45 / 200 = .225. Confidence = 45 / 90 = .50. The base rate of needles is 80 / 200 = .40, so lift = .50 / .40 = 1.25. Yarn buyers take needles 1.25 times as often as a random customer: the two go together, a little more than chance would predict. The association is moderate, so a placement test is reasonable, but it is far weaker than a pair like matcha and bamboo whisk at 7.80.

Week 4 Classification

A brand that runs a campaign on a short-video platform gets thousands of comments in a week. Some are customers asking real questions. Some are spam: scam links, fake praise, copy-paste adverts. A small social team cannot read them all, so it wants a machine to decide which comments stay up and which come down. That is classification: a model learns from past cases whose answer is known, then gives every new case a score, and a person turns that score into a decision. This week is less about how the models work and more about how they fail. Every filter makes two kinds of mistake, the two mistakes hurt different people, and one dial, the threshold, moves cost from one group to the other. In Week 3 you counted what appears together with no answer column at all. Here, for the first time, there is an answer column to learn from. In Week 5 the answer becomes a number instead of a category, and the same warning returns: one summary number can flatter.

Labels, features and the hidden test set

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

A label is the answer on a past case. Training a model on examples whose answers are already known is called supervised learning. When the label is a category, such as spam or not spam, the task is classification; when it is a number, it is regression (Week 5). In the TeaPop data the label is is_spam: 1 if the platform removed the comment as spam, 0 if it stayed up. Each comment also has 15 features, the counted signals the model may see: the account (age, followers, verified or not), the text as numbers (length, links, emoji, digits, contact and price keywords) and behaviour (posting hour, comments in the last hour, repeated identical texts). The model never reads the words, only these counts.

The step that matters most for the exam pages is the train and test split. The model learns from 3,000 comments with the answers visible and is judged on the other 1,000, whose answers stay hidden during training. Grading a model on the comments it learned from grades its memory, not its skill, just as a student who saw the paper in advance proves nothing by scoring well. The split is stratified: both parts carry the same 31.2 percent spam share, so the hidden 1,000 holds 312 spam and 688 genuine comments. Every verdict number in this chapter comes from that hidden 1,000. One last warning from these pages: prediction is not causation. The filter uses patterns that went with spam in the past; it cannot say why spam arrives or whether a stricter filter would deter it.

The score is a probability; the verdict is a cutoff

On the exam: slide 14

This idea holds the whole week together. A classification model does not say "spam" or "not spam". It gives each comment a spam probability, a number between 0 and 1. On the slide, one comment scores 0.97 (very likely spam) and another 0.02 (very likely genuine). Sorted by score, the comments form a ranking from safest to spammiest.

A verdict (keep or delete) appears only when someone chooses a threshold, a cutoff on the score. The slide's example is 0.5: score 0.5 or higher, delete; below, keep. But 0.5 is a choice, not a law; at 0.9 or 0.1 the same model gives different verdicts. That is why the slide says to keep the probability: stored scores let you recut the verdicts later, when the business situation changes, without retraining anything. The slide's line is "The model proposes; the threshold disposes." The model proposes a probability; the person who chooses the threshold decides what happens, and owns that business decision.

verdict = delete if score ≥ t; keep if score < t

The score is the model's spam probability for one comment. The threshold t is chosen by a person. A high t means the filter deletes only comments it is very sure about. A low t means it deletes anything that looks even a little suspicious.

Worked example: one comment, two verdicts (background from slide 30)

The lecture scores one real comment from the data with a small three-signal model. The comment posted identical texts twice, posted 8 times in the last hour, and had no links. The model gives it a spam probability of 0.594. At threshold 0.5, 0.594 is above the cutoff, so the comment is deleted. At threshold 0.9, 0.594 is below the cutoff, so the comment is kept. Same comment, same model; only the dial moved. Remember this picture: the model did not change its mind. The person who set the threshold did.

On the exam: slide 15

Once a verdict exists, you can compare it with the truth. Two truths (actually genuine, actually spam) times two verdicts (keep, delete) give four cells. The slide puts the truth in the rows and the filter's action in the columns. Spam is the "positive" class, because spam is what the filter looks for; "positive" means "flagged as spam", not "good".

The accuracy trap

On the exam: slide 17

Accuracy is the share of all comments where the filter was right. It adds the two correct cells and divides by everything.

accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + FN + TN)

The top counts every correct verdict: spam that was deleted plus genuine that was kept. The bottom is all the comments that were judged.

Slide 17 compares two filters. Filter A deletes nothing. It is right about every genuine comment and wrong about every spam comment: 688 of 1,000 right, accuracy 0.688, zero spam caught. Filter B, this week's tree, is right on 298 + 679 = 977 of 1,000, accuracy 0.977, and catches 298 of 312 spam.

The trap is that 0.688 sounds respectable: almost seven in ten, for doing nothing. The data is lopsided (genuine comments far outnumber spam), so a useless model that always says "genuine" scores well for free. This number is the majority baseline, the accuracy of always predicting the larger class. It is the floor every model must beat, not a target. And neither 0.977 nor 0.688 says which mistakes were made. The slide's rule: "Never ask how accurate. Ask which mistakes, at what price."

Trap

Accuracy flatters on lopsided data. When most cases belong to one class, a model that ignores the rare class still scores high. Always compare accuracy with the majority baseline, and always report the two errors separately. If a question gives you a stream where 95 percent of cases are one class, the do-nothing model already scores 0.95, so a model at 0.96 has done very little.

Precision and recall: who feels each

On the exam: slides 18-19

Precision and recall look at the two errors one at a time. Both put true positives on top; they differ in what they divide by.

Precision: how clean is the delete pile

precision = spam caught / all deleted = TP / (TP + FP)

The bottom is everything the filter deleted: the spam it caught plus the genuine comments it deleted by mistake. Precision asks: of the comments I removed, what share deserved it? The only error in this formula is FP, the false alarm.

For the tree: 298 / (298 + 9) = 298 / 307 = 0.971. Of every 100 deleted comments, about 97 were truly spam. The slide names who feels precision: the user whose genuine comment vanished, and the teammate who re-checks the delete pile by hand.

Recall: how much spam got caught

recall = spam caught / all spam = TP / (TP + FN)

The bottom is all the spam that actually arrived: the spam caught plus the spam that slipped through. Recall asks: of all the spam out there, what share did I remove? The only error in this formula is FN, the miss.

For the tree: 298 / (298 + 14) = 298 / 312 = 0.955. So 14 spam comments stayed visible. The slide names who feels recall: the audience that still sees scam links, and the brand whose comment section keeps rotting.

To keep them apart: precision divides by what the filter did (the delete column), recall by what the world delivered (the spam row). Precision audits the delete pile; recall audits the stream.

Exam tip

When a question asks "who is hurt if precision falls?", think of the false positive: a genuine voice deleted, so the commenter and the person checking the delete pile. When it asks "who is hurt if recall falls?", think of the false negative: spam left up, so the audience and the brand's comment section.

You cannot maximise both: the dial and F1

On the exam: slide 20

Precision and recall pull against each other, and the threshold moves them. Raise the dial (stricter, say 0.5 to 0.9) and the filter deletes only what it is very sure about: it deletes less and re-checks more, so precision climbs, but borderline spam survives and recall falls. Lower the dial (looser, say 0.5 to 0.1) and it deletes more and catches more, so recall climbs, but more genuine voices are lost and precision falls. One dial, two errors moving in opposite directions. The extremes make it plain: a threshold of 1.0 deletes nothing (no false alarms, zero recall), and 0 deletes everything (perfect recall, every customer silenced).

The F1 score

To rank models quickly with one number, the F1 score combines precision and recall using the harmonic mean, an average pulled down hard by whichever of the two is smaller.

F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)

Multiply precision by recall, double it, and divide by their sum. If both are equal, F1 equals that value. If they are far apart, F1 sits close to the smaller one. F1 ignores true negatives completely: it looks only at the spam problem.

Worked example: checking the tree's F1 of 0.963 (slide 20)

Precision = 0.971 and recall = 0.955.

Top: 2 × 0.971 × 0.955 = 2 × 0.927305 = 1.85461.

Bottom: 0.971 + 0.955 = 1.926.

F1 = 1.85461 / 1.926 = 0.9629, which rounds to 0.963, the slide's number. You can also do it straight from the counts, with no rounding: F1 = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN) = 596 / (596 + 9 + 14) = 596 / 619 = 0.963.

Why not the ordinary average? Because a filter should not hide a terrible score behind a great one. In my own illustration, a filter with precision 1.00 and recall 0.10 misses nine spam in ten. The ordinary average is 0.55, which sounds half decent; F1 is 2 × 1.00 × 0.10 / 1.10 = 0.18, which tells the truth. The slide adds a limit: F1 ranks models, but it never replaces the pricing question. It treats a missed spam and a lost customer as equally bad, which a real brand rarely does.

Two errors, two prices

On the exam: slide 22

You cannot choose a threshold until you know what each error costs. Slide 22 prices them in words. A miss (spam left up, a false negative) means the audience keeps seeing scam links, trust in the comment section erodes, and the campaign pays because its engagement starts to look fake. A false alarm (genuine deleted, a false positive) means a real customer is silenced and notices, creators and repeat buyers drift away, and the brand gets support tickets and public complaints.

The slide's rule: "Price the two errors separately; then set the dial." Do not pick 0.5 because it is the default, or whatever scores the best accuracy. First ask which error hurts this brand more, now. If misses are expensive, lower the threshold and accept more false alarms. If false alarms are expensive, raise it and accept more misses.

Prices change with the situation. Slide 25 asks: a luxury launch post versus a flash-sale post, which fears a missed scam link more? The slide gives no answer, so reason it out: who reads these comments, what would a scam link do to them, and what would a deleted genuine comment do? A flash sale draws buyers ready to click and pay, an easy target for scam links that cost followers money; a luxury launch lives on image, where both visible spam and a silenced customer can hurt. Good answers name both errors, say who pays each, and then move the dial with a reason.

Every threshold on one picture

On the exam: slide 23

A curve shows every threshold at once. Slide 23 plots the logistic regression filter on the same split: each threshold gives one point, and joining the points gives the curve. Textbooks call this a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve; the slide does not use that name, so use the slide's words on the exam.

The vertical axis is the catch rate: spam deleted, as a share of all spam. It is the same as recall, also called the true positive rate. Higher is better. The horizontal axis is the false-alarm rate: genuine comments deleted, as a share of all genuine comments, also called the false positive rate. Lower is better. It is not 1 minus precision: it divides by all 688 genuine comments, not by the delete pile.

true positive rate = TP / (TP + FN)     false positive rate = FP / (FP + TN)

The first is the share of real spam the filter caught. The second is the share of real customers the filter wrongly deleted. Each threshold gives one pair of these numbers, and so one point.

The slide gives four reading rules. Every threshold is one point. Up and left is better: more catch, fewer false alarms, so a good model's curve hugs the top-left corner. The dotted diagonal is a coin flip: a random guesser catches spam at the same rate it wrongly deletes customers, so its points lie on the line from bottom-left to top-right; a useful model sits well above it. And choosing a point is a pricing decision, not a technical one: the curve shows the options, not which to take.

Two thresholds make a review queue

Moving the boundaries changes the queue. A wider review band means more staff time and fewer machine errors. A narrower band hands comments back to the machine. Raising the lower boundary sends some comments straight to publish, risking more missed spam; lowering the upper boundary sends some straight to deletion, risking more genuine comments deleted. Without the true labels of the moved comments, you can say a risk may rise, but not by how much.

Three models: logistic regression, decision trees, random forests

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

The middle of the lecture introduces the three models behind the numbers. The case pages compare all three and the mock exam asks you to read a tree, so you need to know what each does and what it gives up. The lecture places them on one line, with interpretability (can you defend a decision?) at one end and predictive power (does it catch what simple rules miss?) at the other.

Logistic regression: the readable scorecard

Logistic regression adds up the signals, each with its own weight, into one score, then squeezes that score through an S-shaped curve into a probability between 0 and 1. Each signal has one readable weight, so you can say what drives the score: here repeated characters push hardest toward spam (+2.57), and being verified pulls toward genuine (−0.66). Its weakness is that it draws only smooth, simple boundaries and misses signals that matter only in combination. The lecture calls it the benchmark every fancier model must beat.

How a decision tree asks questions

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions. Each node tests one feature. The branches are the answers: yes goes one way, no the other. The leaves are the verdicts, keep or delete, each with a probability. To classify a comment, start at the top and follow its answers down to a leaf. This week's tree opens with "identical text posted 3 or more times?" and, on the yes branch, "posted before 21:30 or after?", going six questions deep. Reading a tree is careful following. In my own example, a tree asks "price keyword?" (no: keep; yes: next question), then "fewer than 10 followers?" (yes: delete; no: keep). A comment with a price keyword from a 250-follower account passes the first question, answers no at the second, and is kept. Follow only the path the case takes, and check each boundary exactly.

Pure and mixed splits

The tree grows greedily: at each step it asks the single most informative question and never goes back. An informative question separates spam from genuine. A branch is pure when it holds only one label, and mixed when it still holds both. A question that leaves both branches as mixed as the starting node has separated nothing and is discarded.

Worked example: the root question, measured (background from slide 34)

On the 3,000 training comments (about 31 percent spam), the root question is "identical text posted 3 or more times?" The no branch holds 2,410 comments, 14.8 percent spam. The yes branch holds 590 comments, 98.1 percent spam. One question produced a nearly pure spam branch and a mostly genuine one. That is what a tree searches for: copy-paste posting is the loudest spam signal in this stream.

The mock exam tests this with a 50/50 node. A split whose two branches are still half spam and half genuine has done nothing. A split that sends all spam one way and all genuine the other makes two pure branches and is the best possible. Equal branch sizes do not matter; the mix inside each branch does.

Depth, overfitting and the forest

A tree that keeps asking questions until every leaf is pure memorises the training file, noise included, and does worse on new comments. This is overfitting: learning history too well and new comments too little. Stopped too early, a tree underfits and misses real patterns. In the lecture's sweep, test accuracy peaks at depth 5 (0.980), the shipped depth-6 tree scores 0.977, and by depth 12 training accuracy reaches 0.991 while test accuracy sinks to 0.960. Only the hidden test set reveals this. A random forest grows many trees (200 here), each on a random slice of comments with a random subset of signals, so their mistakes differ and the majority vote cancels much of the noise. The price is that the flowchart is gone: a forest answers with votes. On the hidden 1,000 the three models nearly tie (accuracy 0.979, 0.977 and 0.980), which is exactly the case report that follows. The lecture's advice: always run the simple model first.

The dial is asymmetric

On the exam: slide 52

Slide 52 reads the posture table as steps away from BALANCED, turning it into a trade-off you can state in one sentence.

Worked example: pricing each step of the dial (slide 52)

BALANCED to STRICT (0.5 up to 0.9): genuine deleted goes from 9 down to 8, so it saves exactly 1 customer. Spam caught goes from 300 down to 267, so it lets 33 more spam through.

BALANCED to SWEEP (0.5 down to 0.1): spam caught goes from 300 up to 302, so it catches 2 more spam. Genuine deleted goes from 9 up to 37, so it deletes 28 more genuine voices.

Reading, in the slide's words: one step strict is cheap on paper and expensive in spam; one step sweep is the reverse. In both directions you give up a lot to gain a little, which is why BALANCED sits near the corner of the curve on slide 23.

"Asymmetric" means the two sides of a move are not the same size: the dial swaps 1 for 33, or 2 for 28. A brand should leave BALANCED only if it prices one error far above the other. STRICT pays off only if one deleted customer costs more than 33 visible spam comments, as in a public censorship storm. SWEEP pays off only if 2 more spam caught are worth more than 28 silenced customers, as when each missed scam link is very costly during a scam wave. The slide's closing question: where on that dial is the brand safe?

Trap

Do not assume a move of the dial changes both errors by similar amounts. Here the step from 0.5 to 0.9 and the step from 0.5 to 0.1 behave very differently. Always read the actual counts at each setting before you claim what a move will do.

What the report does not say: interpretability is operational

On the exam: slide 53

Slide 53 gives two lessons the consultant's tables do not state. The first repeats the accuracy warning with the case's extremes: 0.688 for deleting nothing, 0.980 for the best filter. Report the two errors separately.

The second breaks the tie. Interpretability is operational: explaining a decision is not a nice extra but part of running the filter. When a commenter appeals in public that her comment was deleted, the brand must say why. With the tree it can walk her comment down the flowchart, question by question. With logistic regression, the readable scorecard, it can say which signals pushed the score up. With the forest it can only say that 200 trees voted, which is no public reply. The slide's line: "The forest's extra 0.001 buys nothing in a public reply", the 0.001 being the gap between 0.980 and 0.979. Exam tip

If an exam question shows models with near-equal scores and asks which to deploy, the expected reasoning is: the scores cannot separate them, so choose on an operational property such as whether you can explain each decision to the people affected. Name the model and the reason.

Who joins the private domain: the same two errors

On the exam: slides 54-55

The last case pages carry the logic to a new decision: not which comment a person handles, but who gets invited in. A skincare brand sells in a nightly livestream room on Douyin. After each stream it can invite viewers into its private domain, the owned channel where a brand messages buyers directly, such as a WeChat Work (企业微信) group. The same two errors appear. Inviting a viewer who never buys again spends staff time on someone who only browses: a false positive. Leaving out a viewer who would have come back loses repeat revenue: a false negative. The two errors carry different prices, so the cut is a business choice.

The short version

A classifier gives a probability; a person turns it into a verdict by choosing a threshold, so the model proposes and the threshold disposes. Every verdict lands in one of four cells, and the two wrong cells hurt different people: a false positive deletes a real customer, a false negative leaves spam up for the audience. Build the confusion matrix from the counts (for the tree: TP 298, FP 9, FN 14, TN 679), then remember that precision is TP / (TP + FP), the cleanliness of the delete pile, and recall is TP / (TP + FN), the share of spam caught. Accuracy flatters on lopsided data: deleting nothing scores 0.688 here, so always report both errors. Raising the threshold raises precision and lowers recall, and lowering it does the reverse; F1 (0.963 for the tree) ranks models but never sets the dial. The curve of every threshold plots catch rate against false-alarm rate, with up and left better and the diagonal a coin flip. Two thresholds make a review queue: keep below 0.1, review 0.1 to 0.9, delete 0.9 and above. In the TeaPop case the three filters tie on accuracy, the dial is asymmetric (BALANCED to STRICT saves 1 customer for 33 more spam; BALANCED to SWEEP catches 2 more spam for 28 more customers), and the tie is broken by interpretability, because a brand must be able to explain a deletion in public.

Week 5 Regression

In Week 4 the question was "which one": is this comment spam, yes or no. This week the question becomes "how much": how much will this user spend next month, how large will the next basket be, how many sales will one more unit of advertising bring. Regression answers with a number and gives each driver a coefficient you can read as per-unit economics. The examined pages teach you to read every number in a regression output table, to judge how good a fit is, and to use a penalty (Ridge and LASSO) to keep a model simple enough to work on new data. In Week 6 the same learners return without a target at all, and the algorithm groups them by resemblance.

Predicting a number: residuals and the least-squares line

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

The lecture opens with twenty weeks of one shop's ad spend and sales. The points rise uphill, and a straight line measures how steeply. To say which line is "better", you use the residual, the miss on one point: residual = actual − fitted. A positive residual means the line underestimated that week, and a negative one means it overestimated. Ordinary least squares (OLS) picks the intercept b0 and slope b1 of y-hat = b0 + b1x that make the total squared miss, Σ(yi − y-hati)2, as small as possible. The misses are squared so that positive and negative misses cannot cancel, and so that one big miss costs more than several small ones. The correlation coefficient r (from −1 to +1) measures how closely two columns follow a straight line, but a strong r is not yet a good model.

Trap

Do not confuse 0.248 with the slope. The slope is 2.097. The number 0.248 is its standard error, the size of the noise around the slope. The slide's own footnote makes this point.

Units, and the intercept as an anchor

On the exam: slide 18

A coefficient inherits the units of the data. Its unit is "units of the target per unit of the feature". If you report sales in thousands instead of single units, and you keep ad spend in the same units, every sales number becomes a thousand times smaller, so the slope is divided by a thousand: 2.097 becomes 0.002097 thousand units of sales per unit of ad spend. The shop did not change; only the ruler did. The slide's donut, priced at 1.19 in lev and 0.61 in euro, makes the same point: the number moves with the unit, the donut does not. So a big coefficient is not automatically an important one. Ask "in which units?" before you compare sizes. This idea returns on slide 38, where it explains why predictors must be standardized before a penalty is applied.

The intercept, −3.604, is the fitted sales when ad spend is zero. Read literally, it says the shop sells minus 3.6 units with no advertising, which cannot happen. The slide's reading is that the intercept is an anchor, not a prediction. The line is a local approximation: it describes the range of ad spend the shop actually used. The intercept fixes where the line sits so that it passes through that cloud of data in the right place. If zero ad spend lies outside the data, the line's value there has no business meaning.

Exam tip

If a question gives you a negative intercept and asks what it means, the full answer has two parts. Say that it is the fitted value when the feature is zero, then say that it is an anchor for a line that only approximates the data range, so it is not a forecast of negative sales.

A coefficient is not a lever

On the exam: slide 19

The fitted line describes association: what went together in the data. Larger budgets and larger sales moved together. That is not the same as causation, the claim that pushing spend up will push sales up. For example, the shop may simply spend more in busy seasons, when sales would have been high anyway. Proof that more spend causes more sales needs a designed experiment, in which spend is changed on purpose (for example at random across weeks or markets) while everything else is kept comparable. A fitted line alone cannot deliver that proof. The slide's rule: use coefficients for prediction and prioritisation; bring causal claims to an experiment.

Trap

"The coefficient is 2.097 with p = 0.000, so each extra unit of spend will bring 2.097 more units of sales." This is false. The p-value says the association is unlikely to be pure noise. It says nothing about cause. A tiny p-value does not turn a coefficient into a lever you can pull.

Many features at once

On the exam: slide 20

Real models have several drivers. Multiple regression gives every feature its own coefficient:

yi = β0 + β1·X1 + β2·X2 + … + βp·Xp + εi

yi is the outcome for row i. β0 is the intercept. Each βj is the slope of feature Xj, and there are p features. εi (epsilon) is the error, the part of row i that the features do not explain.

The slide's example models sales on ad spend, price and streamer hours. The reading rule is the one you will use for the rest of the course: read each coefficient holding the rest fixed. The ad-spend coefficient is the change in predicted sales for one more unit of ad spend while price and streamer hours stay the same. That clause matters. If two features usually move together, the model has little evidence about either one alone, and it splits the credit between them almost arbitrarily. The Week 5 notebook shows this with likes and shares, which mostly echo sessions.

Is the gap real? The two-campus t-test

On the exam: slides 21-22

Before going back to the table, the lecture teaches the logic of testing with a simpler question. Two campuses report mean spend on one common scale. Is the 5-point gap between them real, or just sampling noise?

The starting point is the null hypothesis (H0): the two campus means are equal. It plays the role of "nothing is there". Then you measure how far the data sit from that null, in units of noise. That number is the test statistic.

t = (80 − 75) / √(122/200 + 132/150) = 3.68

The top is the data minus the null value: the observed gap, 5, minus the gap of 0 that H0 expects. The bottom is the standard error of the gap. Each campus adds its own noise, its variance (standard deviation squared) divided by its sample size, and the square root turns the total back into points of spend. So t is the gap in noise units.

Worked example: the two-campus t (slides 21-22)

Step 1, the gap: 80 − 75 = 5 points.

Step 2, Tongji's noise share: 132 = 169, and 169 / 150 = 1.127. Fudan's noise share: 122 = 144, and 144 / 200 = 0.720.

Step 3, add the two shares: 0.720 + 1.127 = 1.847.

Step 4, take the square root to get the standard error of the gap: √1.847 = 1.359 points.

Step 5, divide: t = 5 / 1.359 = 3.68.

Reading: the gap is 3.68 times as large as the noise you would expect in a gap between two samples of this size. The slide calls a gap of 3.68 noise units large, because chance alone rarely makes a gap this wide. The business reading is that the Tongji students in this data really do spend more on average than the Fudan students. The difference is not just an accident of who was sampled.

Exam tip

Keep each sample size under its own variance: 144 goes with 200 and 169 goes with 150. Square the standard deviations before dividing, and take the square root only at the end. Write every step. A clear method earns marks even after a slip in the arithmetic.

Significance level, degrees of freedom and the rejection region

On the exam: slides 23-24

To turn a t into a decision, you need a rule fixed in advance. The significance level alpha (α) is the probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis, chosen before the test. The common choice is 0.05. The slide calls it the false-alarm budget. If nothing is really there, you accept that 5 tests in 100 will still raise an alarm by chance. A smaller α means fewer false alarms but needs stronger evidence.

Degrees of freedom (df) count how many numbers are free to move once the model has used some of the information. The slide's formula is df = n − k: observations minus fitted coefficients. Its example: four numbers have mean 5, so they must add up to 20. Three are reported, 4, 2 and 5, which add to 11, so the last one must be 20 − 11 = 9. Only three numbers were free to move, so df = 3. Fixing the mean used up one degree of freedom. In the regression table, 30 observations and 2 fitted coefficients (intercept and slope) leave df = 30 − 2 = 28. The df set the exact shape of the reference curve that t is compared with.

The rejection region is the set of values of the statistic that would be rare if H0 were true. The slide draws the bell-shaped curve of values t would take under H0. With α = 0.05 and a two-sided test, the middle 95 percent is "do not reject H0". The outer 2.5 percent in each tail, beyond the two critical values, is "reject H0". The 5 percent budget is split in half because a gap could be extreme in either direction. The campus t of 3.68 lands deep in the right tail, so you reject H0. The slide deliberately shows no numerical critical values; what you need is the picture and the logic.

The p-value and the decision rule

On the exam: slide 25

p = P(data at least this extreme | H0 true)

The p-value is the probability, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true, of seeing data at least as extreme as what you observed. On the slide's curves it is the shaded tail area beyond your statistic.

A one-tail p-value counts results extreme in one direction only. A two-tail p-value counts results extreme on either side, and it is the common choice for coefficients, because a coefficient could be wrongly positive or wrongly negative. The decision rule is simple: if p is below 0.05, reject H0. The rejection region and the p-value always agree. A t in the 2.5 percent tail is the same event as a two-tail p below 0.05.

Be careful about what p is not. It is not the probability that H0 is true, because it is calculated by assuming H0 is true. And it says nothing about whether the effect is big enough to matter for money. That judgment needs the coefficient and its units.

Three yardsticks, the split of the total miss, and R squared

On the exam: slides 29-31

Once the line is drawn, the lecture uses three yardsticks to say how good it is. R squared says how much of the ups and downs the line explains. The residuals show what the misses look like when the model is wrong. The complexity budget tells you when a fancier model stops helping.

Σ(yi − ȳ)2 = Σ(y-hati − ȳ)2 + Σ(yi − y-hati)2, that is, SST = SSR + SSE

ȳ ("y-bar") is the mean of the actual values. The total sum of squares (SST) measures how much sales vary at all around their mean. The regression sum of squares (SSR) is the part the line accounts for: how far the fitted values move away from the mean. The error sum of squares (SSE) is what the line still misses: the squared residuals added up. It is the same quantity OLS minimises.

Because the explained part and the leftover add up to the whole, the explained part can be written as a share:

R2 = SSR / SST = 1 − SSE / SST

R squared is the share of the total variation that the line explains. It runs from 0 (the line explains nothing) to 1 (every point sits exactly on the line).

For the 30-observation example, R2 = 0.719. Read it as: the line explains about 72 percent of the weekly swings in sales, and about 28 percent (1 − 0.719 = 0.281) is left in the residuals. Look again at the second form of the formula. On the same training rows, a lower SSE always means a higher R2. That is useful, but it is also the start of a trap, because you can always lower the training SSE by making the model more flexible.

Residual plots, and residuals on new weeks

On the exam: slide 33 (with the residual idea used on slide 36)

R squared gives you one number. The residual plot shows whether the line had the right shape in the first place. You plot each residual against its fitted value and look for a pattern. In the slide's GOOD panel the points are scattered above and below the zero line with no pattern. The misses look like noise, so the model has captured the structure. In the WARNING panel the residuals rise and then fall: a curve remains. A curve means the line is systematically too low in one range and too high in another. Something systematic is left behind, usually because the true relationship bends and a straight line was the wrong shape.

Residuals are also how you judge a forecast on new data. The method is short, and exams like it. For each new week, compute residual = actual − predicted and keep its sign. Square each residual. Add the squares. The total is the total squared prediction error, which is the same idea as SSE, but measured on weeks the model did not see during fitting. A smaller total is better.

Worked example (my own illustration): two forecasts on three new weeks

Two models were fitted on earlier weeks. Model M had a training SSE of 40. Model N, a more flexible model, had a training SSE of 6. On three later weeks that neither model saw during fitting, the actual orders were 50, 64 and 58. Model M predicted 54, 60 and 58. Model N predicted 52, 70 and 55.

Model M's residuals: 50 − 54 = −4, 64 − 60 = +4, 58 − 58 = 0. Their sum is 0. Squares: 16 + 16 + 0 = 32.

Model N's residuals: 50 − 52 = −2, 64 − 70 = −6, 58 − 55 = +3. Squares: 4 + 36 + 9 = 49.

On the new weeks Model M wins, 32 against 49, even though Model N had the much smaller training error. Training error measures fit to the data the model was built from; prediction error measures what you care about, the next weeks. A colleague might say Model M "made no error" because its residuals sum to zero. That is wrong. It missed week 1 by 4 orders (too high) and week 2 by 4 orders (too low), and the two misses cancelled in the sum. This is the same reason OLS squares the misses in the first place. Squared errors cannot cancel, so they show the real size of the misses. And with only three weeks, the comparison is evidence, not proof. Other weeks might favour the other model, so the result needs further validation.

Trap

A sum of residuals equal to zero does not mean zero error. In fact an OLS line with an intercept always has training residuals that sum to zero, however poor it is. The Week 5 notebook shows this: its residual mean is −0.00 minutes, yet the typical miss is 86.7 minutes. To measure error, square the residuals before adding them.

The wiggly line and the tailor's suit

On the exam: slides 34-35

If the residual plot shows a curve, the truth probably curves, and the slide's advice is: if the truth curves, add curves. Polynomial regression bends the line by adding terms such as x2 and x3, so the fitted shape can follow a bend. The slide then shows the temptation. On the twenty-week data, a straight line runs through the middle of the points, while a wiggly curve hits every one of them. The warning: a flexible enough curve fits history perfectly, and new data badly. The wiggles follow the noise of those twenty weeks, and that noise will not repeat next month.

Slide 35 gives the picture to remember: a tailor taking a customer's measurements. A suit cut to one customer's every bump fits that customer perfectly, and nobody else. An overfit model is that suit: it has learned the quirks of the fitting data as if they were rules. A perfect fit on the fitting data is evidence of nothing. The test is the next customer through the door, meaning new data the model has not seen.

Training fit versus prediction error

On the exam: slide 36

Slide 36 puts the two kinds of error side by side. On the left is the OLS training fit: SST = SSR + SSE and R2 = 1 − SSE/SST, so on the same training rows lower SSE means higher R2. More flexibility can always improve this training fit while it hurts new predictions. On the right is what matters for a forecast, the expected squared prediction error on new data, which splits into three parts:

Total = bias2 + variance + irreducible noise

Bias squared is the error from a missed pattern: the model is too simple, like a straight line through a curved truth. Variance is the error from an unstable fit: the model is so flexible that it changes a lot when the sample changes, so it chases noise. Irreducible noise is the part no model can remove, because the outcome has real randomness in it.

The slide's schematic plots these against model complexity from 1 to 8. As complexity grows, bias squared falls (the model can follow more patterns) and variance rises (the model becomes jumpier). The noise line stays flat. Their sum, the total error on new data, first falls and then rises, giving a U shape with its lowest point, the sweet spot, in the middle (at 4 on the schematic). Left of the sweet spot a model underfits; right of it, it overfits. Training error keeps falling as you move right, which is why it cannot find the sweet spot.

The complexity budget and the penalty dial

On the exam: slide 37

The course turns this trade-off into business language as the complexity budget: a feature stays only if its evidence pays for it. There is a fit benefit, because extra flexibility may reduce training error. There is a complexity cost, because large coefficients add a penalty to the fit. The penalty dial sets how strongly the fit discourages large coefficients. Turn it to zero and you have plain OLS. Turn it up and the model is pushed toward smaller, simpler coefficients.

How do you set the dial? Choose the strength using validation data: rows held back from fitting. Try several strengths, fit on the training rows, and keep the strength with the lowest error on the validation rows. Do not choose by training error, because training error always prefers the weakest penalty. The slide adds: shrinking coefficients and selecting features are different decisions. Making every coefficient smaller is one thing; removing some features entirely is another. The next slide shows which method does which.

OLS, Ridge and LASSO, and what happens as the penalty grows

On the exam: slides 38-39 (including the coefficient-path figure)

All three methods start from the same SSE, the sum of squared residuals. The penalised methods add a cost for coefficient size. Here p is the number of predictors, bj is slope j, and λ (lambda) ≥ 0 is the penalty strength, the dial from slide 37.

MethodEach method minimisesEffect and use
OLSSSENo shrinkage. Baseline for comparison.
Ridge (L2): squared sizesSSE + λ Σj=1..p bj2Coefficients smaller, usually nonzero. Many correlated predictors.
Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) (L1): absolute sizesSSE + λ Σj=1..p |bj|Can set some coefficients to zero. Fewer predictors.

Ridge charges for the squared size of each slope. Squaring makes large coefficients very expensive, so Ridge pulls all of them toward zero, but it rarely puts any of them exactly at zero. Every feature stays, just smaller, which suits many correlated predictors: where OLS lets echo columns split credit wildly, Ridge calms them together. LASSO charges for the absolute size. Under that penalty the cheapest choice for a weak predictor is often exactly zero, so LASSO can set some coefficients to zero. It shrinks and selects at once, leaving fewer predictors. A zero coefficient removes that predictor's contribution from the fitted equation; it does not prove the feature could never matter in other data.

Two rules sit under the table. First, standardize predictors (put them on a common scale) before penalising. Remember slide 18: a coefficient's size depends on the units, so without standardizing, a feature measured in small units gets a large coefficient and is punished harder for no real reason. Second, the intercept b0 is not penalized; the penalty sums start at j = 1. The intercept is the anchor that sets the level of the line, and shrinking it would only drag predictions toward zero. When λ = 0 both methods become plain OLS; as λ grows the coefficients shrink more.

Trap

"Ridge deletes correlated predictors" is wrong. Ridge shrinks and keeps everything. "OLS selects a subset" is also wrong, because OLS has no penalty and keeps every feature you give it. Only LASSO directly offers coefficients of exactly zero. And the strongest penalty is not automatically best. Pushed too far, LASSO zeroes out useful predictors and validation error rises. Among the settings that meet the team's practical limits (for example a maximum number of predictors), choose the one with the lowest validation error.

Distance and standardization

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

To say two viewers are "close", you need a ruler. The default ruler is Euclidean distance (slide 9): for each feature, take the gap between the two viewers, square it, add all the squared gaps, and take the square root. It is the straight-line distance you know from a map, extended to many features.

d(x, x′) = √[ Σj (xj − x′j)2 ]

x and x′ are two viewers, j runs over the features, and (xj − x′j) is their gap on feature j. Every feature adds its squared gap to the sum, so the scale of a feature decides how much its gap costs.

Slide 10 calls this the unit trap. Two viewers differ by 300 watch minutes, 10 sessions and 2 questions. The squared gaps are 90,000, 100 and 4, so the minutes gap outvotes the sessions gap 900 to 1 and the distance is really just watch time. That is an accident of units, not a business decision. The fix (slides 11 and 12) is to standardize every feature first, turning each value into a z-score: how many standard deviations it sits above or below its column's mean.

z = (x − μ) / σ

x is the viewer's raw value, μ is the feature's mean across all viewers, and σ is its standard deviation, the typical spread. After this, every feature has mean 0 and spread 1, so a gap of one σ costs the same in every feature. Each feature gets an equal vote.

The slide's worked number: a feature with mean 600 and spread 300, and a viewer at 1,200. Then z = (1,200 − 600) / 300 = 2, two typical spreads above average. The mock exam (A11) asked the same thing the other way round: a viewer at 180 minutes, mean 240, standard deviation 120, gives z = (180 − 240) / 120 = −0.5, half a standard deviation below the mean. Read the sign: negative means below average. Slide 12 also has an honesty note: outliers stretch σ, so with heavy tails you take logs or clip the extremes first.

Slide 13 lists other rulers (Manhattan, cosine for Week 7's word counts, Jaccard for yes/no features), but Euclidean on standardized features is the default.

Trap

Standardizing does not make every feature equally important to the business. It only stops the units from choosing for you. Which features to include is still a business choice, made before the algorithm runs.

The K-means algorithm

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

K-means (MacQueen 1967, slide 19) is the workhorse of the week. You must first choose k, the number of groups. Then the algorithm runs four steps. Initialize: place k centroids somewhere, at random or with a smart method. Assign: every viewer joins the cluster of its nearest centroid. Update: every centroid moves to the mean of its members. Repeat: assign and update again until nothing moves.

A centroid (slide 20) is the average viewer of its group, feature by feature: a representative, not a person. Profiling means translating its numbers into a plain-language kind of viewer.

K-means has one score it tries to make small, the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) (slide 21). For every viewer, take the squared distance to the centroid of its own group, then add these up over all viewers in all groups. It measures how tightly the groups hold together: how much, in total, the members "miss" their own centre. Lower is tighter. Software often reports it as inertia.

WCSS = Σgroups Σmembers x d(x, c)2

c is the centroid of the group that x belongs to, and d is the distance. The inner sum adds up one group's squared misses; the outer sum adds up all groups.

Both steps of the loop can only lower WCSS or leave it unchanged (slide 22). Moving a viewer to a nearer centroid shrinks its squared miss, and the mean is the point that makes a group's squared misses as small as possible. Because WCSS can never rise and cannot go below zero, the loop must stop, usually within a handful of rounds.

Worked example: one round by hand (slide 23)

Five viewers sit on one standardized axis, and k = 2. The starting centroids are at −0.5 and +0.5. Reading the slide's figure, the viewers sit at roughly −2.1, −1.8, −0.2, 1.9 and 2.2 (the slide prints only the centroids, so these positions are my reading of the dots).

Assign: the three left viewers are nearer to −0.5. For example, the viewer at −0.2 is 0.3 from −0.5 and 0.7 from +0.5. The two right viewers are nearer to +0.5. Update: the left centroid moves to the mean of its members, (−2.1 − 1.8 − 0.2) / 3 = −4.1 / 3 ≈ −1.37, and the right one to (1.9 + 2.2) / 2 = 2.05. These are the slide's numbers, −1.37 and +2.05. In the next round nobody switches sides, so the run has converged.

The mock exam (A12) tested only the assign step. Centroids at −2 and 4, a viewer at 1.5. The distances are |1.5 − (−2)| = 3.5 and |1.5 − 4| = 2.5, so the viewer joins the centroid at 4. Notice what assignment does not do: it never changes the viewer's data, and it never leaves a viewer unassigned.

Local solutions, restarts and K-means' opinions

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

"It must stop" is a trap (slide 24). The loop is guaranteed to reach a minimum, not the minimum. An unlucky start, such as two starting centroids inside the same natural group (slide 26), can freeze a stable but second-best grouping. Slide 25 gives two "seatbelts": k-means++, which places the starting centroids far apart on purpose, and restarts, which run the whole algorithm many times from different starts and keep the run with the lowest WCSS. The rule: never present a single run as "the" segmentation.

Worked example: the restart rule (mock exam B4)

Same data, features, scaling and k. Three starts converge with WCSS 210, 168 and 189. You keep the run with 168, because it is the tightest grouping by the K-means objective. But be careful with words. Each run converged, which only means its loop stopped changing. Different starts can stop at different local solutions, so 168 is the best among these starts, not proof of the best possible grouping. Another start could still find something lower. And a low WCSS on its own says nothing about commercial usefulness.

Slide 27 lists K-means' "opinions": it draws round-ish territories, prefers groups of similar size (so a huge group can swallow a niche), needs k before it starts, and drafts every viewer into some group. Slide 28 shows that k too small fuses unlike viewers into one blob and k too large splits real kinds into slivers; only profiling exposes either. Slide 29 leads into the examined pages: inertia always falls as k grows (145 at k = 2, 24 at k = 3 and 12 at k = 5 in the illustration), and at k = n every viewer is its own group with inertia 0. Perfectly tight, perfectly useless, so the objective itself cannot pick k.

How many groups is a judgment

On the exam: slide 32

Slide 32 shows the same points circled three ways: as two groups, as four, and as six. Every version is defensible. The slide's message is that the definition of a cluster is not precise, so the count of clusters is a judgment, not a lookup. There is no hidden "true k" that a formula can read off. The useful question is which grouping pays, and the rest of the section prices it: two geometric tools (the elbow and the silhouette), a stability check, and the business tests.

So never write "the algorithm found 4 segments". It found 4 because someone told it k = 4; a good answer says who chose k and on what evidence.

The elbow, read as marginal returns

On the exam: slide 33

The elbow plot puts k on the horizontal axis (1 to 8 on the slide) and WCSS on the vertical axis. Remember what WCSS measures: the total squared distance from every viewer to the centre of its own group. When k grows, every viewer has more centres to choose from, so WCSS always falls. That is why you cannot simply pick the k with the lowest WCSS; you would always pick the largest k.

Instead, slide 33 tells you to read the curve as marginal returns. Ask: what does one more segment buy? Look at the drop in WCSS from k to k + 1. At first the drops are big, because a new centroid rescues many badly placed viewers. Later the drops become small, because a new centroid only splits an existing group that was already tight. The point where the curve bends from steep to flat is the knee or elbow. On the slide's illustrative curve, the knee is at k = 3: big drops from 1 to 2 and from 2 to 3, then only small drops after. The slide's rule: stop when the next segment's geometric gain no longer justifies its keep. "Keep" is a business word: every extra segment costs a campaign, a manager, content and reporting.

The bottom line of the slide is important: there is no automatic rule. Saying "we stopped at the knee" is honest, because it admits a judgment. Pretending a formula chose k is not. Real curves are often smooth, and two people may see the knee in different places.

Worked example: reading the SkillCraft elbow (notebook section 4)

The notebook fits K-means on the eight standardized SkillCraft columns for k = 1 to 8. The WCSS values are 32,000, 21,470, 17,067, 14,552, 13,094, 11,869, 11,073 and 10,624. Compute the drop per added k: 10,530 (1 to 2), 4,403 (2 to 3), 2,515 (3 to 4), 1,458 (4 to 5), 1,225 (5 to 6), 796 (6 to 7) and 449 (7 to 8). The first segment split buys a lot. Each later one buys less. The notebook's reading is that returns shrink after k = 4, but you can see the curve has no sharp corner. This is exactly why the slide says no automatic rule, and why you want a second piece of evidence: the silhouette.

Exam tip

If a question shows a WCSS table, compute the drops, not just the levels. Say where the drops become small, call that the knee, and add that the choice is a judgment that the business tests must still confirm.

The silhouette score

On the exam: slides 34-35

The elbow judges the whole map at once. The silhouette (Rousseeuw 1987, slide 34) judges each viewer. It asks: how close is this viewer to its own group, compared with the nearest other group? You compute two numbers for each viewer. The first, a, is the average distance from the viewer to the other members of its own group. The second, b, is the average distance from the viewer to the members of the nearest other group, the neighbouring crowd it would most likely be confused with.

s = (b − a) / max(a, b)

The top, b − a, is how much closer the viewer is to its own group than to the next one. The bottom, max(a, b), is the larger of the two distances; dividing by it keeps s between −1 and +1, so viewers can be compared.

The score runs from −1 to +1, and slide 34 gives three readings. Near +1, a is much smaller than b: the viewer is well inside its own group, a confident member. Near 0, a and b are about equal: the viewer sits on the boundary between two groups, a coin flip. Negative, b is smaller than a: the viewer is closer to another group than to its own, so it was probably filed in the wrong group. If you average s over all viewers, you get one number per candidate k, which you can compare against the elbow.

Worked example: one viewer's silhouette (slide 35)

One viewer has a = 2.0 (average distance to its own group) and b = 5.0 (average distance to the nearest other group). The larger of the two is 5.0. So s = (5.0 − 2.0) / 5.0 = 3.0 / 5.0 = 0.6. The slide's reading: comfortably placed, well inside its own group.

Now move its own group farther away, so a = 4.8 while b stays 5.0. The larger is still 5.0. So s = (5.0 − 4.8) / 5.0 = 0.2 / 5.0 = 0.04. That is almost zero: the viewer is on the boundary. It is barely closer to its own group than to the next one.

Finally, if b is smaller than a, the top of the fraction is negative, so s is negative. The viewer sits closer to another group. As my own illustration with the same viewer: if a = 4.8 and b = 2.0, the larger is now a, and s = (2.0 − 4.8) / 4.8 = −2.8 / 4.8 ≈ −0.58.

Trap

The bottom of the fraction is the larger of a and b, not always b. When b is smaller than a, you divide by a. Students who always divide by b get a number below −1 in the negative case, which is impossible. If your silhouette falls outside −1 to +1, check the denominator.

Reading a silhouette plot, and silhouette by k

On the exam: slides 36-37

A silhouette plot (slide 36) draws one horizontal bar per viewer, with length equal to that viewer's silhouette. The bars are sorted from longest to shortest inside each group, the groups are stacked, and a dashed vertical line marks the average. On the slide's illustration, the first of three groups has long bars, the second falls to short bars at the bottom, and one small bar at the very bottom sticks out to the left of zero.

The slide gives three things to look for. Wide, even bars mean a healthy, confident grouping. Bars dipping below the group's average are boundary members, worth a second look. Bars below zero are viewers filed in the wrong group. The plot shows you what a single average can hide: one group can be fuzzy while the others are clean, and the average alone will not tell you which. In the SkillCraft notebook, for instance, the four clusters at k = 4 have average silhouettes of 0.412 (casual scrollers), 0.223 (live regulars), 0.164 (systematic learners) and 0.331 (credential seekers), around an overall average of 0.3115. The systematic learners hold together much less firmly than the largest group.

Slide 37 then turns the silhouette into a tool for choosing k. For each candidate k (2 to 8 on the slide), fit K-means, compute every viewer's silhouette and take the average. That gives one number per candidate. You cannot compute it for k = 1, because there is no "other group" to measure b. The average silhouette peaks where membership is most confident; in the slide's illustration the peak is at k = 4.

The second message of slide 37 is "two evidences, one answer". The elbow and the silhouette measure different things. The elbow measures tightness: how much WCSS one more group buys. The silhouette measures separation: how much closer viewers are to their own group than to the next. When the structure in the data is real, the two point the same way. When they disagree, the structure is weak and your judgment, and the business, must carry more of the decision.

Worked example: SkillCraft's silhouette by k (notebook section 5)

The average silhouettes are 0.3078 at k = 2, 0.3101 at k = 3, 0.3115 at k = 4, then 0.2212 at k = 5, 0.2263 at k = 6, 0.2201 at k = 7 and 0.1860 at k = 8. The peak is k = 4 at 0.3115. Read it honestly: k = 2, 3 and 4 are very close, and the big fall comes after 4. The elbow's drops also shrink after k = 4. So both evidences allow four, and the silhouette is what nominates it. A silhouette around 0.31 is a moderate, not a strong, separation, which is one more reason to check the business tests next.

Kotler's four tests and the floor

On the exam: slide 38

The curves are geometry. A segment must also work as a business object. Slide 38 uses four tests from Kotler and Keller (2016). Measurable: you can count it and describe it, with a size and a profile. Substantial: it is big enough to serve profitably. This is about profit, not about a percentage on its own. Reachable: your channels actually touch it. A group you cannot target with a post, a message or a live room is a fact, not a segment. Actionable: someone will run a campaign for it. The slide's rule is strict: a segment earns a budget only if it passes all four tests. (The weekly notes also mention "differentiable", that segments respond differently to different actions, but the slide shows four.)

The slide then adds the floor: if the business has three campaigns planned, k is at least 3, whatever the curves say. The plan sets a lower limit on the number of groups, because with fewer groups at least one campaign would have no audience of its own. So the business can overrule the curves from below. This connects to the lab discussion (slide 66): the silhouette peaks at k = 4, but a team needs three segments for a campaign. A sensible answer reports both: what the geometry nominates, what the plan needs, and why the final k was chosen.

Exam tip

When a case question asks you to choose k, use the order the notes summarize: the curves nominate, stability cross-examines, the business decides. Then apply the four tests by name to the segment in question.

When geometry and business disagree

On the exam: slide 39

Slide 39 gives a picture of a conflict. A loyalty file has a silhouette that peaks at k = 2: one clean split, "active versus gone". Geometrically it is beautiful. But the actionable structure sits at k = 5, including a 4 percent premium niche. With k = 2, that niche is silently dissolved into the mass: its members are drafted into the big "active" group, and nobody ever sees them as a separate kind of customer. The slide's image is a boutique, "small, premium, and worth keeping".

The slide's verdict: the silhouette is a geometric criterion, and when geometry and business disagree, geometry loses. The silhouette rewards groups that are far apart. It knows nothing about margins, prices or campaigns. A small group that is very valuable may be close to a bigger group in behavior space, so splitting it off lowers the average silhouette, but it still pays. Recall also slide 27: K-means prefers groups of similar size, so a huge group can swallow a niche. Choosing a larger k is one way to protect the niche.

Trap

"The silhouette is highest at k = 2, so k = 2 is correct" is the wrong reasoning. The silhouette nominates; it does not decide. A higher score does not mean more business value.

Stability, and the 2 percent sliver

On the exam: slides 40-41

Slide 40 asks: would the same k win again tomorrow? A k that wins once may be one seed's opinion, because K-means depends on its random start and on the exact rows in the file. The slide gives three steps. Re-run the whole pipeline on resamples of the data and with different seeds. Compare: do the same segments reappear, with similar sizes and profiles, not just the same count? Report stability next to the silhouette, because both are evidence. A stable structure is safe to plan campaigns against; an unstable one is "a story about the seed". Note the difference from restarts: restarts pick the best of several runs on the same data; stability asks whether the answer survives when you change the data and the seed.

Slide 41 is a discussion: a 2 percent sliver with extreme watch time and high conversion. Serve it, or fold it into the mass? The slide asks you to argue substantial versus actionable. For folding it in: 2 percent of the file may be too few people to pay for its own content and campaign, so it may fail "substantial"; it may also be unstable, a group that appears with one seed and vanishes with another. For serving it: its behavior is extreme and it converts well, so a campaign for it is easy to design and likely to work, which makes it actionable; and slide 39 already showed that a small premium niche can be worth keeping.

The slide's third question asks what single number would settle it. The slides do not print an answer, so here is the reasoning that follows from the tests. "Substantial" means big enough to serve profitably, so the deciding number is the profit from serving the sliver: roughly its size times its conversion times the value per conversion, minus the cost of running a separate campaign for it. If that is positive, the sliver passes, however small its share. You should also confirm it reappears across resamples before you spend on it.

Case: Etsy and a buyer's lasting interests

On the exam: slide 42

The Etsy case (Bhadauria et al. 2024) shows clustering used on products, not people. Etsy is a marketplace with a huge number of listings. K-means groups products using image and text features, meaning the product photos and descriptions. The result is about 1,000 interest groups of similar listings. The slide's flow has four boxes: product features (images and descriptions), interest groups (1,000 groups of similar listings), buyer history (purchases over time), and recommendations (items linked to lasting interests). The product groups feed a broader recommendation system.

Follow the products first, then the buyer, as the slide says. First the clustering turns millions of individual listings into a manageable set of interests. Then a buyer's purchase history is read in terms of those groups: a buyer who bought several items from the same interest group over time shows a lasting interest, not a one-time need. The slide's question is how this helps Etsy stay relevant after a first purchase. Beyond K-means

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

Slides 43 to 50 show other tools. Hierarchical clustering needs no k up front: every viewer starts alone and the closest pairs merge until one root remains, giving a tree called a dendrogram. Cutting the tree at a height chooses k afterwards. It is heavy on big files, so it suits small, precious sets such as forty key accounts. Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) follows density instead of centroids, so it can keep a curved arc whole and labels isolated points as noise. The default stays K-means on standardized features.

The short version

Clustering groups people with no target, so success means a better decision, not a score. Standardize first with z = (x − mean) / SD, or the feature with the biggest units decides every distance. K-means assigns each point to its nearest centroid and moves each centroid to its group's mean until nothing changes; it always stops, but only at a local solution, so use restarts and keep the lowest within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), calling it the best among these starts, not the best possible. The number of groups is a judgment. The elbow reads WCSS as marginal returns and you stop at the knee, with no automatic rule. The silhouette s = (b − a) / max(a, b) compares a viewer's average distance to its own group (a) with that to the nearest other group (b): near +1 is confident, near 0 is on the boundary, negative is misfiled; 2.0 and 5.0 give 0.6, and 4.8 and 5.0 give 0.04. When the elbow and the average silhouette agree, the structure is real; still check stability across resamples and seeds. Then the business decides: Kotler's four tests (measurable, substantial, reachable, actionable), the floor (three campaigns means k is at least 3), and when geometry and business disagree, geometry loses. A cluster becomes a segment when you name it from its extremes and it passes the line-up test. Judge a campaign against the segment's own baseline, use segments as the language and behaviors as the forecast (R squared 0.4372 against 0.6807), and put the budget where engagement is high and conversion has room to move, which at SkillCraft means the systematic learners.

P6.1 (Multiple choice) A fictional podcast app standardizes "episodes finished per month" before clustering. The mean is 8 and the standard deviation is 2.5. A listener finished 3 episodes. What is the listener's z-score, and what does it mean? (a) +2.0, two standard deviations above the mean. (b) −2.0, two standard deviations below the mean. (c) −5.0, five episodes below the mean. (d) −0.5, half a standard deviation below the mean.

Show the answer

(b). z = (3 − 8) / 2.5 = −5 / 2.5 = −2.0. The listener sits two typical spreads below the average. Option (c) is the raw gap in episodes, not a z-score; the point of standardizing is to count in units of spread so that features with different units can be compared fairly in a distance.

P6.2 (Multiple choice) In a fitted K-means on a fictional shopping app, a customer's average distance to the other members of its own cluster is a = 3.6, and its average distance to the members of the nearest other cluster is b = 3.0. What is its silhouette, and what does it say? (a) +0.17, well inside its cluster. (b) −0.20, so the customer must be an outlier with no group. (c) −0.17, the customer sits closer to another cluster and was probably filed in the wrong one. (d) 0.83, a confident member.

P6.4 (Short answer) An analyst runs K-means once on a fictional gaming platform's player file with k = 5, gets a WCSS of 2,410, and says: "The algorithm converged, so this is the best segmentation." Give two reasons why this claim is too strong, in three to five sentences.

Show the answer

Week 7 Text Analysis and Natural Language Processing (NLP)

In Weeks 4 to 6 every input was already a number: comment counts, watch time, viewer behaviour. This week the data are customers' own words. A brand that launches a product can receive thousands of reviews a day, in more than one language, and no team can read them all. Natural language processing (NLP) is the set of methods that turns that text into numbers, so you can count themes, find the reviews that matter, and estimate how customers feel. Once text is numbers, the classifiers of Week 4, the regressions of Week 5 and the clustering of Week 6 all work on it again. The week has two halves. The first half, "from words to numbers", shows how you clean text and represent it: counts, TF-IDF, n-grams and embeddings, then topic models. The second half compares three ways to measure sentiment: a word list, a supervised model, and a large language model (LLM). The running case is SoundPeak AeroBuds, a fictional earbud line with 4,000 synthetic launch reviews, half Chinese and half English. Next week moves from what people say to how they are connected, in social network analysis.

Text as data: why the week exists

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

Booking.com reported more than 300 million verified guest reviews in 2023, and new media adds livestream chat, Dianping reviews, Xiaohongshu posts and Weibo comments: far more than a team can read. The course frames the answer as "Voice of the Customer", a repeating process: collect customers' own descriptions, organize them by topic, prioritize with frequency × impact, act by assigning a responsible person, and verify that the metric changed. Language is hard for machines because meaning depends on order and background knowledge: "Stolen Painting Found by Tree" has a sensible reading and a silly one (a tree found it), and a model that only counts words cannot tell them apart. Slang, idioms, new product words and ambiguous names add more trouble. The NLP pipeline on slide 14 has five steps: collect, normalize, represent, model, decide. Prepared code does each step, but a human checks the outputs and makes the decisions. The examined pages start at step 2.

Why models need numbers: four representations

On the exam: slide 16

A classifier or a regression cannot read a sentence. It needs a table of numbers, one row per document and one column per feature. So the first job is to choose a representation: a rule that turns each review into a row of numbers. Slide 16 names four, and each keeps different information. Counts record how often each word occurs. TF-IDF (term frequency-inverse document frequency) weights those counts by how rare each word is across all reviews. N-grams count short runs of consecutive words, so they keep some local word order. Embeddings place words in a space of learned coordinates, so words used in similar ways sit close together and the numbers carry some meaning.

The slide's message is that no representation is best in general. They keep different information and need different resources, so you choose the one that supports your analytical question. If you only need to know which product features people mention most, counts may be enough. If you need to find reviews that say the same thing in different words, you need something like embeddings. Keep this "fit the representation to the question" idea in mind, because the comparison table on slide 30 returns to it.

Normalization: tokens, lemmas and stems

On the exam: slides 17-18

Before you can count words, you must decide what a "word" is. Normalization is the cleaning step that does this, and slide 17 names three operations.

Tokenize means split the text into words and symbols. "The battery died fast!" becomes [ The · battery · died · fast · ! ]. Each piece is a token, the unit a tokenizer produces; it may be a word, a punctuation mark or part of a word. Tokenizing only splits. It does not change any word.

Lemmatize means replace each word with its dictionary form, called the lemma. "was", "were" and "is" all become "be"; "batteries" becomes "battery". The point is that different grammatical forms of the same word should count as one feature. The mock exam asks exactly this: a pipeline that maps "was", "were" and "is" to "be" is doing lemmatization, not tokenization.

Stem means cut word endings off using simple rules. "automation" and "automatic" both become "automat". Stemming is computationally simple, but the result need not be a real dictionary word. That is the difference you must remember: a lemma is always a dictionary word found by knowing the grammar; a stem is whatever is left after chopping, and "automat" is not an English word.

Why should a manager care? Because preprocessing decides what information the model can see. The slide's warning is that removing "not" can reverse sentiment: "not good" becomes "good". The practical rule on the slide is to inspect the tokenizer's input and output, even when you use prepared code. You do not need to write the code, but you must look at what it produced.

Worked example: one review through normalization (slide 18)

Take the illustrative review "The batteries barely lasted a day!". The raw text is a single string. After tokenizing (and lowercasing) it becomes seven tokens: [ the · batteries · barely · lasted · a · day · ! ]. After lemmatizing it becomes [ the · battery · barely · last · a · day · ! ].

Two things changed. "batteries" became "battery" and "lasted" became "last". Now this review and another review that says "the battery lasts" share the same features, so a count of battery complaints will find both. Nothing else changed: "barely" and "day" were already in dictionary form.

The slide adds one more point. If the review had said "did not last", you must keep "not", because it carries the negative meaning. A cleaning step that drops "not" would turn a complaint into a neutral or positive statement about lasting.

English and Chinese tokenization, and the dictionary

On the exam: slides 19-21

English and Chinese mark word boundaries differently, so they need different tokenizers. In English, spaces and punctuation already show most boundaries. The course uses the Natural Language Toolkit (nltk), which splits "The battery died fast, honestly!" into [ The · battery · died · fast · , · honestly · ! ]. Punctuation and contractions still need rules, but spaces do most of the work.

Chinese usually has no spaces between words. The course uses jieba, which decides where one word ends and the next begins by using a dictionary and probabilities: it matches the sentence against dictionary entries and uses word frequencies to choose the most likely split. The slide's example is 续航很差，戴久了耳朵疼 ("Battery life is poor, and my ears hurt after wearing them for a long time"), which jieba splits into [续航 · 很差 · ， · 戴久 · 了 · 耳朵 · 疼]: battery life, very poor, punctuation, wear for a long time, a grammatical particle, ears, hurt. The tokenization methods differ, but after this step both languages go through the same counting and weighting steps. The lab runs "two tokenizers, one pipeline" on the same file.

Because jieba depends on its dictionary, a word that is not in the dictionary gets cut into the wrong pieces. This matters most for brand terms, which companies invent all the time.

Worked example: a coined brand term, before and after (slide 20)

The illustrative sentence is 声阔小金标的调音偏暖 ("Soundcore's 'Little Gold Label' has a warm tuning"). The product term is 小金标, an invented name that the default dictionary does not know.

Before the fix, jieba gives [ 声阔 · 小金 · 标的 · 调音 · 偏暖 ]. The product name is broken in two, and its last character 标 is glued to the particle 的 to form 标的, which is a different word. So two errors happen at once: the product is never counted as itself, and 的 joins the wrong token.

After adding 小金标 to the dictionary, jieba gives [ 声阔 · 小金标 · 的 · 调音 · 偏暖 ]. The product term stays one token, and 的 is back in its normal role of marking possession. The lesson on the slide: add brand terms to the dictionary before counting.

Slide 21 turns this into a maintenance duty. Common words such as 入耳式 (in-ear) and 降噪 (noise cancellation) are already built in and are split correctly without any change. What you must add are coined brand terms, product codes and campaign hashtags, one entry per line. Every new campaign brings new terms, so an outdated dictionary causes segmentation errors, and those errors flow into every count, frequency and sentiment score built later. The slide's picture of sixty years of Xinhua Dictionary editions makes the same point: a dictionary is something someone has to keep updating. In the SoundPeak lab, the same problem appears with 充电盒 (charging case), which the default dictionary splits into 充电 and 盒真 until one line is added.

Trap

"Tokenization is a technicality; the model sees the same text either way" is wrong. The tokenizer decides what the words are before any model runs. If a product name is split, its mentions scatter across wrong tokens and are undercounted, and every later number inherits the error.

Stop words: what to remove and what to keep

On the exam: slides 20 and 22

Stop words are tokens you exclude for a task, usually very common grammatical words that carry little topic information. In English these include "the", "a", "and" and "of"; the standard English list contains 179 words. Chinese uses a separate list, with particles such as 的 (linking particle), 了 (aspect particle) and 在 (at/in). Stop lists are language-specific files: you cannot apply the English list to Chinese text.

Some words are a judgement call. Slide 20 notes that 很 (very) can be noise for counting topics but useful for sentiment, so its relevance depends on the analytical task. The big exception is negation. Slide 22 says to retain negation terms: "not", "never", "no", 不 (not) and 没 (did not / have not). If you remove 不, then 不好 (not good) becomes 好 (good), and the meaning is reversed. The principle on the slide is to inspect the stop list before applying it and adapt it to your question.

Even if you keep "not", a plain word count only records that a negator appeared somewhere in the review, not that it sat next to "good". Keeping negation is necessary, but word order is still lost, which is the problem the next section shows.

On the exam: slide 23

The simplest representation is the bag of words: each document becomes a row of token counts, with one column per word in the vocabulary and no information about word order. Imagine pouring the words of a review into a bag and shaking it. You know which words are inside and how many times, but not their order.

Yet their rows are identical, because both contain the same four words once each. This is the limitation: different meanings, identical rows, because word order is not preserved.

Exam tip

If a question shows two reviews with the same words in a different order and asks why a model treats them alike, the answer is that bag of words (and TF-IDF, which is built on it) ignores word order. The fix is n-grams for short phrases or embeddings for wider context.

TF-IDF: weighting words by frequency and rarity

On the exam: slides 24-26

Raw counts reward words that appear everywhere. In earbud reviews, "earbuds" appears in nearly every review, so it tells you almost nothing about what makes one review different. TF-IDF fixes this by multiplying two factors. Term frequency (TF) is how often the word appears in this review. Inverse document frequency (IDF) measures how rare the word is across the whole collection, including this review. The slide's summary is "TF-IDF = frequent here × rare elsewhere = informative".

TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t),   with   IDF(t) = ln(N / df(t))

TF(t, d) is the number of times term t appears in document d. N is the number of documents in the collection. df(t), the document frequency, is the number of documents that contain t at least once. ln is the natural logarithm. A word in every document has df = N, so IDF = ln(1) = 0 and its weight is zero however often it appears. A word in only one document gets the largest IDF, ln(N).

Slide 24 gives two words to compare. "earbuds", if it appears in nearly every review, has low IDF and distinguishes reviews weakly. "firmware" is rarer, so it has higher IDF. But the slide adds a caution: interpret the context of a rare word before concluding that it indicates a business problem. A high weight tells you a word is distinctive, not that it is bad news. TF-IDF is a common baseline for document retrieval and text analysis.

Worked example: TF-IDF on three reviews (slides 25-26)

The collection has N = 3 reviews:

d1: "Great sound, perfect fit, lovely case". d2: "Terrible battery, battery died every hour". d3: "Great price, lovely case, perfect fit".

Step 1: document frequency. "fit" is in d1 and d3, so df = 2. "great" is in d1 and d3, so df = 2. "battery" is only in d2, so df = 1. "sound" is only in d1, so df = 1. "died" is only in d2, so df = 1.

Step 2: IDF with the natural log. For words in 2 of 3 reviews: ln(3/2) = ln(1.5) = 0.405, which rounds to 0.41. For words in 1 of 3 reviews: ln(3/1) = ln(3) = 1.099, which rounds to 1.10.

Step 3: weights in d2. "fit" and "great" do not appear in d2, so TF = 0 and their weight in d2 is 0 × 0.41 = 0. "battery" appears twice in d2, so its weight is 2 × 1.10 = 2.20. "died" appears once, so its weight is 1 × 1.10 = 1.10.

Step 4: the "your turn" cell. "sound" appears once in d1 and its IDF is ln(3) ≈ 1.10, so its weight in d1 is 1 × 1.10 = 1.10. Every other word in d1 ("great", "perfect", "fit", "lovely", "case") also appears in d3, so each has weight 1 × 0.41 = 0.41. "sound" is the highest weight in d1.

Business reading. In d2, "battery" has the highest weight, and reading the original review confirms that the battery is the subject of the complaint. In d1, the word that sets the review apart is "sound", because the rest of d1 repeats d3.

A high TF-IDF weight does not mean "a problem". It means "distinctive in this document". "firmware" could be distinctive because people praise a firmware update. Always read the reviews behind a high-weight word before you name it a complaint.

Exam tip

Use the natural log, ln, not log base 10. ln(3) = 1.10 and ln(1.5) = 0.41; with log base 10 you would get 0.48 and 0.18, which do not match the slide. If a word appears in every document, its IDF is ln(1) = 0, so its weight is zero.

N-grams: keeping local word order

On the exam: slide 27

An n-gram is a sequence of n consecutive words (tokens) that keeps their local order. A unigram is one word, such as 'not'. A bigram is two words, such as 'not good'. A trigram is three words, such as 'not good value'. If you add bigrams as features, "not good" becomes its own column, separate from "good", and the bag of words no longer confuses the two. In the bag-of-words example, the bigrams "great sound" and "terrible battery" would finally separate d1 from d2.

The slide illustrates context with letters: among all letters an "h" might appear about 4% of the time, but right after a "t" about 20% of the time (both numbers illustrative). The preceding letter changes the probability of the next one, and words work the same way. N-grams record this local context.

The cost is size. Each extra word of length creates many more possible combinations, so longer n-grams give larger, sparser vocabularies: more columns, and most of them are zero for most reviews. Choose the length for the task; bigrams are often enough to capture negation. The slide closes with a note: a Transformer uses a mechanism called attention to represent context, instead of a fixed table of n-gram counts, and language prediction is one application of it. You only need to know that Transformers handle context more flexibly than n-grams.

Embeddings and cosine similarity

On the exam: slides 28-29

An embedding is a vector representation that can encode patterns of word usage. Each word gets a list of coordinates, and training adjusts these coordinates using the contexts in which words appear. Words used in similar contexts end up close together. The number of dimensions depends on the model.

The famous illustration is king − man + woman ≈ queen. On slide 28, the arrow from "man" to "king" is about the same as the arrow from "woman" to "queen": similar offsets encode a relation. Start at "king", subtract the "man" direction, add the "woman" direction, and you land near "queen". The slide labels this "an analogy, not a rule". The relation is approximate, it depends on the training data, and the positions on the slide are only a sketch. For business use, the slide adds that review search still needs the original text and task-specific validation.

Once texts are vectors, you need a way to say how close two of them are. The course uses cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between two nonzero vectors.

cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

a and b are the two vectors (for example two reviews). a · b is the dot product: multiply matching coordinates and add them up. ‖a‖ and ‖b‖ are the lengths of the vectors. Dividing by the lengths removes the effect of size, so only direction matters. A value of 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular directions (unrelated), and −1 means opposite directions.

Three facts on slide 29 are easy exam material. First, smaller angle means larger cosine. Second, vector length does not affect cosine: a long review and a short review can point the same way and get a high score. Third, a zero vector has no direction, so its cosine is undefined. This happens, for example, when none of the query's words are in the vocabulary.

Worked example: the battery query (slide 29)

The query is "The battery died within an hour". Against "Power drains faster than advertised", the illustrative cosine is about 0.7. Against "The case scratches easily", it is about 0.4. The first candidate shares no words with the query, yet it ranks higher, because in an embedding space "battery died" and "power drains" point in similar directions. The second shares "The" but is about a different problem.

The reading: a keyword search for "battery" would miss the power-drain review completely. A meaning-based search finds it first. That ranking is the value of embeddings. Remember the values are illustrative and precomputed; you are expected to read them, not calculate them.

Worked example: cosine by hand (my own illustration)

Suppose two reviews have count vectors a = (2, 0, 1) and b = (4, 0, 2) over the words (battery, case, drain). Then a · b = 2×4 + 0×0 + 1×2 = 10. ‖a‖ = √(4 + 0 + 1) = √5, and ‖b‖ = √(16 + 0 + 4) = √20. So cos θ = 10 / (√5 × √20) = 10 / √100 = 10 / 10 = 1. Review b is just review a written twice as long, and cosine says they point the same way. That is what "length does not matter" means. If a third review has the vector (0, 3, 0), its dot product with a is 0, so the cosine is 0: no shared direction.

Topic models and latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)

On the exam: slides 33-36

Counting words tells you which words are common. A topic model goes one step further and asks what the reviews are about, by finding groups of words that tend to appear together. The words are observed; the topics are hidden and must be estimated.

Slide 33 starts from a documents × words count table. Four invented reviews are R1 "Comfortable fit, but the battery fades", R2 "Light on my ears; charging takes hours", R3 "Battery lasts hours after each charge" and R4 "Light fit, easy on my ears". Over six words they give this table:

Here D = 4 reviews and V = 6 words, and you choose K = 2 topics. K is chosen by the analyst, not found by the model. A fitted model estimates two tables: topic-word probabilities, with K × V = 2 × 6 numbers, and review mixtures, with D × K = 4 × 2 numbers. You can already guess the pattern: fit, ears and light go together, and battery, charge and hours go together.

The model the slides use is latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA). "Latent" means hidden. LDA is built on two mixtures. First, a review is a mixture of topics: the review "Comfortable, but the battery fades" might be 60% Comfort and 40% Battery. One review can contain both topics, which is more realistic than forcing each review into one group as clustering does. Second, each topic assigns probabilities to words: the Comfort topic gives high probability to fit, ears and light; the Battery life topic gives high probability to battery, charge and hours. A word can have nonzero probability under several topics. "battery" has probability 0.06 under Comfort and 0.35 under Battery life.

P(w = v | θd, Φ) = Σk=1K θdk φkv

This is the probability that a word drawn from review d is the word v. d is the review, v is the word, k is the topic. θdk is the share of topic k in review d (the review mixture). φkv is the probability of word v inside topic k (the topic-word probability). You multiply "how much of this topic is in the review" by "how likely this word is in the topic", then add over all topics.

Worked example: the probability of "battery" (slide 34)

The review mixture is Comfort 60% and Battery life 40%, so θ = 0.60 and 0.40. The topic-word probabilities for "battery" are P(battery | Comfort) = 0.06 and P(battery | Battery life) = 0.35.

Comfort contributes 0.60 × 0.06 = 0.036. Battery life contributes 0.40 × 0.35 = 0.140. The total is 0.036 + 0.140 = 0.176, or 17.6%.

The reading: a randomly chosen word from this review has a 17.6% chance of being "battery". Most of that chance comes from the Battery life topic, even though that topic is the smaller share of the review, because "battery" is much more likely inside that topic. All values are illustrative.

The process on the slide is observed counts, then approximate inference, then topic distributions, and the label comes only after you inspect the top words and the original reviews.

Evaluate topics before choosing an action

Slide 36 names two settings. K specifies the number of topics. α (alpha) controls prior expectations about review mixtures, roughly how many topics you expect one review to mix. Different K values, different preprocessing and even repeated fits of the same model can give different topics, so compare them. The slide also warns that lower perplexity need not produce clearer topics. Perplexity is a statistical fit score where lower means the model predicts the words better, but a better fit score can still give topics that humans find harder to interpret.

The biggest trap on this slide is that one topic can hold different meanings. A "battery" topic can contain praise ("The battery lasts for hours. Excellent."), which is a product strength; a complaint ("The battery dies after two hours."), which is a report to investigate; and a question ("How many hours should the battery last?"), which is an information need. All three use battery and hours, so the topic model puts them together. A topic tells you what people talk about, not how they feel or what they want. Inspect the original comments and their sentiment before choosing an action.

Trap

"The battery topic is large, so we have a battery problem" does not follow. A topic groups words, not attitudes. Praise, complaints and questions can share one topic. You need sentiment, and a look at the reviews, before you act.

Sentiment analysis at scale

On the exam: slides 38-39

The second half of the week asks how customers feel. The business task on slide 38 is to estimate the sentiment expressed in each review, every day. The input is the review text plus a star rating where available. The output can be one of three forms: a class (positive, negative or neutral), a sentiment score, or sentiment per aspect. An aspect is an entity, attribute or component that the text evaluates, such as sound, battery or case.

The level of analysis matters. Document-level sentiment gives one label for the whole review. Sentence-level sentiment gives one per sentence. Aspect-level sentiment gives one per feature. The slide's example: 'Great sound, terrible battery' becomes sound + and battery −. A manager can act on that, because it points to the battery.

Worked example: three analyses of one review (slide 39)

The illustrative review has 3 stars out of 5: 'Sound is amazing and the case feels premium. Battery died within a week.'

A document-level analysis labels it 'mixed'. That is an honest overall summary, but the specific issue remains unclear: a manager does not know what to fix.

An aspect-level analysis gives sound +, case + and battery −. Now the action is clear: investigate the battery problem.

A rating check reads the text alongside the 3-star rating. Here they roughly agree (a middle rating for a mixed review), but in general you should review cases where the text and the rating disagree, because those are where errors and sarcasm hide.

The point: document-level sentiment summarizes the review; aspect-level sentiment identifies specific issues.

Method 1: lexicon scoring, and its failures

On the exam: slides 40-42

A lexicon is a word list with sentiment scores or categories. Lexicon scoring uses a fixed list and adds up the hits.

score = positive hits − negative hits

Count the words in the review that are on the positive list and the words on the negative list, and subtract. A score above zero is classed positive, below zero negative, and zero neutral (the rule the course notebook uses).

A well-known lexicon is the Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER), which adds rules for English social text. The lab uses its own English word list, and for Chinese it uses SnowNLP, which returns a score between 0 and 1 (0.5 or more reads as positive). The advantages are fast scoring, low computational cost, and an inspectable word list: you can open the list and see exactly why a review got its score. The limitations on slide 40 are three. Slang: 'sick fit!' may be scored as negative, because "sick" is on the negative list even though here it is praise. Negation: 'not bad at all' needs a negation rule, otherwise "bad" counts as a negative hit. Domain-specific terms may be absent from a general list. The potential use is rapid sentiment summaries for standard language, subject to validation.

Worked example: the lexicon, word by word (slide 41)

Row C000301 of the course dataset, rated 1 star out of 5, says: 'Amazing battery, I charge them twice a day.'

Go word by word. "amazing" is a positive word: +1. "battery" is not in the list: 0. "charge" is not in the list: 0. No other word matches. The total is +1, so the review is classified POSITIVE.

That is wrong. Charging earbuds twice a day means the battery is bad, and the 1-star rating agrees. The positive word is used sarcastically. Word-level scoring cannot see sarcasm, because the meaning lives in the situation the sentence describes, not in any single word.

Slide 42 tests the challenge cases on the course data. The 4,000 synthetic reviews include 15 examples of each of three challenge families, and not all are misclassified. Negation: 'Not bad at all for the price' needs a negation rule; the notebook's English scorer flips a hit when a negator appears within two places before it, so it handles this example correctly. Sarcasm: 'Amazing battery, I charge them twice a day', where positive wording expresses dissatisfaction. Mixed language: 这个bass真的顶 ("This bass is really excellent"), where 顶 is positive slang and the English word "bass" sits inside a Chinese sentence; SnowNLP classifies the full example correctly.

The overall result on Chinese: SnowNLP matches the provided labels on 243 of 355 Chinese reviews. Check it: 243 / 355 = 0.6845, or 68.45%. So about one in three Chinese reviews gets the wrong label. The lab reports that the English course lexicon matches on 254 of 319, which is 79.62%.

Before reporting any sentiment figure, slide 42 gives three validation steps. First, inspect 50 randomly selected cases: a human sees sarcasm in seconds. Second, examine positive wording beside a 1-star rating: that disagreement is a red flag, not a compliment. Third, evaluate lexicon rules and classifier thresholds separately before changing either, so you know which change fixed what.

Trap

A sentiment score you have not checked is a number you cannot quote. A lexicon fails in predictable families (negation, sarcasm, slang, mixed language, missing domain words). Measure agreement with labels and read the errors before you report a dashboard figure.

On the exam: slides 43-46

The second method is Week 4's classification, with text features. Slide 43 lists four steps. Labels: you need a target. Reading logistic regression coefficients

Slide 46 explains how to read a fitted logistic regression when positive sentiment is the target class. Each TF-IDF word column gets a coefficient. The slide adds the text-specific warning: unigram features omit word order. If "good" gets a positive coefficient, that does not tell you what happens in "not good". As my own illustration: if "drain" had a negative coefficient, a review mentioning "drain" more would get a lower predicted probability of being positive, other words fixed. That says "drain" is associated with negative reviews in this data. It does not say that removing the word would make customers happier; the coefficient describes the model, not a lever.

Trap

A supervised model learns your labels, including their errors. If ratings are used as labels and some 5-star or 1-star reviews are sarcastic, the model learns the wrong lesson from them. Validate the target definition before training, and do not read a single-word coefficient as if it captured negation.

Sentiment analysis estimates the attitude a text expresses. The short version

Models need numbers, so text must be cleaned and represented first. Tokenizing splits text; lemmatizing maps forms to a dictionary word ("was, were, is" to "be"); stemming chops endings and may leave a non-word ("automat"). English uses spaces (nltk); Chinese uses a dictionary and probabilities (jieba), so brand terms must be added or they split wrongly. Remove stop words, but keep negation, or "not good" becomes "good". Bag of words loses order, so "great sound, terrible battery" and "terrible sound, great battery" get identical rows. TF-IDF is TF × ln(N/df): frequent here and rare elsewhere; in the three-review example battery scores 2 × 1.10 = 2.20 in d2, sound 1.10 in d1, while great and fit have IDF 0.41. N-grams keep local order at the cost of larger, sparser vocabularies. Embeddings put similar usage close together; king − man + woman ≈ queen is an analogy, not a rule. Cosine compares direction only (1 same, 0 perpendicular, −1 opposite), ignores length, is undefined for a zero vector, and a high cosine does not prove the same complaint. LDA treats each review as a mixture of topics and each topic as word probabilities (0.60 × 0.06 + 0.40 × 0.35 = 0.176); read the word bars and the reviews before naming a topic, and remember one topic can mix praise, complaints and questions. For sentiment, aspect level tells you what to fix. The lexicon (positive hits minus negative hits) is cheap and auditable but misses sarcasm (C000301 scores +1 at 1 star); SnowNLP agrees on only 243 of 355 Chinese reviews (68.45%), so validate before reporting. Supervised learning is Week 4 with TF-IDF columns and needs good labels, which star ratings are not. An LLM zero-shot with fixed output fields can catch sarcasm but costs more and varies. Route clean language to a lexicon, stable labeled tasks to a supervised model, and aspects, sarcasm or mixed language to LLM-assisted analysis.

Show the answer

(b). A query with no known words gives a zero vector, which has no direction, so cosine is undefined (slides 29 and 32). Most of the data you have met so far sits in tables: one row per customer, one row per review. Nodes, edges and components

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

A network has two parts. A node (also called a vertex) is the thing being connected: a person, a company, an account. An edge (also called a tie or a link) is a relationship between two nodes: collaborates with, reposts, mentions. The lecture uses five words for everything else. An edge is undirected when the relationship is mutual by nature, such as a collaboration where both accounts appeared together. An edge is directed when it has a direction: account X reposted account Y, which does not mean Y reposted X. A component is an island of connected nodes: inside it, every node can reach every other node by following edges, and no edge leaves the island. The largest component is where most of the action is.

Reachability follows directly from the edges you are given. If two components have no edge between them, a message that moves only along edges cannot cross from one to the other, however close they look on the drawing. The picture is just a layout; only the edge list is data. With directed edges you must also respect the arrow: an arrow from U to P lets a message go from U to P, never from P back to U. Social network analysis (SNA) is the general method of network analysis applied to people, and it adds social ideas such as brokers and weak ties.

The whole examined section uses one small example from the course network of 240 creator and brand accounts and 1,162 ties. It is a corner of eight accounts with eight undirected ties, where a tie means repeated collaboration. On the left, A003, A005 and A038 form a triangle, and A173 hangs off A038. In the middle, A012 links A003 to A126. On the right, A126 has two tail accounts, A073 and A089. Every account can reach every other, so the corner is one component. Which account matters most? Three instincts

On the exam: slides 17-18

Slide 17 shows the corner and asks a simple question: which account matters most here? The class usually gives three different answers, and each one is a reasonable instinct. The first instinct points to the most ties, the popular one. The second points to the crossing, the broker who sits where the two sides meet. The third points to the fastest reach, the insider who can get a message to everyone in the fewest steps. The point of the slide is that "matters most" is not one question. It is three questions, and each one has its own measure.

Slide 18 names the three measures. Degree asks "how many ties?" and finds the popular account. Betweenness asks "how often are you the bridge?" and finds the broker. Closeness asks "how fast can you reach everyone?" and finds the insider. Together they are called centrality measures, because each one gives a different meaning to being central.

The slide adds a fourth idea, for concept only: eigenvector centrality and PageRank, the prestige account. Here an account is important if its contacts are important. Ten ties to well-connected accounts count for more than ten ties to isolated ones. PageRank, which Google introduced in 1998 to rank web pages, uses the same trick: a page matters if pages that matter link to it. You do not need to compute it; you need to recognise it as "connected to the well-connected". The managerial line at the bottom of slide 18 tells you why any of this matters: the measures tell you whom to seed, whom to retain, and whom to watch.

Degree: counting ties

On the exam: slides 19-20

Degree is the simplest measure: count the ties an account has, nothing else. The slide calls it local popularity, because it only looks at an account's immediate neighbours. You do not need any algorithm, only the edge list.

Worked example: degree in the corner (slide 19)

Go through the edge list and count how often each account appears. A003 appears in three edges (with A005, A038 and A012), so its degree is 3. A038 appears with A003, A005 and A173: degree 3. A126 appears with A012, A073 and A089: degree 3. A005 has A003 and A038: degree 2. A012 has A003 and A126: degree 2. A073, A089 and A173 each have one tie: degree 1. As a check, the degrees add up to 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 16, which is twice the 8 edges, because every edge has two ends.

The business reading: three accounts tie at the top with three ties each. The slide's bottom line says it plainly: the count alone cannot separate the three leaders. Degree gives you a shortlist, not a winner.

Slide 20 lists what degree buys you. First, reach: degree is a measure of audience size, so it is the first filter when a brand shortlists accounts. Second, seeding deals: the count prices the placement, which is why influencer fees follow follower numbers. Third, it is fast to read: no algorithm needed.

The same slide lists what degree misses, and this is the part exams like. Degree misses position. Three ties say nothing about where those ties lead. A003 and A038 both hold three ties, but A003's ties lead across to the other side of the corner, while A038's ties mostly stay inside the left triangle plus one tail. Degree also cannot tell real ties from fake ones: bought followers raise the count without raising real reach. And the broker in this corner, A012, holds only two ties, so a ranking by degree would put it in the lower half. The slide's summary: a high count answers "how many", never "where".

Trap

"The account with the most ties is the most important" is the classic wrong answer. Degree measures popularity. It does not tell you whether the account holds the network together, and it can be inflated with bought followers. If the question is about connecting groups or about what breaks when someone leaves, degree is the wrong measure.

Betweenness: counting the paths through you

On the exam: slides 21-22

Betweenness asks how often an account lies on the shortest path between two other accounts. Take every pair of accounts. Find the shortest route between them. If that route passes through account v (and v is not one of the two ends), v gets a point. Add up the points over all pairs, and you have v's betweenness. An account with high betweenness is a broker: many conversations have to pass through it. In the corner every pair has exactly one shortest path, because the only loop is the left triangle, so you can count whole pairs. In bigger networks, when a pair has several equally short paths, each path carries a share of the point.

Worked example: betweenness by hand (slide 21)

Start with A003. Ask which pairs must go through it. A005, A038 and A173 are on the left. A012, A126, A073 and A089 are on the right. Every route from the left group to the right group crosses A003, because A003 is the only left account tied to A012. That gives 3 left accounts × 4 right accounts = 12 pairs. Pairs inside the left group do not use A003: A005 and A038 are tied directly, and A005 reaches A173 through A038. So A003's betweenness is 12.

A012 is the mirror image. The four accounts on its left (A003, A005, A038, A173) can reach the three on its right (A126, A073, A089) only through A012: 4 × 3 = 12 pairs. Betweenness 12.

A126: the five accounts on the far side (A003, A005, A012, A038, A173) reach A073 only through A126, and the same five reach A089 only through A126. That is 5 + 5 = 10 pairs. A073 and A089 are not tied to each other, so their own pair also goes through A126: one more. Total 5 + 5 + 1 = 11.

A038: A173's only tie is to A038, so A173 reaches each of the other six accounts through A038. That is 6 pairs. Betweenness 6.

Everyone else, A005, A073, A089 and A173, scores 0. They lie on no shortest path between two other accounts. A005 is the telling case: it has the same degree as A012 (two ties each), yet A012 carries 12 paths and A005 carries none. The slide's bottom line: position, not popularity.

Slide 22 explains what betweenness buys. The first use is crossing communities: if you want a message to travel from one group into another, seed it at the broker, because the broker is where the groups touch. The second use is finding change agents. The slide cites Battilana and Casciaro (2012), who found that people who drive organisational change tend to sit in high-betweenness positions: they bridge groups that do not otherwise talk, so they see more information and can carry ideas across. The photo on the slide, a single bridge, makes the point: every crossing pays it a visit.

The third point is the risk side. A broker can be a single point of failure. Where no second road exists, removing the broker splits the map. So high betweenness is good news when you want to spread something, and a warning when you depend on one account to hold two groups together. The slide closes with a neat contrast: degree never saw the crossing; betweenness sees nothing else.

Exam tip

For a betweenness count by hand, do not list all 21 pairs blindly. For each account, ask: "if I delete this account, which accounts end up on different sides?" Multiply the group sizes on each side, and add pairs on the same side that still had to pass through it (like A073 and A089 for A126). In the corner this gives 12, 12, 11 and 6 quickly.

Closeness: how fast you reach everyone

On the exam: slide 23

Closeness measures how near an account is to everyone else. For each account, find the shortest distance in hops (number of edges) to every other account, and add those distances up. A small distance sum means the account can get a message to everyone quickly. To turn the sum into a score where bigger means better, the slide divides the number of other accounts by the sum.

Closeness = (n − 1) / distance sum

Here n is the number of accounts in the network, so n − 1 is the number of other accounts (7 in the corner). The distance sum is the total of the shortest hop distances from this account to every other account. A lower sum gives a higher closeness. The highest possible score is 1, for an account tied directly to everyone.

Worked example: closeness in the corner (slide 23)

A003: its neighbours A005, A038 and A012 are 1 hop away. A126 and A173 are 2 hops away (through A012 and A038). A073 and A089 are 3 hops away (A003 to A012 to A126 to the tail). Sum: 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 13. Closeness = 7 / 13 = 0.538.

A012: A003 and A126 are 1 hop; A005, A038, A073 and A089 are 2 hops; A173 is 3 hops. Sum: 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 13. Closeness = 7 / 13 = 0.538, exactly the same as A003.

A126: A012, A073 and A089 are 1 hop; A003 is 2; A005 and A038 are 3; A173 is 4. Sum: 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 = 15. Closeness = 7 / 15 = 0.467.

What a community is

On the exam: slide 29

The second examined part of the week moves from single accounts to groups. A community is a set of nodes that is denser inside than outside: the members have many ties to each other and few ties to the rest of the network. The slide says this is the whole definition. Its picture shows two dense blobs with one thin crossing between them. The slide's phrase "markets within the market" is the business meaning: a large network is often several smaller networks loosely joined.

What does a community buy you? The slide lists natural segments (groups of customers or creators who actually interact), regional pockets (a city or district where ties cluster), and fraud rings: accounts that trade only with each other. A fraud ring shows up as a community because its members are very densely tied inside and barely tied to anyone outside, which is unusual for honest buyers and sellers. The key line on the slide is that nobody declares the groups: the algorithm finds them in the ties.

Modularity and the Louvain search

On the exam: slide 30

To find communities, a computer needs two things: a score that says how good a split is, and a search method that looks for a split with a high score.

The score is modularity, written Q. Every way of splitting the network into groups gets a Q value. The slide defines it in words: the edges inside the groups, minus what chance alone would give. Higher is tighter. "What chance alone would give" matters because big, well-connected groups would hold many inside ties even if ties were placed at random. Modularity only rewards inside ties beyond that random expectation. A Q near zero means the split is no better than chance.

Q = (share of edges inside the groups) − (share expected inside the groups by chance)

The first part is the actual fraction of all edges that fall inside groups. The second part is the fraction you would expect inside those same groups if the ties were scattered at random, keeping each account's number of ties. A good split has many more inside edges than chance would predict, so Q is clearly positive. The week notes write the full version as Q = Σ over groups of [ lc/m − (dc/2m)2 ], where m is the number of edges, lc the edges inside group c, and dc the sum of degrees in group c. The slides only ask you to read Q, not to compute it.

The search is the Louvain method (Blondel et al., 2008). It works in three plain steps. Every account starts alone, in a group of one. Then the algorithm moves each account to the neighbouring group that raises Q the most. It repeats until nothing moves, because no single move can raise Q any further. The week notes add that Louvain then merges each group into a single super-node and runs the moves again, but the three steps on the slide are what you need. The important feature for the exam is that you never tell Louvain how many groups to find; the number of groups comes out of the search. Platforms run this nightly on millions of nodes; the lab ran it on 240 accounts and found seven communities at Q = 0.808.

The slide also mentions a second method, label propagation: every account starts with its own label, and at each step an account takes the label most common among its neighbours. Labels spread through dense areas until they stabilise, and each final label is a community.

Then comes the honest limit. Modularity has a blind spot for size: small groups get merged into big ones, because the score cannot see a group below a certain size. If you are looking for a tiny fraud ring of four accounts inside a network of millions, a modularity search may swallow it into a larger community.

Trap

A high Q says the split is tight. It does not say the groups mean anything in business terms, and it does not promise that every small real group was found. To check meaning, you compare the groups with an outside variable, as the lab did with the account handles.

Here is where the number comes from (from the week notes, for understanding only). There are 8 edges. The left group holds 4 inside edges (the triangle plus A038–A173) and its degrees add to 3 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 9. The right group holds 3 inside edges (A012–A126, A126–A073, A126–A089) and its degrees add to 2 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 7. Only one edge, A003–A012, crosses. How things travel: cascades and seeds

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

The rest of the lecture asks how things spread through the map. Information, adoption and rumours move along ties, not through the air. A cascade is a spread that starts from one or more seeds and moves outward, round by round. The lecture's warm-up uses the corner with the simplest rule: every newly active account activates all its neighbours in the next round. Seed A003 (round 0). Round 1: A005, A012 and A038. Round 2: A126 and A173. Round 3: A073 and A089, and the corner is covered. Seed A012 and the corner is also covered in three rounds. Seed A126 and it takes four, because the spread has to drain one hop per round down the left side. Same map, different clock: the seed sets the speed.

The mock exam's Case 2 uses this kind of round-by-round rule on a small directed network, so learn the rules well. An account activates only once. A newly activated account sends in the next round, not immediately. If two arrows reach the same account in the same round, or a later arrow reaches an account that is already active, it does not count as extra reach: you count distinct accounts. And you can only move in the direction of the arrow, so an account whose only arrow points into the active set can never be activated. Do not forget to state whether your total includes the seed.

Real spread is random: a message passes along a tie only with some probability. The lab's independent cascade model gives each newly active account one chance to activate each neighbour, with probability 0.10. Because the outcome changes from run to run, one run proves nothing. The lecture compares seed choices fairly: the same 240-account network, the same model and probability, equal budgets (one head account costs what three waist accounts cost), and 300 runs each, reporting the average reach. One head account, A003, reached 11.6 accounts on average. Three waist accounts with 12 ties each and barely overlapping neighbourhoods reached 24.2, which is 2.09 times the head. One bridge account, A126, reached 10.5, so it still lost to the head. Portfolios beat heroes; one bridge alone is not a strategy. The lecture also mentions thresholds (people often adopt only after two or more exposures), weak ties (Granovetter, 1973: acquaintances carry new information across groups) and the K-factor, K = invites sent × conversion, where K above 1 means the spread sustains itself.

Exam tip

For a "is seed X the best?" question, the marks are for fairness: keep the network, transmission rule, probability, budget and number of rounds the same; run each seed many times because transmissions are random; report the average number of distinct accounts reached (with the same convention about counting the seed), and ideally the spread of results too. One successful cascade, or a simulation where every transmission succeeds, cannot rank seeds.

Network effects: registrations versus active ties

Background: not examined, but it helps the examined pages make sense

The lecture separates two ideas. Network analysis is the method: it reads the shape of the network. A network effect is the economics: each additional user makes the platform more valuable to every other user. It cares about size, not shape. Metcalfe's law prices a network at about n2; Odlyzko and Tilly's correction is n × log n, because most ties are weak or dormant. The manager's version on slide 48 is the part to remember: value tracks active, weighted ties, not registrations. A platform with a million registrations and ten thousand monthly transactions is worth the ten thousand.

The case section shows this in marketplaces. Vinted kept listing free for sellers and charged buyers a protection fee; BlaBlaCar was free in France for about six years before charging; Xianyu launched into Taobao's existing graph and never had a cold start. The mock exam's B5 asks the same idea from the other side: if registrations double while transactions stay flat, value has not quadrupled. A good answer names an activity measure, such as completed monthly transactions or distinct buyer-seller pairs that traded this month, and adds that mapping those ties answers a structural question a user count cannot: are there disconnected components, is trade concentrated on a few hubs, are some groups cut off?

The short version

"Which account matters most" has three answers. Degree counts ties and measures reach, but it ignores position and can be inflated with bought followers. Betweenness counts the shortest paths that pass through an account; in the corner A003 and A012 score 12, A126 scores 11, A038 scores 6 and the rest score 0, and these brokers are exactly the accounts whose removal breaks the map, which makes them both change agents and single points of failure. Closeness is (n − 1) divided by the distance sum; A003 and A012 tie at 7/13 = 0.538 and A126 has 7/15 = 0.467. On the full 240-account map, A003 leads degree with 34 ties while A126 leads betweenness with only 15 ties, so pick the measure to match the job. A community is denser inside than outside; modularity Q scores a split as inside ties minus what chance would give, Louvain searches for a high Q without being told how many groups to find, and it can merge small groups. In the corner, the ties put A012 with the city side at Q = 0.367 even though its handle says food. Clustering groups by features with k chosen in advance; communities group by ties and find k themselves.

(b). Removing K deletes the ties K–J, K–L and K–M. L and M each lose their only tie, so the pieces are {G, H, J}, {L} and {M}: three pieces, just like removing A126 in the corner. P8.2 (Calculation) Use the same six accounts as P8.1. Compute the betweenness of J and K by hand, and the closeness of J, G and L using closeness = (n − 1) / distance sum. Which account would you choose to announce a product recall fastest, and which would you worry about losing?

Show the answer

Betweenness of J: the left group {G, H} reaches the right group {K, L, M} only through J, so 2 × 3 = 6. Betweenness of K: the left group {G, H, J} reaches L and M only through K, which is 3 × 2 = 6, and L and M reach each other through K, one more pair, so 7. Closeness, with n − 1 = 5: J's distances are G 1, H 1, K 1, L 2, M 2, sum 7, so 5/7 = 0.714. K's sum is also 7 (J, L, M at 1; G, H at 2), so K ties J at 0.714. G's distances are H 1, J 1, K 2, L 3, M 3, sum 10, so 5/10 = 0.500. L's distances are K 1, J 2, M 2, G 3, H 3, sum 11, so 5/11 = 0.455. P8.3 (Case reading) A fictional bakery app maps seven accounts. An arrow X → Y means a message can travel from X to Y only. The complete list of arrows is: T1 → T2, T1 → T3, T2 → T4, T3 → T4, T3 → T5, T5 → T3, T4 → T6, T7 → T1. Only T1 is active at round 0. Show the answer

(i) Round 1: T2 and T3 (T1's two outgoing arrows). Round 2: T4 (from T2 and from T3) and T5 (from T3). Total after round 2: T1, T2, T3, T4 and T5, which is 5 distinct accounts. Slides 6 to 12 tell a short history. Livestream 1.0 was talent shows on desktop computers. Livestream 2.0 was game streaming, which then split into niches. Today a streamer can earn in five ways: viewer gifts, sales commission, a slot fee per product, app promotion paid per install, and paid co-streaming. Slide 11 adds three types of content. User generated content (UGC) is made by amateurs. Professionally generated content (PGC) is made inside professional routines. What the numbers decide: elasticity, average and marginal return

Not on the exam: Week 9 is outside the final's scope

Slide 34 brings back the Week 1 map of four parties: the platform, which sees everything; the streamer, who holds attention; the brand, which pays; and the consumer, who responds. Slide 36 names three things advertising does that a single number hides. Its effect arrives late. It leaks across channels, so crediting one channel is a convention, not a measurement. And it has diminishing returns: the same extra spend does more on a small budget than on a large one.

elasticity = (percentage change in sales) / (percentage change in spend) = (ΔQ / Q) / (ΔS / S)

Q is sales and S is advertising spend. The source's estimate is that spending 1 percent more goes with about 0.1 percent more sales, so the elasticity is 0.1 / 1 = 0.1. It has no currency unit, and it holds only near the budget where it was measured.

Trap

An elasticity of 0.1 does not mean "one yuan of spend brings 0.1 yuan of sales". That reads a ratio as a slope. The 94-page file adds a rough conversion: sales per extra yuan ≈ 0.1 × baseline sales ÷ baseline spend, so the starting amounts decide the answer. The 95-page copy skips the conversion and just says to read it as a ratio, not a slope.

Worked example: two hundred thousand yuan, two platforms (slides 38 and 39)

A brand has spent 1,000,000 yuan on Douyin and earned 2,000,000. It has spent 800,000 on Kuaishou and earned 1,200,000. The average return is 2,000,000 / 1,000,000 = 2.0 on Douyin and 1,200,000 / 800,000 = 1.5 on Kuaishou. All of the remaining 200,000 must go to one platform. On averages, Douyin wins.

But the next 200,000 does not earn the average. It earns the marginal return, meaning the revenue from one more yuan. That return falls as spend rises, so it sits below the average. Douyin has already absorbed more, so it may be further along its curve. The table gives two points on two unknown curves, and two points are not a curve. The 95-page copy says to split the money, measure both platforms, and let the next budget follow the result. The 94-page file says to ask for evidence on marginal returns first, and adds that a split test needs a separate budget or permission to break the one-platform rule.

This is Week 5 again. An elasticity is a coefficient. It describes how sales and spend moved together near one budget, not what happens if you push harder. "A coefficient is not a lever" applies directly, and the honest answer is once again an experiment.

Worked example: a matched pair (slide 60)

S01 and S02 had the same senior host, the same fruit category and the same two hours, one week apart. Only S01 ran a coupon. S01 had 7,065 orders from 114,618 entrants (6.16 percent) and 627,173 yuan of GMV (5.47 yuan per viewer). S02 had 4,957 orders from 113,048 entrants (4.38 percent) and 469,154 yuan (4.15 yuan per viewer). Conversion rose 1.78 points and value per viewer 1.32 yuan, but dwell stayed at 4.66 against 4.67 minutes. The coupon turned people already present into buyers without keeping anyone longer. A studio that used dwell as its headline metric would have seen nothing. The claim stays modest: two sessions are two sessions, and the gap runs in the expected direction.

Trap

Every headline number is a ratio, so name the denominator first. Conversion can rise while revenue falls, if casual viewers leave. The ratio you rank by changes the ranking: S06, a seafood session, is last on conversion (4.01 percent) but third on value per viewer (5.63 yuan), because few buyers spend a lot each. A pooled figure hides variation: the coupon multiple of 9.74 splits into 11.10, 4.62 and 5.57 across S01, S03 and S05, mostly because their quiet minutes differ (practice solutions). And GMV leaves out returns and cancellations.

Practice

P9.1 A fictional tea studio scores its actions against plain-talk minutes. Giveaway minutes show 3.1 times the people in the room and 0.5 times the orders. Coupon minutes show 1.4 times the people and 8.0 times the orders. A manager says: "Cut the giveaway, it does not sell." Which reply is best? (A) Agree: orders below 1.0 mean the giveaway destroys sales. (B) Disagree: the giveaway has the highest room multiple, so it is the best action. (C) Partly agree: it does not sell while it runs, but it may build the audience that a later coupon converts, so test removing it before deciding. (D) Agree: the room multiple proves the coupon works.

Show the answer

C. A multiple below 1.0 means fewer orders during giveaway minutes than during plain talk. It does not show that the giveaway harms the session, because its job is attention. B mistakes attention for purchase, and D mixes up which multiple belongs to which action. C treats the running order as a sequence, where each block inherits the room the last one left, and asks for a test rather than a verdict from averages.

P9.2 Two fictional sessions. Session A had 40,000 entrants, 1,800 orders and a GMV of 216,000 yuan, and its per-minute viewer counts add up to 180,000. Session B had 20,000 entrants, 1,100 orders and a GMV of 88,000 yuan. Compute the conversion rate and value per viewer for both sessions, and A's average dwell. Which session is "better"?

Show the answer

A: conversion = 1,800 / 40,000 = 4.5 percent; value per viewer = 216,000 / 40,000 = 5.40 yuan; dwell = 180,000 / 40,000 = 4.5 minutes. B: conversion = 1,100 / 20,000 = 5.5 percent; value per viewer = 88,000 / 20,000 = 4.40 yuan. B converts a larger share of a smaller audience. A earns more per viewer and more in total. Which is "better" depends on the question you are asking, so ranking by one ratio is a choice, not a neutral summary. Read each ratio beside the audience size, and remember that GMV ignores returns.

P9.3 A fictional snack brand spends 400,000 yuan a month on advertising and sells 10 million yuan. Its estimated elasticity is 0.1 and its margin on sales is 30 percent. It considers raising spend by 10 percent. Roughly how much extra sales and profit should it expect, is the raise worth it, and what two cautions apply?

Show the answer

Ten percent more spend is 40,000 yuan. Sales should rise by about 0.1 × 10% = 1 percent, so 10,000,000 × 1% = 100,000 yuan. The slide's conversion gives the same result: 0.1 × 10,000,000 / 400,000 = 2.5 yuan of sales per extra yuan, and 40,000 × 2.5 = 100,000. At a 30 percent margin, that is 30,000 yuan of extra profit against 40,000 yuan of extra spend, so the raise loses about 10,000 yuan. First caution: the elasticity was measured at the current budget, and with diminishing returns the next yuan may earn even less. Second caution: an elasticity is a coefficient from observed data, not a lever, so only a test can confirm the effect.

Last chapter How to write answers that earn the marks

You now know the methods. This chapter is about turning what you know into marks on Thursday morning. I built it from the marking guide that came with the mock exam, because that guide shows exactly how the examiners think. They do not give marks for length or for nice phrases. They give one mark for each distinct, correct point, and they say so again and again. Once you see the pattern, you can write answers that hit every point on purpose.

The pattern behind almost every mark

Look at any Part B question in the mock guide and the 6 marks split the same way: two points of two marks each, plus a third point of two marks, and each two-mark point splits again into "state it" and "connect it to the scenario". When the question involves data, the points almost always come out as four moves. First, the number, worked out from the exhibit. Second, what that number means in plain words. Third, the decision it supports. Fourth, one honest limit on that decision. If your answer makes all four moves in three to five sentences, you have usually covered the marking scheme without seeing it.

Take mock question B2 as an example. The review queue holds 16 + 20 = 36 messages, which is 12 more than the team can check (the number). Raising the lower boundary to 0.50 sends the 16 messages in that band straight to "keep", so the queue falls to 20 and fits the capacity (what it means and the decision). But some of those 16 may be spam, so missed spam could rise, and the table cannot say by how much because it has no true labels (the limit). That is four sentences and all 6 marks. Notice what the guide refuses to reward: calling all 16 messages spam. You only say what the evidence supports.

Exam tip

Before you write, underline every number in the question and every limit it states, such as "at most four predictors" or "a team of three can review 24 messages". The marking almost always checks that you used each of them. A limit stated in the question is a hint that the "obvious" answer is not allowed.

The limits the examiners keep rewarding

The fourth move, the limit, is the one students forget, and it is often worth a whole mark. Over and over, the mock rewards the same small set of honest cautions. A model that settled is not the best possible model: a converged K-means run is the best among the starts you tried, not proof that no start could do better. A model that fits the past closely may predict the future badly: lower training error is not lower prediction error, and you compare models on the same new weeks. Errors that sum to zero are not zero errors: a miss of +10 and a miss of −10 cancel in the sum but not in the squared total of 200. A small sample is limited evidence: three validation weeks cannot settle the question for good. Counting users is not counting activity: registrations can double while active ties stay flat. And a comparison is only fair if everything else is held equal: the same network, the same probabilities, the same budget, the same number of rounds, repeated many times.

Learn these as sentences you can drop into almost any answer. "This is the best result among the runs we tried, not proof of the best possible result." "This compares fit on the data used for training, not prediction on new data." "With only three weeks this is limited evidence, so I would validate further." "These comments support investigation, not a claim about how often the problem occurs among all users."

Trap

The guide says that "all models are imperfect" earns nothing. A limit only scores when it is specific to the case and explains its consequence. "Only three validation weeks, so a different season could favour model Q" is a limit. "More data is always better" is not.

Part A: how to avoid losing easy marks

Multiple-choice questions in this course are rarely about obscure facts. They test whether you can apply a definition exactly to a tiny example. A 1/0 basket table records presence, not quantity, so three print packs are still just a 1. "Ten days" is not "less than seven days", so you follow the "no" branch of a tree. Eighty shares are twice forty shares because a count has a true zero, which makes it a ratio scale. A component is a group with no path to the rest, and moving the drawing around adds no edge. When two options look right, the wrong one usually confuses two neighbouring ideas: support with confidence, precision with recall, Ridge with LASSO, tokenizing with lemmatizing, degree with betweenness, a cluster with a community. Read every option against the exact words of the definition.

Remember the mechanical rule too. You circle your choice and write its letter in the answer grid, and if they differ, the grid is what gets marked. Leave two minutes at the end to compare them.

Part C: working through a case

A case gives you a scenario, one or two exhibits, and three sub-questions with their own marks, usually 8, 6 and 6. Treat the marks as a guide to how many distinct points each part needs. An 8-mark question wants about eight scored elements. In the mock's Apriori question, that meant the minimum count, then each candidate kept or discarded with its reason, then the warning that surviving the pruning is not the same as being frequent.

Show your working the way the guide marks it: set up the formula with the numbers in it, then give the result. If you set it up correctly but slip on the arithmetic, you still get the method mark, and a later step that correctly follows your earlier wrong number is not penalised twice. So never skip the setup line, even when you can do the sum in your head. Put units on everything: orders, squared orders, viewers, percent.

When a sub-question asks you to choose, say your choice in the first sentence and then justify it with the comparison: "P, because its squared prediction error on the same three later weeks is 200 against Q's 650." Then add the limit. If you think an alternative is defensible, the guide often accepts it, as long as it meets the stated conditions and the evidence supports it.
