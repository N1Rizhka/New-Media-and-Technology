Week 1 Introduction and Fundamentals I

What the field is: one definition, three branches

On the exam

Every platform you use keeps a log of what people watch, click, buy and say. A business that wants to use that log has two problems. First, it must know what the new media world looks like: which technologies create the data. Second, it must know what kind of numbers it is holding, because a number can look like a number and still not allow you to average it, divide it, or compare it. Both problems start with a working definition of the field, then the technologies that create the data, then the vocabulary of objects, attributes and measurement scales.

The working definition you should be able to write from memory is: new media technology and analytics studies how digital media and data analytics reshape business, information systems, and marketing. The definition has a cause and three things it affects. The cause is two forces together: digital media (feeds, livestreams, AI content, extended reality) and data analytics (the methods that turn platform logs into decisions). Those forces change three fields, called the three branches.

New Media Technology and Analytics: three branches

  • New Media Technology and Analytics
    • Business: how firms create and capture value
      • platform business models
      • pricing, channels, monetisation
    • Information systems: the technologies and data infrastructures
      • platforms and recommendation
      • data pipelines, AI and AIGC
    • Marketing: reach, understand, measure people
      • consumers, engagement, targeting
      • campaign measurement

The business branch asks how firms create and capture value: platform business models, and pricing, channels and monetisation. A typical business question is "how does a livestream platform make money, and who keeps the margin?" The information systems branch covers the technologies and data infrastructures the field is built on: platforms and recommendation, data pipelines, and AI and AI-generated content (AIGC). A typical question is "what does the recommendation system log, and how does that data reach an analyst?" The marketing branch is about how organisations reach, understand, and measure people: consumers, engagement, targeting, and campaign measurement. A typical question is "did this campaign bring buyers, or only views?"

Each field is an entry point, not a fence. A business student starts from the cases, an information systems student from data types, pipelines and the method weeks, and a marketing student from the Week 2 frameworks and the campaign cases. The course connects all three, and that connection is the definition.

Exam tip

If a question gives you a scenario and asks which branch it belongs to, look at the verb. "Capture value, price, monetise" points to business. "Store, log, recommend, build a pipeline" points to information systems. "Reach, target, engage, measure a campaign" points to marketing. Real problems usually touch all three, so name the main branch and say why.

On the exam

A live page is a table. Every box on a livestream page (room, category, followers, popularity, heat index, hours live, rating) is a column waiting to be filled. The running example is six streamers with seven attributes, and every concept in the rest of the week fits inside this table.

Two words carry the vocabulary. A dataset is objects (rows) plus attributes (columns). An object is one row, here one streamer. Other books call it a record, case, sample or observation; they all mean the row. An attribute is one column, such as followers or category. Other books call it a variable, field or feature; they all mean the column. To find the object in any table, ask "what does one row describe?"

A value is the number (or label) assigned to an attribute for one object: the cell where a row meets a column. A scale is the rule that maps a property of the world to a value. Hours live can be measured in hours or in minutes (one attribute, two measurement rules). Room ID is an integer with no minimum and no maximum. Followers is an integer with a minimum of 0 and a maximum equal to all platform users. The platform gives you numbers but not the rule behind them, and the rule decides which arithmetic makes sense.

Trap

Do not confuse the object with a value or with a grouping column. In a table with one row per video and columns for creator, length and shares, the object is one video. The share count in a cell is a value, and "creator category" is an attribute you might group by. This was tested on the mock exam.

Four measurement scales and the operations ladder

On the exam

Four scales sit as a ladder. Each adds one property to the one before it.

Four measurement scales

  1. Nominalnames (room ID); labels only
  2. Ordinalranks (tier); order, not gaps
  3. Intervalgaps, no true zero (heat)
  4. Ratiotrue zero (followers); gaps and ratios

Nominal values are names: they only tell you whether two things are the same or different (room ID, gender, category). Ordinal values are ranks: they have an order, but the gaps between them have no fixed size (popularity High, Mid, Low; tier Master, Elite, Warrior). Interval values have equal gaps but no true zero (the heat index). Ratio values have equal gaps and a true zero, where zero means "none of it" (followers).

Every column of the extended streamer table has a scale label, and you should know these labels because they show the reasoning. Room, gender and category are nominal. Popularity, recommended (Front page, Trending, Not listed) and tier are ordinal. Last live (a date), heat and rating are interval. Followers, resolution and hours live are ratio. The 1 to 5 rating is labelled interval: each step between stars is treated as equal in size. A date is interval because gaps between dates are meaningful but there is no "zero date". Resolution is ratio if you read it as a count of pixels.

A second, separate split cuts attributes another way. A discrete attribute takes finite or countable values, usually stored as integers: room ID, or the number of words in a comment. Trap

Storage type does not decide the scale. Room IDs are stored as integers, but they are nominal, so the computer will happily give you a "mean room ID" that means nothing. Always ask what the number means before you compute with it. To justify a "twice as many" claim, point to the true zero: 80 shares is twice 40 shares because zero shares means no shares. One summary per scale, and the superstar problem

On the exam

Because each scale allows different operations, each scale has its own summary.

One summary per scale

Medianordinalmiddle of the sorted ranks; robust to extremes

For nominal data you use the mode, the most frequent value. In the six streamers, the categories tie: Lifestyle and Gaming appear 2 of 6 times each, so there are two modes. For ordinal data you use the median, the middle of the sorted ranks, and percentiles. A streamer who received 85 gifts ranks at the 95th percentile of 100 streamers, which means 95% received fewer. For interval and ratio data you may also use the mean and the standard deviation, because gaps are meaningful, so adding and averaging make sense.

One warning: one superstar doubles the mean. Creator data is heavily skewed: a few accounts are huge and most are small. The mean adds everything and divides by the count, so a single huge value pulls it up. The median only looks at the middle position, so it is robust to extremes.

Worked example (my own illustration): one superstar joins five small creators

Five small creators have 10K, 12K, 15K, 18K and 20K followers. The mean is (10 + 12 + 15 + 18 + 20) / 5 = 75 / 5 = 15K, and the median (the third value) is also 15K. Now one superstar with 110K followers joins. The total becomes 185K, and the mean is 185 / 6 = 30.8K, about double. The median of six values is the average of the third and fourth sorted values, (15 + 18) / 2 = 16.5K, which barely moved.

Business reading: if an agency tells a brand "our average creator has 30.8K followers", the brand will expect a typical creator far bigger than it will get. Five of the six creators have 20K or fewer. For skewed creator data, report the median for the typical case, and use the mean only when you really need the total divided by the count.

Entropy: how much information is in an answer

On the exam

Entropy measures how much you learn from an answer. The principle is "information = uncertainty: the harder to guess, the more an answer tells". If you can almost always guess the answer before you hear it, hearing it tells you little. If every answer is equally likely, hearing it tells you a lot.

H(U) = −Σ pi log2 pi

pi is the probability (here, the share of rows) of each value the attribute can take. You multiply each share by its log base 2, add the results, and put a minus sign in front so the answer is positive (logs of numbers below 1 are negative). H is measured in bits. A value with probability 1 adds nothing, because log2 1 = 0. H is largest when all values are equally likely.

A useful shortcut: −p log2 p is the same as p × log2(1/p). The values you need are log2 6 = 2.585, log2 3 = 1.585, and log2(6/5) = 0.263.

Worked example: three questions about the six streamers

The table has six rooms. Gender is Female for rooms 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, and Male for room 5. Category is Film & TV, Gaming, Lifestyle, Lifestyle, Gaming, Music.

Which room? Six different rooms, each with probability 1/6. H = 6 × (1/6 × log2 6) = log2 6 = 2.585, shown as 2.58.

Gender? The split is 5/6 Female and 1/6 Male. The Female term is 5/6 × log2(6/5) = 0.833 × 0.263 = 0.219. The Male term is 1/6 × log2 6 = 0.167 × 2.585 = 0.431. H = 0.219 + 0.431 = 0.650, shown as 0.65.

Which category? Gaming 2/6, Lifestyle 2/6, Film & TV 1/6, Music 1/6. Each 2/6 term is 1/3 × log2 3 = 0.528, and there are two, giving 1.057. Each 1/6 term is 0.431, and there are two, giving 0.862. H = 1.057 + 0.862 = 1.918, shown as 1.92.

Reading: the room number has the largest H, so it is the hardest to guess and carries the most information. Gender has the smallest H because you would guess "Female" and be right five times out of six. Category sits in between. Note that entropy depends only on the shares, never on the labels: renaming "Gaming" to anything else does not change H.

Trap

"Most information" here does not mean "most useful for a business decision". The room number has the highest entropy because every row is unique, but an ID tells you nothing about why a stream sells. Entropy measures how unpredictable an attribute is, not whether it explains an outcome. In Week 4, decision trees use the reduction of entropy to choose splits, which is a different question.

The chi-square test: are two attributes linked?

On the exam

The chi-square (χ2) test asks whether two categorical (nominal) attributes are related. Is a streamer's category related to gender, among 420 streamers? The logic has three steps. First, assume there is no link. If category and gender were unrelated, every category would have the same female share as the whole sample, and that assumption tells you the count you would expect in each cell. Second, measure how far the observed counts are from the expected counts. Third, compare that distance with a threshold, the critical value.

e = row total × column total / n

χ2 = Σ (f − e)2 / e

f is the observed count in a cell, e is the expected count if there were no link, and n is the total (420). Squaring makes all gaps positive. Dividing by e scales each gap to its cell: a gap of 5 matters more when you expected 30 than when you expected 300. Degrees of freedom (df) = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1).

"No evidence of a link" is not the same as "proven unrelated". The test only says this table, at this sample size, does not show enough difference between observed and expected counts. A larger sample could reach a different verdict. Also, the decision rule points one way: a χ2 above the critical value is evidence of a link; below it is not.

Rank correlation: do two orderings move together?

On the exam

Chi-square is for two nominal attributes. For two ordinal attributes you want to know whether the two rankings move together. Because ordinal gaps are not meaningful, you correlate the ranks, not the raw values. The tool is Spearman's rank correlation.

rs = 1 − 6 Σ di2 / ( n (n2 − 1) )

For each object i, di = xi − yi is the gap between its rank on the first attribute and its rank on the second. You square each gap, add them up, multiply by 6, and divide by n(n2 − 1), where n is the number of objects. Then subtract that fraction from 1.

Reading Spearman's rs

rsMeaning
+1the two rankings move together
0no relation
−1the rankings move in opposite directions

At +1 every di is 0, the fraction is 0, and rs = 1. At 0 there is no relation: knowing one ranking tells you nothing about the other. At −1 the top on one attribute is the bottom on the other. As my own illustration of −1, take three streamers ranked 1, 2, 3 on one attribute and 3, 2, 1 on the other. The gaps are −2, 0, 2, so Σd2 = 8, and rs = 1 − 6 × 8 / (3 × 8) = 1 − 2 = −1. Week 1 shows the question shape; the mechanics return in Weeks 4 and 5, but be ready to plug ranks into the formula if the paper prints it.

Preprocessing: aggregate, discretize, binarize

On the exam

Preprocessing shapes raw data before modelling. Three operations do most of the work.

Preprocessing: three operations

Aggregate

single events → per-stream totals

Discretize

Binarize

Aggregate: roll single events up into per-stream totals. The raw log has one row per click or order; aggregation turns it into one row per stream (total viewers, total orders, total GMV). This decides what one object means, so it is a modelling choice: totals per stream and totals per creator answer different questions.

Discretize: turn a continuous value into a grade, such as a popularity value into High, Mid or Low. Three ways set the cut points. Equal width splits the range into intervals of the same size. Equal frequency splits so that each grade holds the same number of objects. K-means places the boundaries where the data naturally separates, in the gaps between groups. Discretizing loses detail, but the result is easier to read and less sensitive to small changes.

Worked example (my own illustration): three grades for the heat index

The six heat values are 90, 80, 50, 45, 60 and −5. Equal width: the range is 90 − (−5) = 95, so each of three bins is 95 / 3 = 31.67 wide. Low is −5 up to 26.67, Mid is 26.67 up to 58.33, High is 58.33 to 90. Low gets only −5; Mid gets 45 and 50; High gets 60, 80 and 90. Equal frequency: sort the values (−5, 45, 50, 60, 80, 90) and put two in each grade: Low is −5 and 45, Mid is 50 and 60, High is 80 and 90. Notice that room 5 (heat 60) is High under equal width but Mid under equal frequency. A k-means cut would look for natural gaps, and the obvious one is the large gap between −5 and 45.

The lesson: one extreme value (−5) stretched the range, so equal width left a Low grade with a single streamer. Equal width is the easiest to explain but is distorted most by skewed data, which platform data usually is. The method you choose changes who lands in which grade.

Similarity and distance between streamers

On the exam

Similarity and distance are two views of the same idea, scaled in opposite directions. A similarity of 1 means identical; higher means more alike. A distance of 0 means identical; higher means more different. Four streamers sit on two attributes, a popularity score (x) and a tier (y), and d(S2, S3) = 1.41 is the closest pair. That number only comes out of the Euclidean (straight-line) distance.

d(p, q) = √( Σk (pk − qk)2 )

For each attribute k, take the difference between the two objects, square it, add over all attributes, and take the square root. With two attributes this is Pythagoras: the length of the straight line between two points on a plot.

Trap

Euclidean distance adds the columns as it finds them, so a column in big units dominates. The analytics ladder: describe, diagnose, predict, prescribe

On the exam

Each rung of the ladder has a definition and an example. Learn the question each rung asks, because exam questions often give you a manager's request and ask which rung it is.

The analytics ladder, rung by rung

  1. Describesummarize what the data says happened — dashboards, totals, trends: 12 streams, ¥2.1M GMV, 3.4% conversion
  2. Diagnosebreak the description apart to find the driver — views fell only on paid streams: the creative changed
  3. Predictuse patterns in past data to score future cases — classification (buy?) · regression (how much?) · Weeks 4–5
  4. Prescribeturn predictions into actions and compare options — shift 20% of budget to retargeting the flagged 8%

Describe asks "what happened?" It reports; it does not explain. Diagnose asks "why did it happen?" Breaking total views into paid and unpaid streams located the problem. Predict asks "what will happen?" Predicting needs past cases where you already know the outcome. Prescribe asks "what should we do?" Here a prediction (which 8% of viewers are flagged as likely buyers) is turned into a budget decision.

The important break is between the second and third rungs: describe and diagnose look backward at data you hold, while predict and prescribe look forward and need a model. Each rung builds on the one below it.

Trap

The word "why" signals diagnose, and "should" or "which option" signals prescribe. A request like "which streamer should get next month's budget?" is prescribe, even though it sounds simple. A request like "which viewers will buy next month?" is predict, not prescribe, because it scores cases without choosing an action.

The short version

The field studies how digital media and analytics reshape business (creating and capturing value), information systems (technologies and data infrastructures) and marketing (reaching, understanding and measuring people). Four technologies (social media, livestreaming, AI and AIGC, XR) each have a China and an international track, and together they produce five data types: behavioural events, text, networks, transactions and profiles. An app's primary platform shift is the one whose removal would kill it. Analysis follows question, data, prepare, model, decide, and climbs the ladder from describe to diagnose to predict to prescribe. In a table, a row is an object and a column is an attribute. The scale of an attribute (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) decides what you may compute: only ratio data has a true zero, so only ratio data supports "twice as much". Use the mode for nominal, the median and percentiles for ordinal, and the mean and standard deviation for interval and ratio, but prefer the median when one superstar can double the mean. Entropy is highest when values are hardest to guess (2.58 for room, 0.65 for gender, 1.92 for category). Chi-square compares observed with expected counts; 3.03 below 6.25 means no evidence of a link, not proof of no link. Spearman reads +1 as rankings moving together, 0 as no relation and −1 as opposite. Clean and shape data before modelling (aggregate, discretize, binarize), measure closeness with Euclidean distance (S2 and S3 at 1.41), and always check the denominator in conversion rate = orders / viewers.

P1.1 A fictional short-video app gives each post a "buzz score" that can be negative, and the app says a score of 0 is just a reference point, not "no buzz". Post A scored 30 last week and 60 this week. Which statement is correct? (a) The buzz doubled, because 60 = 2 × 30. (b) The buzz rose by 30 points, but you cannot say it doubled, because the scale has no true zero. (c) Nothing can be said, because the score is nominal. (d) The median of the two scores is the only valid summary.

Show the answer

(b). A score that can be negative and whose zero is only a reference point is an interval scale, like the heat index. Interval data allows adding and subtracting, so "rose by 30 points" is valid. It does not allow multiplying or dividing, so "doubled" is not valid. (a) would need a ratio scale with a true zero. (c) is wrong because the scores have order and meaningful gaps. (d) is wrong because interval data allows the mean as well as the median.

P1.3 A fictional electronics brand studies 200 viewers of its livestream. 120 watched on a phone and 80 on a PC. 50 bought and 150 did not. Observed counts: phone and bought 36, phone and did not buy 84, PC and bought 14, PC and did not buy 66. Compute the expected counts and χ2 = Σ(f − e)2/e. The critical value printed on the paper is 2.71 at α = 0.1 with df = 1. What do you conclude?

Show the answer

Expected counts use e = row total × column total / 200. Phone and bought: 120 × 50 / 200 = 30. Phone and not: 120 × 150 / 200 = 90. PC and bought: 80 × 50 / 200 = 20. PC and not: 80 × 150 / 200 = 60. Each gap is 6 in size (36 − 30 = 6, 84 − 90 = −6, 14 − 20 = −6, 66 − 60 = 6), so each squared gap is 36. χ2 = 36/30 + 36/90 + 36/20 + 36/60 = 1.2 + 0.4 + 1.8 + 0.6 = 4.0. The degrees of freedom are (2 − 1) × (2 − 1) = 1. Since 4.0 > 2.71, there is evidence of a link between device and buying at α = 0.1: phone viewers bought more often (36 of 120, 30%) than PC viewers (14 of 80, 17.5%). This is evidence of an association, not proof that the device causes buying; for example, the people who watch on phones may simply be different customers.

P1.4 A fictional brand ranks five creators by audience fit (1 = best) and, separately, by last month's sales (1 = highest). Creator A: fit 1, sales 2. B: fit 2, sales 1. C: fit 3, sales 3. D: fit 4, sales 5. E: fit 5, sales 4. Compute Spearman's rs = 1 − 6Σdi2 / (n(n2 − 1)) and interpret it.

Show the answer

The rank gaps d = fit rank − sales rank are A: −1, B: 1, C: 0, D: −1, E: 1. The squares are 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, so Σd2 = 4. With n = 5, n(n2 − 1) = 5 × 24 = 120. rs = 1 − 6 × 4 / 120 = 1 − 24/120 = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8. This is close to +1, so the two rankings move together strongly: creators with better audience fit tended to rank higher in sales. With only five creators, treat this as a pattern worth checking, not a law, and remember that a rank correlation says nothing about cause.

P1.5 A fictional MCN pitches ten creators to a snack brand and says "our creators average ¥50,000 GMV per month". You learn that nine creators each made ¥20,000 and one made ¥320,000. In three to five sentences, check the claim, say which summary the brand should use, and name one data-quality question to ask before signing.

Show the answer

The claim is arithmetically true: total GMV is 9 × 20,000 + 320,000 = 500,000, and 500,000 / 10 = ¥50,000. But the median is ¥20,000, because nine of the ten creators made exactly that. Week 2 Fundamentals II: marketing frameworks and data preparation

Porter's five forces, platform edition

On the exam

Week 1 showed you that new media leaves data on every edge. This week answers two questions that come before any analysis. The first is a business question: which customers should this firm fight for, against whom, and with what kind of advantage? The marketing frameworks (five forces, cost leadership and differentiation, segmentation, targeting and positioning) are the vocabulary for that decision; analytics answers questions, and marketing picks them. The second is a data question: before you count anything, can you trust the file? Every method from Week 3 to Week 8 assumes a table that has already been cleaned and reshaped, so this week is the floor the rest of the course stands on.

The five forces model, from Porter (1980), asks a simple question: who else wants your customer's money, and who can squeeze your profit? It looks at an industry, not at one firm. In the middle sits industry rivalry, the fight among firms that already sell the same thing. Four other forces push into that middle from outside. When the forces are strong, profit leaks away from every firm in the industry. When they are weak, firms keep more of what they earn. You use the model to judge how attractive a market is and to see where the pressure on your margin comes from. On platforms each force has a clear reading.

Porter's five forces, platform edition

  • Industry rivalry: coupon wars in one category feed
    • New entrants: a channel opens in a day
    • Substitutes: short video, stream and search ads split one budget
    • Supplier power: top hosts name their price
    • Buyer power: one swipe to the next shop

New entrants arrive easily: anyone can open a shop or a stream account almost at once, so the barrier to entry is very low and new rivals keep taking a share of the profit. A substitute is a different product that does the same job; a brand has one marketing budget, and short video, livestreams and search ads all compete for that same money. Supplier power shows up when the key input is scarce: top hosts can set their own fee, and the brand's margin shrinks. Buyer power is high when switching costs almost nothing; a buyer who can compare rivals in one swipe has strong power over price. Industry rivalry is the coupon war inside one category feed: rivals sit side by side and fight on price, which pushes margins down for everyone. On platforms almost every force is stronger than in a classic shop, which is why the next section asks how a firm can still earn above-average profit.

Exam tip

In a multiple-choice question, match the story to the actor. If the pressure comes from the people who sell you an input (hosts, factories), it is supplier power. If it comes from the people who buy from you and can leave easily, it is buyer power. If it is a different kind of product doing the same job, it is a substitute, not a rival.

Segmentation: from mass market to a segment of one

On the exam

The next block is STP: segmentation, targeting and positioning. The one-line meaning is "who you serve, which word you own". It runs in that order. You first cut the market, then choose which pieces to serve, then decide how you want those buyers to see you.

Segmentation is cutting a market into sub-markets whose needs differ, where each sub-market is a group of buyers who want much the same thing. The two halves of the definition matter. Between segments, needs must differ, or there is no reason to treat them differently. Inside a segment, buyers must be similar, or one offer cannot serve them all.

From mass market to a segment of one

  1. Massone offer for everyone (a classic soda for everyone)
  2. Segmenta few large groups (skincare for teens versus for mothers)
  3. Nichenarrow, defined, served deep (sunscreen only for sensitive skin)
  4. Customone buyer, one offer (print-on-demand merch; one buyer prints one shirt)

As you move down the ladder, the group gets smaller and the fit to each buyer gets closer. Data and platforms make small groups cheap to find and serve.

Disruption enters from below

On the exam

Christensen (1997) tells disruption in three steps. A segment the leaders ignore is not always a small one forever. It can be the door through which a future rival enters.

Disruption enters from below

  1. Overshootincumbents overshoot what most buyers need
  2. Low endnew entrants serve the low end cheaply
  3. Climbthen they climb

First, incumbents (the established firms) keep improving their products until they offer more than most buyers need, and they charge for it. Second, new entrants serve the low end of the market cheaply, with a simpler product that is good enough for those buyers; the incumbents tend to leave this end alone, because it looks unattractive to them. Third, the entrants improve step by step and move up into the incumbents' own segments. Factory-direct livestreams undercut mid-market beauty brands and then climb. That case connects to two ideas you have already seen: the factory-direct stream is a cost leader, and the low end is a segment the mid-market firms overlooked.

Three targeting strategies

On the exam

Once the market is cut, targeting decides which segments to serve and how. There are three ways.

Undifferentiated targeting ignores the differences between segments. It is the cheapest way to serve a market, but it only works when buyers really do want the same thing. Concentrated targeting is the natural choice for a small firm, and it is the targeting version of the focus strategy you just read about.

The difference between first place and excavate is a common question. First place is about timing: you are first into a slot. In share you accept the leader's slot and live next to it; in reposition you attack what the leader's slot means. For any brand you follow, you should be able to name which of the five it is running right now.

Variable types and three traps

On the exam

Ask "what kind of number is it?" The question matters because the kind of variable decides which summaries are valid. A number stored in a computer is not always a quantity.

Variables: what kind of number is it?

Continuous vs discrete

means make sense?

Nominal vs ordinal

Identifiers (IDs)

a name stored as a number

Small-base ratios

Bots

Among numeric variables, continuous variables can take any value on a scale, like GMV in yuan or average watch time in minutes. Discrete variables are counts, like units sold, likes and comments. The test is "means make sense?" For both continuous and discrete variables an average is meaningful: a mean of about 2,502 comments per session is a fair summary, even though no single session can have a fraction of a comment. Among categorical variables, nominal variables are labels with no order, like a session ID or a product name. Ordinal variables are labels with an order, like ranks or a 1-to-5 rating.

Satisfaction scored 1 to 5 is ordinal: the levels are ordered, but the step from 4 to 5 is not a fixed amount of satisfaction. So "average satisfaction rose from 4.0 to 4.5" is a weaker claim than "average order value rose from 100 to 150 yuan", where every yuan is the same size. For a rating, the median or the share of 4s and 5s is safer.

Identifiers (IDs): a session or product code may be stored as a number, but it is a name. If sessions S01 to S12 were stored as 1 to 12, their average (6.5) is easy to compute and means nothing. Any report that shows an average ID has a bug.

Small-base ratios: a rate computed on a small denominator can look impressive and carry almost no information. One viewer and one order is a 100% conversion. A milder version appears in the Lumen session data. Conversion is units sold divided by peak viewers. Session S03 had 844 peak viewers and sold 139 units, a conversion of 139 / 844 = 16.5%. Session S01 had 1,419 peak viewers and sold 218 units, a conversion of 218 / 1,419 = 15.4%. S03 wins on the rate but sold fewer units. Always report the denominator beside the rate.

Bots: automated or paid accounts inflate views, likes and comments. These counts often sit in the denominator or numerator of engagement rates, so bots distort the rates, and they do not announce themselves.

From export to a dataset you trust: the cleaning order

On the exam

A platform export is not evidence yet. Five steps run in a fixed order. When a fresh export lands on your desk, start at the top of this list: tidy the shape first, then remove duplicates, because every later check depends on a clean row count.

From export to a dataset you trust

  1. Tidyone row per observation, one column per variable
  2. Duplicatesdedupe before judging missingness
  3. Missing valuesdrop the row or flag it, never silently fill
  4. Units and codingfix units before judging outliers
  5. Outlierskeep it or flag it, never silently delete

Tidy data means one row per observation and one column per variable. An observation is one thing you measured, such as one stream, or one product in one week. If a variable such as "week" is hidden in the column names (gmv_w1, gmv_w2 and so on), the table is not tidy, because you cannot group or filter by week.

Duplicates before missing values. A duplicated row copies everything in it, including its gaps. If you count missing values first, the same gap is counted twice and the problem looks bigger than it is. So remove duplicates, then count what is missing.

Missing values: drop or flag, never silently fill. You may drop the row from an analysis that needs the value, or keep it and flag it. What you must not do is quietly put in a plausible number, such as the average of the other rows. That creates data nobody can tell apart from real data. Filling a gap with the mean leaves the mean unchanged, so the headline number gives no sign that anything was invented.

Units and coding before outliers. A value recorded in the wrong unit looks exactly like an extreme value. If you judge outliers first, you may delete a correct observation that was only mislabelled. Fix units first, then see what is still extreme.

Outliers: keep or flag, never silently delete. An outlier is an extreme but real value. Errors get fixed; real extremes stay in the data and get a flag in the report. Deleting a real event to make a chart look neat hides the most interesting thing that happened.

Behind every step sits one habit: log every fix, so that any number can be traced back to the export it came from.

Trap

The two order rules are the ones examiners test: dedupe before judging missingness, and fix units before judging outliers. If a question asks why the order matters, give the reason for each: duplicates double-count gaps, and wrong units look like outliers.

Worked example: cleaning Lumen's session export

Step 1, duplicates. The duplicate check shows session S07 twice, with every field identical: a re-export artifact where the platform wrote the same row twice. Dropping it takes the table from 13 rows to 12, which now matches the 12 streams.

Step 2, missing values. After deduplication, one cell in avg_watch_min is missing; it belongs to session S05. The fix neither fills it nor deletes S05 everywhere. Step 3, units. Session S09 shows an average watch time of 252 for a stream of 150 minutes. An average viewer cannot watch longer than the stream lasted, so 252 cannot be minutes. Week 3 Association and rules

From co-occurrence to baskets

Background

Every shop and every platform keeps the same question in mind: when someone takes this item, what else will they take? A bakery wants to know which tools to place next to which flour. A live channel wants to know which category to show after Gaming. This week answers that question from records of what people actually bought or watched together.

Start by setting co-occurrence against correlation. Correlation needs two variables on a scale, such as watch time and spend, and gives you r between −1 and 1. Co-occurrence needs no scale at all. It only asks which items land in the same basket, and you get the answer by counting transactions. The standard definition of association analysis is: given a set of transactions, find rules that predict the occurrence of an item from the occurrences of other items in the same transaction. The left side of a rule is called the antecedent and the right side the consequent, as in {bread flour} → {instant yeast}.

A basket is the set of distinct items in one order or one session. "Distinct" matters. If a customer buys three print packs and two frames, the basket simply contains "print pack" and "frame". In a basket table you write 1 for present and 0 for absent, never the quantity, so that order is the row 1, 1, 0 if the third column is stickers. Support counts orders, not units.

Trap

Do not add up a quantity column when you compute support. An order with five frames is still one basket that contains "frame". This was tested on the mock exam.

There are four families of rules. Rules by value are about specific items (game streamer 1 → game streamer 2). Rules by category are about whole categories (Gaming → Film). Rules by attribute value are about properties of a session (watched: yes → liked: yes). Rules by time link periods (year one: Gaming → year two: Lifestyle). A rule by time only works if your data keeps each person's items separate for each period. If you merge January and February into one list per viewer, you can no longer ask whether January travel viewing goes with February language viewing. There are also four business uses: placement (put associated items side by side), advertising (target by what was just consumed), bundling (sell the basket as one product, like flight plus hotel plus car) and design (lay out the shelf, the site or the stream). Keep one warning in mind for the whole week: association is not causation.

Itemsets and support

On the exam

An itemset is any set of items you want to count across baskets, for example {Comedy, Gaming, Music}. Three ideas sit side by side here, and each one uses the one before it.

Itemsets and support: three ideas

σ · support count

transactions that contain the itemset

s · support

σ over all N transactions

s ≥ minsup · frequent itemset

minsup is the threshold you set

The first is the support count, written σ (the Greek letter sigma). It is the number of transactions that contain the whole itemset. "Contain" means the basket has every item of the itemset; it may have other items too. The second is the support, written s. It is the support count divided by the total number of transactions, N. So σ is a count of baskets and s is a share between 0 and 1. The third is the frequent itemset: an itemset whose support is at least minsup, the minimum support. Minsup is a threshold you set yourself. The data does not tell you what it should be.

s(I) = σ(I) / N     and     I is frequent if s(I) ≥ minsup

σ(I) is how many baskets hold every item of I. N is how many baskets there are in total. Support is therefore "what share of all baskets show this whole pattern".

Worked example: the support of {Comedy, Gaming, Music}

Go down the table row by row and ask: does this viewer have all three dots? Viewer 1 has Gaming and Comedy but no Music, so no. Viewer 2 has Gaming and Music but no Comedy, so no. Viewer 3 has Comedy and Music but no Gaming, so no. Viewer 4 has all three, yes. Viewer 5 has all three, yes. So σ({Comedy, Gaming, Music}) = 2.

There are N = 5 viewers, so s = 2/5 = .40. In words: two of the five viewers watched all three categories. If you had set minsup at .40 or lower, this itemset would be frequent. At minsup .60 it would not.

Support has no direction. It describes a set, and a set has no left or right side. {Comedy, Gaming, Music} and {Music, Gaming, Comedy} are the same set with the same support. Direction only arrives with rules, in the next section.

A rule and its two strength numbers

On the exam

An association rule is written X → Y, with X and Y disjoint itemsets, which means they share no item. The rule claims that Y tends to appear when X appears. You cannot write {Comedy, Music} → {Music}, because Music would sit on both sides and the rule would say nothing.

A rule gets two strength numbers. Both use the same top number: the count of baskets that contain everything in the rule, σ(X ∪ Y). The symbol ∪ means "union", so X ∪ Y is all the items of X and Y together. The two numbers differ only in what they divide by.

A rule and its two strength numbers: {Comedy, Music} → {Film}

Support

s = σ(X ∪ Y) / N = 2/5 = .40

two of five viewers watch all three

Confidence

c = σ(X ∪ Y) / σ(X) = 2/3 ≈ .67

two of the three Comedy-Music viewers also watch Film

Support divides by all baskets, so it asks how common the whole pattern is. Confidence divides only by the baskets that contain X, so it asks: among the cases where the rule applies, how often is it right? Support tells you whether the rule is worth attention. Confidence tells you whether the arrow can be trusted.

Worked example: {Comedy, Music} → {Film}

Step 1, find the baskets that contain X = {Comedy, Music}. Viewer 3 has both, viewer 4 has both, viewer 5 has both. Viewers 1 and 2 do not. So σ(X) = 3.

Step 2, among those three, find the ones that also contain Film. Viewer 3 has Film, viewer 4 has Film, viewer 5 does not. So σ(X ∪ Y) = σ({Comedy, Music, Film}) = 2.

Step 3, support = 2/5 = .40: two of five viewers watch all three. Confidence = 2/3 ≈ .67: two of the three Comedy-Music viewers also watch Film.

Reading the two numbers

Getting the number is half the job. The other half is saying in words what it means, and the words follow the denominator.

Reading the two numbers: {Gaming} → {Comedy}

Here is where the numbers come from. Gaming viewers are 1, 2, 4 and 5, so σ({Gaming}) = 4. Of these, viewers 1, 4 and 5 also watch Comedy; viewer 2 does not. So σ({Gaming, Comedy}) = 3. Support = 3/5 = .60, and it talks about all viewers. Confidence = 3/4 = .75, and it talks about Gaming viewers only.

Because confidence divides by σ(X), it changes when you flip the arrow. For {Comedy} → {Gaming} the top number is still 3, but Comedy viewers are 1, 3, 4 and 5, so the bottom is also 4 and confidence happens to be .75 again. That is a coincidence of this small table, not a rule. In general the two directions give different confidences, while support stays the same in both directions. In the bakery data, for example, bread flour → bread improver has confidence .470, but bread improver → bread flour has .689.

Exam tip

When a question gives you counts, write down three numbers before anything else: N, σ(X) and σ(X ∪ Y). Support and confidence each take one division from there. If a question asks you to "read" a number, say what the denominator is: "of all viewers" for support, "of the viewers who already watch X" for confidence.

Worked example: one platform, 5,000 viewers

A platform has N = 5,000 viewers. 3,000 watch Gaming, 3,750 watch Comedy, and 2,000 watch both. Score the rule {Gaming} → {Comedy}.

Support = 2,000 / 5,000 = .40. That is a big share of the platform. Confidence = 2,000 / 3,000 = .667. Two out of three Gaming viewers watch Comedy. With both numbers looking healthy, you might pin a Comedy recommendation under every Gaming stream.

Now the catch. How many viewers watch Comedy anyway, whatever else they watch? 3,750 / 5,000 = .75. This is the base rate, P(Comedy) = .75. A random viewer watches Comedy with probability .75. A Gaming viewer watches Comedy with probability .667. So knowing that someone watches Gaming makes Comedy less likely, not more. The rule's confidence sits below the base rate.

Now score the opposite rule, {Gaming} → {not Comedy}. Gaming viewers who do not watch Comedy number 3,000 − 2,000 = 1,000. Support = 1,000 / 5,000 = .20. Confidence = 1,000 / 3,000 = .333. This rule fits the data better, even though its numbers are smaller. Why? Only 1,250 of 5,000 viewers (.25) skip Comedy in general, and among Gaming viewers the share is .333, which is higher than .25. As my own check: .333 / .25 ≈ 1.33, a value above 1 in the lift measure of the next section.

Trap

A rule with high confidence can still be worse than guessing the base rate. Confidence has no idea how common the right-hand side is. Any rule that points at a very popular item will look confident, simply because that item is in most baskets. Before you believe a confident rule, ask: what share of all baskets contain Y anyway? If confidence is not above that share, X adds nothing.

Lift: the number that compares against chance

On the exam

The base rate trap shows what is missing: a comparison against chance. Lift supplies it. It divides the confidence of the rule by the base rate of the right-hand side.

lift(X → Y) = P(Y | X) / P(Y) = c(X → Y) / s(Y)

P(Y | X) is the probability of Y given X, which is exactly the confidence. P(Y) is the probability of Y in any basket, which is the support of Y alone, the base rate. Lift asks: how many times more likely is Y when X is present, compared with a random basket?

Worked example: lift of {Gaming} → {Comedy}

Confidence = .667 and s(Comedy) = .75, so lift = .667 / .75 = .89. Read it as: below 1, Gaming viewers watch Comedy less than a random viewer does.

You can get the same answer straight from the counts, which is handy when an exam gives counts rather than shares: lift = (σ(X ∪ Y) × N) / (σ(X) × σ(Y)) = (2,000 × 5,000) / (3,000 × 3,750) = 10,000,000 / 11,250,000 ≈ .89.

Reading lift: three values

< 1

they substitute

= 1

independent

> 1

they go together

A lift below 1 means the items substitute: having X makes Y less likely than usual, so people tend to choose one or the other. A lift equal to 1 means the items are independent: X tells you nothing about Y. A lift above 1 means they go together more often than chance would predict. A lift below 1 is still a finding. If two teas in a tea shop have lift .55, do not bundle them, but you can show them side by side as alternatives.

One more property is useful. If you write lift out as P(X and Y) / (P(X) × P(Y)), you can see that swapping X and Y gives the same value. Lift is symmetric. It scores how strongly two sides go together, not which way the arrow points. Confidence is the number that differs between the two directions, so you rank by lift to find the association and then read confidence to decide which way to point a recommendation.

Exam tip

If the paper gives you lift and confidence, you can recover the base rate: s(Y) = c / lift. If it gives you confidence and the count of Y, you can compute lift. Practise moving between the three numbers in every direction.

A typical starting point is minsup .5 and minconf .5, which you then move according to the decision. The simplest way to find every rule that passes is brute force: list every possible rule, score each one, and keep the ones that pass. The obvious next question is: how many rules is that?

Counting the rules, exactly

On the exam

For a catalogue of d items, the number of possible rules R can be counted exactly, in three steps that each simplify the one before.

Counting the rules, exactly: three steps

  1. Step 1: R = Σk C(d, k) · Σj C(d − k, j)pick the antecedent (k of d items), then the consequent (j of the rest)
  2. Step 2: R = Σk C(d, k) · (2d−k − 1)the consequent is any non-empty subset of the remaining items
  3. Step 3: R = 3d − 2d+1 + 1the binomial theorem collapses the sum

Check: d = 6 gives 729 − 128 + 1 = 602 rules.

Step 1. First pick the antecedent: choose k items out of d, which can be done in C(d, k) ways ("d choose k"). Then pick the consequent: choose j items out of the d − k that are left, in C(d − k, j) ways. The consequent must come from the leftover items because the two sides are disjoint. Add over every possible k and j, with neither side empty.

Step 2. The inner sum counts every non-empty subset of the d − k remaining items. A set of size d − k has 2d−k subsets in total, and one of them is empty, so there are 2d−k − 1 choices for the consequent. Here k runs from 1 to d − 1, since the antecedent cannot be empty and cannot use up every item.

Step 3. The binomial theorem collapses the sum. Split it into Σ C(d, k) 2d−k minus Σ C(d, k). The full sum of C(d, k) 2d−k over k = 0 to d is (1 + 2)d = 3d; drop the end terms k = 0 (which is 2d) and k = d (which is 1) to get 3d − 2d − 1. The full sum of C(d, k) is 2d; drop the same two end terms, each equal to 1, to get 2d − 2. Subtract: (3d − 2d − 1) − (2d − 2) = 3d − 2d+1 + 1.

The check: d = 6 gives 36 − 27 + 1 = 729 − 128 + 1 = 602 rules. Six live categories already produce 602 possible rules.

If the binomial step feels heavy, here is my own way to see the same formula. For each item, a rule puts it in one of three places: on the left, on the right, or not in the rule at all. That gives 3d ways. Some of these leave the left side empty: then each item has only two places (right or out), so 2d ways. The same number leave the right side empty. Subtract both, 3d − 2 × 2d. But the case where both sides are empty was subtracted twice, so add 1 back. The result is 3d − 2d+1 + 1. You can also check the smallest case by hand. With d = 3 items A, B, C, a one-item antecedent (3 choices) can take any non-empty subset of the other two (3 choices), giving 9 rules, and a two-item antecedent (3 choices) can only take the last item, giving 3 rules. Total 12, which matches the formula.

The two steps of the search

On the exam

The way out of the explosion is to split the search into two steps, done in order.

The search in two steps

  1. Step 1 of the search: frequent itemset generationfind every itemset clearing minsup. The cost explodes with d.
  2. Step 2 of the search: rule generationsplit each frequent itemset into strong rules that clear minconf. Cheap once step 1 is done.

Next: Apriori prunes step one.

Step 1 is frequent itemset generation: find every itemset that clears minsup. Its cost explodes with d, because there are 2d possible itemsets. Step 2 is rule generation: split each frequent itemset into rules and keep the strong ones that clear minconf. This is cheap once step 1 is done. The reason is that every rule built from one itemset has the same support, the support of that itemset. If the itemset is frequent, all its rules already pass minsup, and only confidence is left to check. Confidence uses counts you already have from step 1, so no new pass over the data is needed. Because step 1 carries the cost, that is where an algorithm must save work, and the Apriori algorithm in the next section does exactly that: it prunes step one.

The Apriori principle and candidate pruning

Background

Brute force on itemsets scores all 2d nodes of the itemset lattice, the diagram of every possible itemset from the empty set down to the full set. For six categories that is 26 = 64 nodes. The Apriori principle (Agrawal and Srikant, 1994) lets you skip most of them. It says: if an itemset is frequent, every one of its subsets is frequent too. Turned around: if an itemset is infrequent, every superset of it is infrequent, so you can prune them all and never count them.

The reason is simple. Adding an item to an itemset can never add baskets. A basket that holds Gaming, Comedy and Music also holds Gaming and Comedy, so the count of the bigger set can only be the same or smaller. In the five-viewer table, count({Gaming}) = 4, count({Gaming, Comedy}) = 3 and count({Gaming, Comedy, Music}) = 2. This property is called anti-monotone support: a set never has higher support than any of its subsets. The bound is not always strict. {Film} and {Film, Music} both count 3, because every Film viewer also watches Music.

Apriori uses this level by level, in a loop called generate and test. Count the single items and keep the frequent ones. Build candidate pairs only from frequent singles. Then, before counting any candidate of size k, check that every subset of size k − 1 is frequent; if even one is not, discard the candidate without counting. Count the survivors, keep the frequent ones, and move up one level. Stop when a level produces nothing frequent. A threshold given as a share must first become a count: minimum count = minimum support × number of baskets.

Worked example: Apriori on the five viewers at minimum support 60%

Minimum count = 0.60 × 5 = 3 baskets. Level 1 counts: Gaming 4, Comedy 4, Music 4, Film 3, Food 2, Lifestyle 1. Food and Lifestyle fail, so no set containing either is ever built.

Level 2 builds pairs from the four survivors only: C(4, 2) = 6 candidates instead of C(6, 2) = 15. The counts are {Gaming, Comedy} 3, {Gaming, Film} 2, {Gaming, Music} 3, {Comedy, Film} 2, {Comedy, Music} 3, {Film, Music} 3. Four pairs are frequent: {Gaming, Comedy}, {Gaming, Music}, {Comedy, Music} and {Film, Music}.

Level 3 builds triples from the frequent pairs and prunes. {Gaming, Music, Film} contains the pair {Gaming, Film}, which counted only 2, so it is discarded without counting. {Comedy, Music, Film} contains {Comedy, Film}, also 2, so it is discarded too. {Gaming, Comedy, Music} has all three of its pairs frequent, so it survives pruning. Now it must be counted: viewers 4 and 5 only, a count of 2, below 3. It is not frequent, and the search stops.

Total scored: 6 singles + 6 pairs + 1 triple = 13 candidates, against 64 for brute force.

Trap

Pruning only tells you which candidates cannot be frequent. It never proves that a survivor is frequent. Frequent subsets are a necessary condition, not a guarantee. {Gaming, Comedy, Music} had three frequent pairs and still failed when it was counted. If a colleague says "all its pairs are frequent, so the triple is a confirmed bundle", the answer is: it is only a candidate, and you must count it against the data before you know.

A typical case question works the same way. With 100 baskets and minimum support 20%, the minimum count is 20. Any triple that contains a pair below 20 is discarded, and you name the failing pair as the reason. The one triple whose pairs all clear 20 is the only one left to count, and it is still unconfirmed until you count it.

Two further ideas are worth one sentence each. A frequent itemset of k items can be split into at most 2k − 2 rules (both sides non-empty), so a 3-itemset gives 6 rules and a 4-itemset gives 14. And confidence has its own pruning: for rules from the same itemset, moving items from the left side to the right side can only lower confidence, because the top number stays the same and the bottom number can only grow. So c(ABC → D) ≥ c(AB → CD) ≥ c(A → BCD), and if the first fails minconf, the others fail too. The cost of Apriori is one pass over the data per level, kmax + 1 passes in all, where kmax is the size of the longest frequent itemset. FP-growth is a faster algorithm that compresses the transactions into a tree, but it finds the same frequent itemsets and the same rules; it is an engineering optimization, not a new idea about rules.

The short version

A basket records which items are present, not how many. Support is the share of all baskets that contain the whole pattern, and an itemset is frequent when its support reaches the minsup you chose. A rule X → Y has disjoint sides; its support divides the count of X and Y together by all baskets, and its confidence divides the same count by the baskets that contain X. You need both thresholds, minsup and minconf, and both come from the decision: high support with high confidence is worth acting on, while perfect confidence on one rare viewer is seductive but thin. Even then, confidence can fool you when Y is common anyway, as in the 5,000-viewer case where .667 sits below the base rate of .75. Lift fixes this by dividing confidence by the support of Y: below 1 the items substitute, at 1 they are independent, above 1 they go together. Sort by lift: R1 is killed at lift .89, R2 ignored at 1.02, and matcha → bamboo whisk kept at 7.80, while doilies at support .280 are a decoy with every lift between 0.850 and 1.073. Brute force must score R = 3d − 2d+1 + 1 rules (602 for six items), so the search is split into frequent itemset generation, which is the expensive step, and rule generation, which is cheap. Apriori cuts the first step: an infrequent set condemns every superset, but a candidate that survives pruning must still be counted.

Show the answer

With d = 7, R = 37 − 28 + 1 = 2,187 − 256 + 1 = 1,932 rules. With d = 8, R = 38 − 29 + 1 = 6,561 − 512 + 1 = 6,050 rules, more than three times as many from one extra product. The expensive step is frequent itemset generation, step 1, because the number of possible itemsets is 2d and doubles with every product; rule generation, step 2, is cheap once the frequent itemsets and their counts are known.

P3.5 (Case) A fictional craft store, Loomly, has 200 baskets and four products: yarn (P), knitting needles (Q), pattern books (R) and stitch markers (S). Minimum support is 15%. All four single items are frequent. Pair counts are: PQ 45, PR 38, PS 22, QR 41, QS 33, RS 29. Yarn appears in 90 baskets and needles in 80. (a) Convert the minimum support to a count and apply the Apriori principle to the triples PQR, PQS, PRS and QRS. (b) A colleague says PQR is "a confirmed bundle". Respond. (c) Compute support, confidence and lift of {yarn} → {needles} and say what the lift means.

Show the answer

(a) Minimum count = 0.15 × 200 = 30 baskets. Frequent pairs are PQ (45), PR (38), QR (41) and QS (33). PS (22) and RS (29) are below 30. Discard PQS because PS is infrequent. Discard PRS because PS and RS are infrequent. Discard QRS because RS is infrequent. A larger itemset can never appear in more baskets than any of its subsets, so these three triples cannot reach 30 and need no counting. Only PQR remains, because PQ, PR and QR all clear 30.

(b) PQR is only a candidate. Frequent pairs are a necessary condition for a frequent triple, not a guarantee: the three pairs could each appear in 30 or more baskets without all three items appearing together that often. The store must count PQR against the 200 baskets. If, for example, it appears in 26 baskets, it fails the minimum of 30 and is not frequent.

(c) Support = 45 / 200 = .225. Confidence = 45 / 90 = .50. The base rate of needles is 80 / 200 = .40, so lift = .50 / .40 = 1.25. Yarn buyers take needles 1.25 times as often as a random customer: the two go together, a little more than chance would predict. The association is moderate, so a placement test is reasonable, but it is far weaker than a pair like matcha and bamboo whisk at 7.80.

Week 4 Classification

Labels, features and the hidden test set

Background

A brand that runs a campaign on a short-video platform gets thousands of comments in a week. Some are customers asking real questions. Some are spam: scam links, fake praise, copy-paste adverts. A small social team cannot read them all, so it wants a machine to decide which comments stay up and which come down. That is classification: a model learns from past cases whose answer is known, then gives every new case a score, and a person turns that score into a decision. This week is less about how the models work and more about how they fail. Every filter makes two kinds of mistake, the two mistakes hurt different people, and one dial, the threshold, moves cost from one group to the other. In Week 3 you counted what appears together with no answer column at all. Here, for the first time, there is an answer column to learn from. In Week 5 the answer becomes a number instead of a category, and the same warning returns: one summary number can flatter.

A label is the answer on a past case. Training a model on examples whose answers are already known is called supervised learning. When the label is a category, such as spam or not spam, the task is classification; when it is a number, it is regression (Week 5). In the TeaPop data the label is is_spam: 1 if the platform removed the comment as spam, 0 if it stayed up. Each comment also has 15 features, the counted signals the model may see: the account (age, followers, verified or not), the text as numbers (length, links, emoji, digits, contact and price keywords) and behaviour (posting hour, comments in the last hour, repeated identical texts). The model never reads the words, only these counts.

The step that matters most for the numbers that follow is the train and test split. The model learns from 3,000 comments with the answers visible and is judged on the other 1,000, whose answers stay hidden during training. Grading a model on the comments it learned from grades its memory, not its skill, just as a student who saw the paper in advance proves nothing by scoring well. The split is stratified: both parts carry the same 31.2 percent spam share, so the hidden 1,000 holds 312 spam and 688 genuine comments. Every verdict number in this chapter comes from that hidden 1,000. One last warning: prediction is not causation. The filter uses patterns that went with spam in the past; it cannot say why spam arrives or whether a stricter filter would deter it.

The score is a probability; the verdict is a cutoff

On the exam

This idea holds the whole week together. A classification model does not say "spam" or "not spam". It gives each comment a spam probability, a number between 0 and 1. Sorted by score, the comments form a ranking from safest to spammiest.

The score and the verdict

The score

each comment gets a spam probability; one comment 0.97, another 0.02; probabilities rank comments from safest to spammiest

The verdict

a verdict appears only when someone chooses a threshold; score 0.5 or higher: delete; below: keep; keep the probability; the verdict can be recut later

A verdict (keep or delete) appears only when someone chooses a threshold, a cutoff on the score. The usual starting example is 0.5: score 0.5 or higher, delete; below, keep. But 0.5 is a choice, not a law; at 0.9 or 0.1 the same model gives different verdicts. That is why you keep the probability: stored scores let you recut the verdicts later, when the business situation changes, without retraining anything. The rule is "The model proposes; the threshold disposes." The model proposes a probability; the person who chooses the threshold decides what happens, and owns that business decision.

verdict = delete if score ≥ t; keep if score < t

The score is the model's spam probability for one comment. The threshold t is chosen by a person. A high t means the filter deletes only comments it is very sure about. A low t means it deletes anything that looks even a little suspicious.

Worked example: one comment, two verdicts

Take one real comment scored with a small three-signal model. The comment posted identical texts twice, posted 8 times in the last hour, and had no links. The model gives it a spam probability of 0.594. At threshold 0.5, 0.594 is above the cutoff, so the comment is deleted. At threshold 0.9, 0.594 is below the cutoff, so the comment is kept. Same comment, same model; only the dial moved. The model did not change its mind. The person who set the threshold did.

Two ways to be wrong: the four cells

On the exam

Once a verdict exists, you can compare it with the truth. Two truths (actually genuine, actually spam) times two verdicts (keep, delete) give four cells. Truth sits in the rows and the filter's action in the columns. Spam is the "positive" class, because spam is what the filter looks for; "positive" means "flagged as spam", not "good".

Confusion matrix: two ways to be wrong

Read each name in two parts. The second word is what the filter said: positive means it called the comment spam and deleted it, negative means it kept it. The first word says whether that was right. So a false positive is a real customer the filter wrongly deleted, and a false negative is spam the filter wrongly kept, still visible.

The two wrong cells are where the cost lives, and they land on different people. A false positive is paid by the commenter, whose genuine question vanished, and by the brand, which now looks like it silences customers. A false negative is paid by the audience, who still see scam links, and by the brand, whose comment section looks rotten. Hence "two errors, one dial": two kinds of mistake, and a threshold that trades one for the other.

The accuracy trap

On the exam

Accuracy is the share of all comments where the filter was right. It adds the two correct cells and divides by everything.

accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + FN + TN)

The top counts every correct verdict: spam that was deleted plus genuine that was kept. The bottom is all the comments that were judged.

The accuracy trap: two filters

Filter A: delete nothing

right about every genuine comment: 688 of 1,000; accuracy 0.688; spam caught: zero

Filter B: this week's tree

right about 977 of 1,000; accuracy 0.977; spam caught: 298 of 312

The trap is that 0.688 sounds respectable: almost seven in ten, for doing nothing. The data is lopsided (genuine comments far outnumber spam), so a useless model that always says "genuine" scores well for free. This number is the majority baseline, the accuracy of always predicting the larger class. It is the floor every model must beat, not a target. And neither 0.977 nor 0.688 says which mistakes were made. The rule: "Never ask how accurate. Ask which mistakes, at what price."

Trap

Accuracy flatters on lopsided data. When most cases belong to one class, a model that ignores the rare class still scores high. Always compare accuracy with the majority baseline, and always report the two errors separately. If a question gives you a stream where 95 percent of cases are one class, the do-nothing model already scores 0.95, so a model at 0.96 has done very little.

Precision and recall: who feels each

On the exam

Precision and recall look at the two errors one at a time. Both put true positives on top; they differ in what they divide by.

Precision: how clean is the delete pile

precision = spam caught / all deleted = TP / (TP + FP)

The bottom is everything the filter deleted: the spam it caught plus the genuine comments it deleted by mistake. Precision asks: of the comments I removed, what share deserved it? The only error in this formula is FP, the false alarm.

For the tree: 298 / (298 + 9) = 298 / 307 = 0.971. Of every 100 deleted comments, about 97 were truly spam.

Who feels precision

The user

whose genuine comment vanished

The teammate

who re-checks the delete pile by hand

Recall: how much spam got caught

recall = spam caught / all spam = TP / (TP + FN)

The bottom is all the spam that actually arrived: the spam caught plus the spam that slipped through. Recall asks: of all the spam out there, what share did I remove? The only error in this formula is FN, the miss.

For the tree: 298 / (298 + 14) = 298 / 312 = 0.955. So 14 spam comments stayed visible.

Who feels recall

The audience

that still sees scam links

The brand

whose comment section keeps rotting

To keep them apart: precision divides by what the filter did (the delete column), recall by what the world delivered (the spam row). Precision audits the delete pile; recall audits the stream.

Exam tip

When a question asks "who is hurt if precision falls?", think of the false positive: a genuine voice deleted, so the commenter and the person checking the delete pile. When it asks "who is hurt if recall falls?", think of the false negative: spam left up, so the audience and the brand's comment section.

You cannot maximise both: the dial and F1

On the exam

Precision and recall pull against each other, and the threshold moves them.

One dial, two directions

Stricter: raise the dial

delete less, re-check more; precision climbs; more spam survives: recall falls

Looser: lower the dial

delete more, catch more; recall climbs; more genuine voices lost: precision falls

One dial, two errors moving in opposite directions. The extremes make it plain: a threshold of 1.0 deletes nothing (no false alarms, zero recall), and 0 deletes everything (perfect recall, every customer silenced).

The F1 score

To rank models quickly with one number, the F1 score combines precision and recall using the harmonic mean, an average pulled down hard by whichever of the two is smaller.

F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)

Multiply precision by recall, double it, and divide by their sum. If both are equal, F1 equals that value. If they are far apart, F1 sits close to the smaller one. F1 ignores true negatives completely: it looks only at the spam problem.

Worked example: checking the tree's F1 of 0.963

Precision = 0.971 and recall = 0.955.

Top: 2 × 0.971 × 0.955 = 2 × 0.927305 = 1.85461.

Bottom: 0.971 + 0.955 = 1.926.

F1 = 1.85461 / 1.926 = 0.9629, which rounds to 0.963. You can also do it straight from the counts, with no rounding: F1 = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN) = 596 / (596 + 9 + 14) = 596 / 619 = 0.963.

Why not the ordinary average? Because a filter should not hide a terrible score behind a great one. In my own illustration, a filter with precision 1.00 and recall 0.10 misses nine spam in ten. The ordinary average is 0.55, which sounds half decent; F1 is 2 × 1.00 × 0.10 / 1.10 = 0.18, which tells the truth. One limit remains: F1 ranks models, but it never replaces the pricing question. It treats a missed spam and a lost customer as equally bad, which a real brand rarely does.

Two errors, two prices

On the exam

You cannot choose a threshold until you know what each error costs.

The rule: "Price the two errors separately; then set the dial." Do not pick 0.5 because it is the default, or whatever scores the best accuracy. First ask which error hurts this brand more, now. Prices change with the situation. Compare a luxury launch post with a flash-sale post: which fears a missed scam link more? Reason it out from who reads these comments, what a scam link would do to them, and what a deleted genuine comment would do. A flash sale draws buyers ready to click and pay, an easy target for scam links that cost followers money; a luxury launch lives on image, where both visible spam and a silenced customer can hurt. Good answers name both errors, say who pays each, and then move the dial with a reason.

Two thresholds make a review queue

On the exam

One threshold makes two piles. Two thresholds make three, and the middle pile goes to a human.

The logic is automation where the model is sure, human judgment where it is not. Very low scores are almost certainly genuine, so they publish. The rule: "One threshold makes a filter; two thresholds make a workflow."

Moving the boundaries changes the queue. A wider review band means more staff time and fewer machine errors. A narrower band hands comments back to the machine. Raising the lower boundary sends some comments straight to publish, risking more missed spam; lowering the upper boundary sends some straight to deletion, risking more genuine comments deleted. Without the true labels of the moved comments, you can say a risk may rise, but not by how much.

My own arithmetic: how big would TeaPop's review band be?

The threshold table counts what scores at 0.1 or above and at 0.9 or above, so you can find the middle band for the logistic regression filter on the hidden 1,000.

Score 0.1 or higher: 302 spam + 37 genuine = 339 comments. Score 0.9 or higher: 267 spam + 8 genuine = 275 comments. So the review band (0.1 up to 0.9) holds 339 − 275 = 64 comments: 302 − 267 = 35 spam and 37 − 8 = 29 genuine. The keep band holds 1,000 − 339 = 661 comments, of which 312 − 302 = 10 are spam that still slip through. The delete band holds 275 comments, 8 of them genuine.

Reading: a person reads 64 comments in 1,000 instead of 1,000. If the reviewers judge correctly, the 29 genuine comments in the band are saved and the 35 spam are removed, so compared with the single threshold at 0.9, the queue catches 35 more spam, and compared with the single threshold at 0.1, it saves 29 more customers. The price is human time on 64 comments, plus the 10 misses in the keep band and the 8 false alarms in the delete band, which the queue does not touch.

For TeaPop's first pass, two designs compete. Auto-deleting at 0.9 needs no human time but leaves 312 − 267 = 45 spam up and deletes 8 customers. Sending everything from 0.1 up to a reviewer means reading 339 comments per 1,000, heavy for a team of four, but reviewers could remove up to 302 spam and rescue all 37 genuine comments in that pile. You gain on both errors and pay in staff time. The keep / review / delete design, auto-delete at 0.9 and review only the middle, sits between the two.

Three models: logistic regression, decision trees, random forests

Background

The middle of the week introduces the three models behind the numbers. The case pages compare all three, and the mock exam asks you to read a tree, so you need to know what each does and what it gives up. Place them on one line, with interpretability (can you defend a decision?) at one end and predictive power (does it catch what simple rules miss?) at the other.

Three models on one line

  1. Logistic regressionreadable scorecard; one weight per signal; the benchmark every fancier model must beat
  2. Decision treeflowchart of yes/no questions; you can walk a comment down the path
  3. Random forestmany trees voting; stronger on average, but the flowchart is gone

Logistic regression: the readable scorecard

Logistic regression adds up the signals, each with its own weight, into one score, then squeezes that score through an S-shaped curve into a probability between 0 and 1. Each signal has one readable weight, so you can say what drives the score: here repeated characters push hardest toward spam (+2.57), and being verified pulls toward genuine (−0.66). Its weakness is that it draws only smooth, simple boundaries and misses signals that matter only in combination.

How a decision tree asks questions

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions. Each node tests one feature. The branches are the answers: yes goes one way, no the other. The leaves are the verdicts, keep or delete, each with a probability. To classify a comment, start at the top and follow its answers down to a leaf. This week's tree opens with "identical text posted 3 or more times?" and, on the yes branch, "posted before 21:30 or after?", going six questions deep. Reading a tree is careful following. In my own example, a tree asks "price keyword?" (no: keep; yes: next question), then "fewer than 10 followers?" (yes: delete; no: keep). A comment with a price keyword from a 250-follower account passes the first question, answers no at the second, and is kept. Follow only the path the case takes, and check each boundary exactly.

Pure and mixed splits

The tree grows greedily: at each step it asks the single most informative question and never goes back. An informative question separates spam from genuine. A branch is pure when it holds only one label, and mixed when it still holds both. A question that leaves both branches as mixed as the starting node has separated nothing and is discarded.

Worked example: the root question, measured

On the 3,000 training comments (about 31 percent spam), the root question is "identical text posted 3 or more times?" The no branch holds 2,410 comments, 14.8 percent spam. The yes branch holds 590 comments, 98.1 percent spam. One question produced a nearly pure spam branch and a mostly genuine one. That is what a tree searches for: copy-paste posting is the loudest spam signal in this stream.

The mock exam tests this with a 50/50 node. A split whose two branches are still half spam and half genuine has done nothing. A split that sends all spam one way and all genuine the other makes two pure branches and is the best possible. Equal branch sizes do not matter; the mix inside each branch does.

Depth, overfitting and the forest

A tree that keeps asking questions until every leaf is pure memorises the training file, noise included, and does worse on new comments. This is overfitting: learning history too well and new comments too little. Stopped too early, a tree underfits and misses real patterns. In one depth sweep, test accuracy peaks at depth 5 (0.980), the shipped depth-6 tree scores 0.977, and by depth 12 training accuracy reaches 0.991 while test accuracy sinks to 0.960. Only the hidden test set reveals this. A random forest grows many trees (200 here), each on a random slice of comments with a random subset of signals, so their mistakes differ and the majority vote cancels much of the noise. The price is that the flowchart is gone: a forest answers with votes. On the hidden 1,000 the three models nearly tie (accuracy 0.979, 0.977 and 0.980), which is exactly the case report that follows. The advice: always run the simple model first.

The short version

A classifier gives a probability; a person turns it into a verdict by choosing a threshold, so the model proposes and the threshold disposes. Every verdict lands in one of four cells, and the two wrong cells hurt different people: a false positive deletes a real customer, a false negative leaves spam up for the audience. Build the confusion matrix from the counts (for the tree: TP 298, FP 9, FN 14, TN 679), then remember that precision is TP / (TP + FP), the cleanliness of the delete pile, and recall is TP / (TP + FN), the share of spam caught. Accuracy flatters on lopsided data: deleting nothing scores 0.688 here, so always report both errors. Raising the threshold raises precision and lowers recall, and lowering it does the reverse; F1 (0.963 for the tree) ranks models but never sets the dial. The curve of every threshold plots catch rate against false-alarm rate, with up and left better and the diagonal a coin flip. Two thresholds make a review queue: keep below 0.1, review 0.1 to 0.9, delete 0.9 and above. In the TeaPop case the three filters tie on accuracy, the dial is asymmetric (BALANCED to STRICT saves 1 customer for 33 more spam; BALANCED to SWEEP catches 2 more spam for 28 more customers), and the tie is broken by interpretability, because a brand must be able to explain a deletion in public.

Week 5 Regression

Predicting a number: residuals and the least-squares line

Background

In Week 4 the question was "which one": is this comment spam, yes or no. This week the question becomes "how much": how much will this user spend next month, how large will the next basket be, how many sales will one more unit of advertising bring. Regression answers with a number and gives each driver a coefficient you can read as per-unit economics. You learn to read every number in a regression output table, to judge how good a fit is, and to use a penalty (Ridge or LASSO) when a model has too many drivers.

A shop's ad spend and sales often rise together, and a straight line measures how steeply. To say which line is "better", you use the residual, the miss on one point: residual = actual − fitted. A positive residual means the line underestimated that week, and a negative one means it overestimated. Ordinary least squares (OLS) picks the intercept b0 and slope b1 of y-hat = b0 + b1x that make the total squared miss, Σ(yi − y-hati)2, as small as possible. The misses are squared so that positive and negative misses cannot cancel, and so that one big miss costs more than several small ones. The correlation coefficient r (from −1 to +1) measures how closely two columns follow a straight line, but a strong r is not yet a good model.

A coefficient inherits the units of the data. Its unit is "units of the target per unit of the feature". If you report sales in thousands instead of single units, and you keep ad spend in the same units, every sales number becomes a thousand times smaller, so the slope is divided by a thousand: 2.097 becomes 0.002097 thousand units of sales per unit of ad spend. The shop did not change; only the ruler did. One donut priced at 1.19 in lev and 0.61 in euro makes the same point: the number moves with the unit, the donut does not. So a big coefficient is not automatically an important one. Ask "in which units?" before you compare sizes. The same idea returns when predictors must be standardized before a penalty is applied.

The intercept, −3.604, is the fitted sales when ad spend is zero. Read literally, it says the shop sells minus 3.6 units with no advertising, which cannot happen. The intercept is an anchor, not a prediction. The line is a local approximation: it describes the range of ad spend the shop actually used. The intercept fixes where the line sits so that it passes through that cloud of data in the right place. If zero ad spend lies outside the data, the line's value there has no business meaning.

Exam tip

If a question gives you a negative intercept and asks what it means, the full answer has two parts. Say that it is the fitted value when the feature is zero, then say that it is an anchor for a line that only approximates the data range, so it is not a forecast of negative sales.

A coefficient is not a lever

On the exam

A coefficient is not a lever

Association

The line describes what went together in the data. Larger budgets and larger sales moved together.

Causation

Use coefficients for prediction and prioritisation; bring causal claims to an experiment.

The fitted line describes association: what went together in the data. That is not the same as causation, the claim that pushing spend up will push sales up. For example, the shop may simply spend more in busy seasons, when sales would have been high anyway. Proof that more spend causes more sales needs a designed experiment, in which spend is changed on purpose (for example at random across weeks or markets) while everything else is kept comparable. A fitted line alone cannot deliver that proof.

Trap

"The coefficient is 2.097 with p = 0.000, so each extra unit of spend will bring 2.097 more units of sales." This is false. The p-value says the association is unlikely to be pure noise. It says nothing about cause. A tiny p-value does not turn a coefficient into a lever you can pull.

Significance level, degrees of freedom and the rejection region

On the exam

Alpha and degrees of freedom

α = 0.05

the false-alarm budget: probability of rejecting a true null, chosen before the test

df = n − k

observations minus fitted coefficients

To turn a t into a decision, you need a rule fixed in advance. The significance level alpha (α) is the probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis, chosen before the test. The common choice is 0.05. It is the false-alarm budget. If nothing is really there, you accept that 5 tests in 100 will still raise an alarm by chance. A smaller α means fewer false alarms but needs stronger evidence.

Degrees of freedom (df) count how many numbers are free to move once the model has used some of the information. The formula is df = n − k: observations minus fitted coefficients. Example: four numbers have mean 5, so they must add up to 20. Three are reported, 4, 2 and 5, which add to 11, so the last one must be 20 − 11 = 9. Only three numbers were free to move, so df = 3. Fixing the mean used up one degree of freedom. In the regression table, 30 observations and 2 fitted coefficients (intercept and slope) leave df = 30 − 2 = 28. The df set the exact shape of the reference curve that t is compared with.

The rejection region is the set of values of the statistic that would be rare if H0 were true. With α = 0.05 and a two-sided test, the middle 95 percent is "do not reject H0". The outer 2.5 percent in each tail, beyond the two critical values, is "reject H0". The 5 percent budget is split in half because a gap could be extreme in either direction. A statistic in a tail is rare if H0 is true, so we reject H0. The campus t of 3.68 lands deep in the right tail. A one-tail p-value counts results extreme in one direction only. A two-tail p-value counts results extreme on either side, and it is the common choice for coefficients, because a coefficient could be wrongly positive or wrongly negative. The decision rule is simple: if p is below 0.05, reject H0. The rejection region and the p-value always agree. A t in the 2.5 percent tail is the same event as a two-tail p below 0.05.

Be careful about what p is not. It is not the probability that H0 is true, because it is calculated by assuming H0 is true. And it says nothing about whether the effect is big enough to matter for money. That judgment needs the coefficient and its units.

From a number to a decision

On the exam

Q1. b = 2.097 with p = 0.000. The shop doubles its ad budget. What does the line predict, and what could go wrong? The line predicts that every extra unit of spend comes with 2.097 extra units of sales. If spend goes from some level x to 2x, the predicted rise is 2.097 × x units. Several things can go wrong. A doubled budget may lie outside the range of spend in the data, where the line is only a local approximation. The coefficient is an association, not a lever, so the extra sales may not appear if spend and sales moved together for another reason, such as seasons. And the CI says the true slope could be as low as 1.589. A sound answer recommends testing the bigger budget in an experiment first.

Q2. A classmate reads the intercept as negative sales at zero ads. What is the right reading? The intercept is the fitted value of the line at zero ad spend. It anchors the line so that the line fits the range of spend actually observed. Negative sales cannot exist, and zero spend is probably outside the data, so it is not a prediction.

Q3. A coefficient in a multiple regression is not significant. Drop the feature? Not automatically. A large p means the data cannot distinguish this feature's effect, holding the others fixed, from zero. It does not prove the effect is zero. The feature may be overlapping with another feature that took its credit, or the sample may be too small. Dropping it can also change the other coefficients. Decide with the business question and with performance on new data, which is exactly the complexity budget later in this chapter.

Residual plots: what the misses say

On the exam

R squared gives you one number. The residual plot shows whether the line had the right shape in the first place. You plot each residual against its fitted value and look for a pattern.

Residuals vs fitted: good and warning

Good

residuals vs fitted: no pattern. The misses look like noise; the model took the structure.

Warning

residuals vs fitted: a curve remains. The misses still curve; something systematic is left behind.

In the good case the points are scattered above and below the zero line with no pattern. In the warning case the residuals rise and then fall: a curve remains. A curve means the line is systematically too low in one range and too high in another. Something systematic is left behind, usually because the true relationship bends and a straight line was the wrong shape.

Residuals are also how you judge a forecast on new data. For each new week, compute residual = actual − predicted and keep its sign. Square each residual. Add the squares. The total is the total squared prediction error, which is the same idea as SSE, but measured on weeks the model did not see during fitting. A smaller total is better.

Worked example (my own illustration): two forecasts on three new weeks

Two models were fitted on earlier weeks. Model M had a training SSE of 40. Model N, a more flexible model, had a training SSE of 6. On three later weeks that neither model saw during fitting, the actual orders were 50, 64 and 58. Model M predicted 54, 60 and 58. Model N predicted 52, 70 and 55.

Model M's residuals: 50 − 54 = −4, 64 − 60 = +4, 58 − 58 = 0. Their sum is 0. Squares: 16 + 16 + 0 = 32.

Model N's residuals: 50 − 52 = −2, 64 − 70 = −6, 58 − 55 = +3. Squares: 4 + 36 + 9 = 49.

On the new weeks Model M wins, 32 against 49, even though Model N had the much smaller training error. Training error measures fit to the data the model was built from; prediction error measures what you care about, the next weeks. A colleague might say Model M "made no error" because its residuals sum to zero. That is wrong. It missed week 1 by 4 orders (too high) and week 2 by 4 orders (too low), and the two misses cancelled in the sum. This is the same reason OLS squares the misses in the first place. Squared errors cannot cancel, so they show the real size of the misses. And with only three weeks, the comparison is evidence, not proof. Other weeks might favour the other model, so the result needs further validation.

Trap

A sum of residuals equal to zero does not mean zero error. In fact an OLS line with an intercept always has training residuals that sum to zero, however poor it is. To measure error, square the residuals before adding them.

Bias squared is the error from a missed pattern: the model is too simple, like a straight line through a curved truth. Variance is the error from an unstable fit: the model is so flexible that it changes a lot when the sample changes, so it chases noise. Irreducible noise is the part no model can remove, because the outcome has real randomness in it.

Against model complexity from 1 to 8, bias squared falls (the model can follow more patterns) and variance rises (the model becomes jumpier). The noise line stays flat. Their sum, the total error on new data, first falls and then rises, giving a U shape with its lowest point, the sweet spot, in the middle (at 4 on the schematic). Left of the sweet spot a model underfits; right of it, it overfits. Training error keeps falling as you move right, which is why it cannot find the sweet spot.

The course turns this trade-off into business language as the complexity budget: a feature stays only if its evidence pays for it. There is a fit benefit, because extra flexibility may reduce training error. There is a complexity cost, because large coefficients add a penalty to the fit. The penalty dial sets how strongly the fit discourages large coefficients. Turn it to zero and you have plain OLS. Turn it up and the model is pushed toward smaller, simpler coefficients.

How do you set the dial? Choose the strength using validation data: rows held back from fitting. Try several strengths, fit on the training rows, and keep the strength with the lowest error on the validation rows. Do not choose by training error, because training error always prefers the weakest penalty. Shrinking coefficients and selecting features are different decisions. Making every coefficient smaller is one thing; removing some features entirely is another. The next section shows which method does which.

On the exam

All three methods start from the same SSE, the sum of squared residuals. The penalised methods add a cost for coefficient size. Here p is the number of predictors, bj is slope j, and λ (lambda) ≥ 0 is the penalty strength, the dial from the complexity budget.

Ridge charges for the squared size of each slope. Squaring makes large coefficients very expensive, so Ridge pulls all of them toward zero, but it rarely puts any of them exactly at zero. Every feature stays, just smaller, which suits many correlated predictors: where OLS lets echo columns split credit wildly, Ridge calms them together. LASSO charges for the absolute size. Under that penalty the cheapest choice for a weak predictor is often exactly zero, so LASSO can set some coefficients to zero. It shrinks and selects at once, leaving fewer predictors. A zero coefficient removes that predictor's contribution from the fitted equation; it does not prove the feature could never matter in other data.

Two rules sit under the table. First, standardize predictors (put them on a common scale) before penalising. A coefficient's size depends on the units, so without standardizing, a feature measured in small units gets a large coefficient and is punished harder for no real reason. Second, the intercept b0 is not penalized; the penalty sums start at j = 1. The intercept is the anchor that sets the level of the line, and shrinking it would only drag predictions toward zero. When λ = 0 both methods become plain OLS; as λ grows the coefficients shrink more.

Trap

"Ridge deletes correlated predictors" is wrong. Ridge shrinks and keeps everything. "OLS selects a subset" is also wrong, because OLS has no penalty and keeps every feature you give it. Only LASSO directly offers coefficients of exactly zero. And the strongest penalty is not automatically best. Pushed too far, LASSO zeroes out useful predictors and validation error rises. Among the settings that meet the team's practical limits (for example a maximum number of predictors), choose the one with the lowest validation error.

A complexity budget for your team

On the exam

Q1. Your team forecasts next month's livestream sales. Name one feature you would make prove itself before it enters. A good answer names a feature that is likely to echo an existing driver or is costly to collect, for example a likes count that mostly moves with sessions. Then it says what "prove itself" means: the feature must lower the error on validation data, not just raise the training R squared. A penalty such as LASSO can make that test automatic by zeroing out features that do not pay.

Q2. Who should set the budget: the analyst, the manager, or the platform? There is no single printed answer, so argue it. A defensible position is that the budget is a business decision: the manager weighs what complexity costs, such as data collection, maintenance, and the need to explain the model. The analyst then chooses the penalty strength on validation data inside that budget. The platform limits which data exist at all. The weak answer is "the analyst picks whatever gives the highest R squared".

Q3. The straight line has R squared 0.719. Your wiggly curve hits 0.99 on the same weeks. Which do you ship, and why? Neither number settles it, because both are training fit on the same weeks, and 0.99 is exactly what an overfit suit looks like. Compare the two on held-out weeks by their prediction error. Unless the curve wins there, ship the straight line: it is simpler and more stable, and its 0.719 is more likely to survive next month.

The short version

A coefficient is per-unit economics: one more unit of the feature comes with b more units of the target, holding the other features fixed, in units that change with the ruler. It is association, not a lever, and the intercept is an anchor, not a prediction. t = coefficient / standard error is the estimate in noise units. A t in the 2.5 percent tails, a p below α = 0.05, and a 95 percent interval that excludes zero always agree; p printed as 0.000 means below 0.0005. SST = SSR + SSE and R2 = 1 − SSE/SST, so 0.719 means about 72 percent of the swings are explained, and 0.8482 = 0.719. A curved residual plot means structure was left behind. Week 6 Clustering

Grouping without labels

Background

In Week 4 every row came with a category to predict, and in Week 5 every row came with a number to predict. This week the rows come with no target at all. A business has thousands of viewers or learners and only their behavior: how much they watch, how often they come to live classes, how much they save and ask. The question is no longer "what will this person do?" but "what kinds of people do we have?" Clustering answers it by putting similar people into the same group, so that a manager can run a different campaign, offer or service for each group. The algorithm does the sorting, but you still make the important choices: what "similar" should mean, how many groups the business can use, and what each group is called.

In supervised learning (Weeks 4 and 5) you can score a model with an accuracy or an error, because there is a right answer. In unsupervised learning, viewers arrive with behavior only, and there is no right answer to score against. So success is managerial: did the grouping change a decision? That is why, all through this week, the business and not the geometry gets the last word.

The goal of clustering is "similar inside, different across": small distances within a group, large distances between groups. A cluster is a group whose members resemble each other more than they resemble the rest. What "similar" means is a business choice: viewers can be similar on appetite (watch minutes, sessions), on engagement (likes, saves, questions) or on live habits. Choose the features, and you have already shaped the answer. The course clusters on what people do, not on demographics; demographics come back only at the end, to help name the segments. And there is a simple test for whether clustering is worth doing at all: name the action you would take per segment before you run the algorithm. Airbnb is a good example. Two hosts can both be available about 30 percent of the year, one on weekends and one in a summer block, and they need different advice. Airbnb used K-means to find 8 availability groups.

Distance and standardization

Background

To say two viewers are "close", you need a ruler. The default ruler is Euclidean distance: for each feature, take the gap between the two viewers, square it, add all the squared gaps, and take the square root. It is the straight-line distance you know from a map, extended to many features.

d(x, x′) = √[ Σj (xj − x′j)2 ]

x and x′ are two viewers, j runs over the features, and (xj − x′j) is their gap on feature j. Every feature adds its squared gap to the sum, so the scale of a feature decides how much its gap costs.

This creates the unit trap. Two viewers differ by 300 watch minutes, 10 sessions and 2 questions. The squared gaps are 90,000, 100 and 4, so the minutes gap outvotes the sessions gap 900 to 1 and the distance is really just watch time. That is an accident of units, not a business decision. The fix is to standardize every feature first, turning each value into a z-score: how many standard deviations it sits above or below its column's mean.

z = (x − μ) / σ

x is the viewer's raw value, μ is the feature's mean across all viewers, and σ is its standard deviation, the typical spread. After this, every feature has mean 0 and spread 1, so a gap of one σ costs the same in every feature. Each feature gets an equal vote.

A worked number: a feature with mean 600 and spread 300, and a viewer at 1,200. Then z = (1,200 − 600) / 300 = 2, two typical spreads above average. The same idea the other way round: a viewer at 180 minutes, with mean 240 and standard deviation 120, gives z = (180 − 240) / 120 = −0.5, half a standard deviation below the mean. Read the sign: negative means below average. One honest warning: outliers stretch σ, so with heavy tails you take logs or clip the extremes first.

Other rulers exist (Manhattan distance, cosine for Week 7's word counts, Jaccard for yes/no features), but Euclidean distance on standardized features is the default.

Trap

Standardizing does not make every feature equally important to the business. It only stops the units from choosing for you. Which features to include is still a business choice, made before the algorithm runs.

The K-means algorithm

Background

K-means (MacQueen 1967) is the workhorse of the week. You must first choose k, the number of groups. Then the algorithm runs four steps in a loop.

The K-means loop

  1. Initializeplace k centroids, at random or with a smart method
  2. Assignevery viewer joins the cluster of its nearest centroid
  3. Updateevery centroid moves to the mean of its members
  4. Repeatassign and update again until nothing moves

A centroid is the average viewer of its group, feature by feature: a representative, not a real person. Profiling means translating its numbers into a plain-language kind of viewer.

K-means has one score it tries to make small, the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS). For every viewer, take the squared distance to the centroid of its own group, then add these up over all viewers in all groups. It measures how tightly the groups hold together: how much, in total, the members "miss" their own centre. Lower is tighter. Software often reports it as inertia.

WCSS = Σgroups Σmembers x d(x, c)2

c is the centroid of the group that x belongs to, and d is the distance. The inner sum adds up one group's squared misses; the outer sum adds up all groups.

Both steps of the loop can only lower WCSS or leave it unchanged. Moving a viewer to a nearer centroid shrinks its squared miss, and the mean is the point that makes a group's squared misses as small as possible. Because WCSS can never rise and cannot go below zero, the loop must stop, usually within a handful of rounds.

Worked example: one round by hand

Five viewers sit on one standardized axis, and k = 2. The starting centroids are at −0.5 and +0.5. The viewers sit at roughly −2.1, −1.8, −0.2, 1.9 and 2.2 (these positions are approximate; the two new centroids below are the exact results).

Assign: the three left viewers are nearer to −0.5. For example, the viewer at −0.2 is 0.3 from −0.5 and 0.7 from +0.5. The two right viewers are nearer to +0.5. Update: the left centroid moves to the mean of its members, (−2.1 − 1.8 − 0.2) / 3 = −4.1 / 3 ≈ −1.37, and the right one to (1.9 + 2.2) / 2 = 2.05. In the next round nobody switches sides, so the run has converged.

A one-step check of the assign step: centroids at −2 and 4, a viewer at 1.5. The distances are |1.5 − (−2)| = 3.5 and |1.5 − 4| = 2.5, so the viewer joins the centroid at 4. Notice what assignment does not do: it never changes the viewer's data, and it never leaves a viewer unassigned.

Local solutions, restarts and K-means' opinions

Background

"It must stop" hides a trap. The loop is guaranteed to reach a minimum, not the minimum. An unlucky start, such as two starting centroids inside the same natural group, can freeze a stable but second-best grouping. There are two seatbelts: k-means++, which places the starting centroids far apart on purpose, and restarts, which run the whole algorithm many times from different starts and keep the run with the lowest WCSS. The rule: never present a single run as "the" segmentation.

Worked example: the restart rule

Same data, features, scaling and k. Three starts converge with WCSS 210, 168 and 189. You keep the run with 168, because it is the tightest grouping by the K-means objective. But be careful with words. Each run converged, which only means its loop stopped changing. Different starts can stop at different local solutions, so 168 is the best among these starts, not proof of the best possible grouping. Another start could still find something lower. And a low WCSS on its own says nothing about commercial usefulness.

K-means also has "opinions" built in: it draws round-ish territories, prefers groups of similar size (so a huge group can swallow a niche), needs k before it starts, and drafts every viewer into some group. If k is too small, unlike viewers fuse into one blob; if k is too large, real kinds split into slivers; only profiling exposes either. And inertia always falls as k grows (in one illustration, 145 at k = 2, 24 at k = 3 and 12 at k = 5). At k = n every viewer is its own group with inertia 0: perfectly tight, perfectly useless. So the objective itself cannot pick k, which is where the examined material begins.

The elbow, read as marginal returns

On the exam

Read the margin

  1. Askwhat does one more segment buy?
  2. Follow the dropthe fall in WCSS per added k
  3. Stopwhen the next segment's geometric gain no longer justifies its keep
  4. Say sono automatic rule: "we stopped at the knee" beats pretending a formula chose it

The elbow plot puts k on the horizontal axis (1 to 8 in the standard illustration) and WCSS on the vertical axis. Remember what WCSS measures: the total squared distance from every viewer to the centre of its own group. When k grows, every viewer has more centres to choose from, so WCSS always falls. That is why you cannot simply pick the k with the lowest WCSS; you would always pick the largest k.

Instead, read the curve as marginal returns. Ask what one more segment buys, by looking at the drop in WCSS from k to k + 1. At first the drops are big, because a new centroid rescues many badly placed viewers. Later the drops become small, because a new centroid only splits an existing group that was already tight. The point where the curve bends from steep to flat is the knee or elbow. On the illustrative curve, the knee is at k = 3: big drops from 1 to 2 and from 2 to 3, then only small drops after. You stop when the next segment's geometric gain no longer justifies its keep. "Keep" is a business word: every extra segment costs a campaign, a manager, content and reporting.

There is no automatic rule. Saying "we stopped at the knee" is honest, because it admits a judgment. Pretending a formula chose k is not. Real curves are often smooth, and two people may see the knee in different places.

Worked example: reading the SkillCraft elbow

K-means is fitted on the eight standardized SkillCraft columns for k = 1 to 8. The WCSS values are 32,000, 21,470, 17,067, 14,552, 13,094, 11,869, 11,073 and 10,624. Compute the drop per added k: 10,530 (1 to 2), 4,403 (2 to 3), 2,515 (3 to 4), 1,458 (4 to 5), 1,225 (5 to 6), 796 (6 to 7) and 449 (7 to 8). The first split buys a lot. Each later one buys less. Returns shrink after k = 4, but the curve has no sharp corner. This is exactly why there is no automatic rule, and why you want a second piece of evidence: the silhouette.

The silhouette: membership evidence, per viewer

On the exam

The elbow judges the whole map at once. The silhouette (Rousseeuw 1987) judges each viewer. For each viewer it asks: how similar is it to its own group, compared with the nearest other group? You compute two numbers for each viewer. The first, a, is the average distance from the viewer to the other members of its own group. The second, b, is the average distance from the viewer to the members of the nearest other group, the neighbouring crowd it would most likely be confused with.

s = (b − a) / max(a, b)

The top, b − a, is how much closer the viewer is to its own group than to the next one. The bottom, max(a, b), is the larger of the two distances; dividing by it keeps s between −1 and +1, so viewers can be compared.

The score runs from −1 to +1

Negative

Near 0

Read the scale from left to right. Negative means b is smaller than a: the viewer is closer to another group than to its own, so it was probably filed in the wrong group. Near 0 means a and b are about equal: the viewer sits on the boundary between two groups, and which side it lands on is a coin flip. Near +1 means a is much smaller than b: the viewer is well inside its own group, a confident member. If you average s over all viewers, you get one number per candidate k, which you compare against the elbow.

Worked example: one viewer, three positions

One viewer has a = 2.0 (average distance to its own group) and b = 5.0 (average distance to the nearest other group). The larger of the two is 5.0. So s = (5.0 − 2.0) / 5.0 = 3.0 / 5.0 = 0.6. Comfortably placed: well inside its own group.

Now move its own group farther away, so a = 4.8 while b stays 5.0. The larger is still 5.0. So s = (5.0 − 4.8) / 5.0 = 0.2 / 5.0 = 0.04. That is almost zero: the viewer is on the boundary, barely closer to its own group than to the next one.

Finally, if b is smaller than a, the top of the fraction is negative, so s is negative: the viewer sits closer to another group. As my own illustration with the same viewer: if a = 4.8 and b = 2.0, the larger is now a, and s = (2.0 − 4.8) / 4.8 = −2.8 / 4.8 ≈ −0.58.

Trap

The bottom of the fraction is the larger of a and b, not always b. When b is smaller than a, you divide by a. Students who always divide by b get a number below −1 in the negative case, which is impossible. If your silhouette falls outside −1 to +1, check the denominator.

Now turn the silhouette into a tool for choosing k. For each candidate k (2 to 8 in the illustration), fit K-means, compute every viewer's silhouette and take the average. That gives one number per candidate, and you can draw them as bars. You cannot compute it for k = 1, because there is no "other group" to measure b. The average silhouette peaks where membership is most confident; in the illustration the bars rise from k = 2 to a clear peak at k = 4 and then fall slowly to k = 8.

The second idea is "two evidences, one answer". The elbow and the silhouette measure different things. The elbow measures tightness: how much WCSS one more group buys. The silhouette measures separation: how much closer viewers are to their own group than to the next. When the structure in the data is real, the two point the same way. When they disagree, the structure is weak and your judgment, and the business, must carry more of the decision.

Worked example: SkillCraft's silhouette by k

The curves are geometry. A segment must also work as a business object, and these four tests from Kotler and Keller (2016) check that. Measurable: you can count it and describe it, with a size and a profile. Substantial: it is big enough to serve profitably. This is about profit, not about a percentage on its own. Reachable: your channels actually touch it. A group you cannot target with a post, a message or a live room is a fact, not a segment. Actionable: someone will run a campaign for it. The rule is strict: the four tests all feed into one gate, and a segment earns a budget only if it passes all four.

Then comes the floor: if the business has three campaigns planned, k is at least 3, whatever the curves say. The plan sets a lower limit on the number of groups, because with fewer groups at least one campaign would have no audience of its own. So the business can overrule the curves from below. Suppose the silhouette peaks at k = 4 but a team needs three segments for a campaign, or the elbow says 3 and the team has four campaigns: a sensible answer reports both what the geometry nominates and what the plan needs, and why the final k was chosen.

Exam tip

When a case question asks you to choose k, use this order: the curves nominate, stability cross-examines, the business decides. Then apply the four tests by name to the segment in question.

It knows nothing about margins, prices or campaigns. Remember also that K-means prefers groups of similar size, so a huge group can swallow a niche. Choosing a larger k is one way to protect the niche.

"The silhouette is highest at k = 2, so k = 2 is correct" is the wrong reasoning. The silhouette nominates; it does not decide. A higher score does not mean more business value.

A k that wins once may be one seed's opinion, because K-means depends on its random start and on the exact rows in the file. So you run three steps. A stable structure is safe to plan campaigns against; an unstable one is a story about the seed. The short version

Clustering groups people with no target, so success means a better decision, not a score. Standardize first with z = (x − mean) / SD, or the feature with the biggest units decides every distance. K-means assigns each point to its nearest centroid and moves each centroid to its group's mean until nothing changes; it always stops, but only at a local solution, so use restarts and keep the lowest within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), calling it the best among these starts, not the best possible. The number of groups is a judgment, not a lookup. The elbow reads WCSS as marginal returns and you stop at the knee, with no automatic rule. The silhouette s = (b − a) / max(a, b) compares a viewer's average distance to its own group (a) with that to the nearest other group (b): near +1 is confident, near 0 is on the boundary, negative is misfiled; 2.0 and 5.0 give 0.6, and 4.8 and 5.0 give 0.04. When the elbow and the average silhouette agree, the structure is real; still check stability across resamples and seeds (re-run, compare, report). Then the business decides: Kotler's four tests (measurable, substantial, reachable, actionable), the floor (three campaigns means k is at least 3), and when geometry and business disagree, geometry loses. A cluster becomes a segment when you name it from its extremes and it passes the line-up test. Judge a campaign against the segment's own baseline, use segments as the language and behaviors as the forecast (R squared 0.4372 against 0.6807, a gap of 0.244), and put the budget where engagement is high and conversion has room to move, which at SkillCraft means the systematic learners.

P6.4 (Short answer) An analyst runs K-means once on a fictional gaming platform's player file with k = 5, gets a WCSS of 2,410, and says: "The algorithm converged, so this is the best segmentation." Give two reasons why this claim is too strong, in three to five sentences.

Show the answer

Convergence only means the assign and update loop stopped changing; it reaches a local solution that depends on the starting centroids, so another start could settle on a different grouping with a lower WCSS. The analyst should use restarts (and k-means++ starts) and keep the run with the lowest WCSS, and even then call it the best among those starts, not the best possible grouping. Second, WCSS measures only how tightly members sit around their centroids; it always falls as k grows, so it cannot say whether five is the right number of groups or whether the groups are useful. That needs the elbow and silhouette evidence, a stability check across resamples, and the business tests.

A brand that launches a product can receive thousands of reviews a day, in more than one language, and no team can read them all. Natural language processing (NLP) turns that text into numbers so you can count themes, find the reviews that matter, and estimate how customers feel. Once text is numbers, the classifiers of Week 4, the regressions of Week 5 and the clustering of Week 6 all work on it again.

A classifier or a regression cannot read a sentence. It needs a table of numbers, one row per document and one column per feature. So the first job is to choose a representation: a rule that turns each review into a row of numbers. Four representations appear in order, and each keeps different information.

No representation is best in general. They keep different information and need different resources, so you choose the one that supports your analytical question. Normalization: tokens, lemmas and stems

On the exam

Before you can count words, you must decide what a "word" is. Normalization is the cleaning step that does this, in three operations.

Normalization: three operations

  1. Tokenizesplit into words and symbols: 'The battery died fast!' → [ The · battery · died · fast · ! ]
  2. Lemmatizedictionary form: was, were, is → be; batteries → battery
  3. Stemremove word endings using rules: automation, automatic → automat (need not be a dictionary word)

Tokenize only splits. Each piece is a token, the unit a tokenizer produces; it may be a word, a punctuation mark or part of a word. Lemmatize replaces each word with its dictionary form, called the lemma, so different grammatical forms of the same word count as one feature. A pipeline that maps "was", "were" and "is" to "be" is doing lemmatization, not tokenization. Stem cuts endings off with simple rules. Stemming is computationally simple, but the result need not be a real dictionary word. That is the difference you must remember: a lemma is always a dictionary word; a stem is whatever is left after chopping, and "automat" is not an English word.

Why should a manager care? Removing "not" can reverse sentiment: "not good" becomes "good". Preprocessing choices affect the information available to the model. The practical requirement is to inspect the tokenizer's input and output, even when you use prepared code. You do not need to write the code, but you must look at what it produced.

Worked example: one review through normalization

Take the illustrative review "The batteries barely lasted a day!". The raw text is a single string. After tokenizing (and lowercasing) it becomes seven tokens: [ the · batteries · barely · lasted · a · day · ! ]. After lemmatizing it becomes [ the · battery · barely · last · a · day · ! ].

Two things changed. "batteries" became "battery" and "lasted" became "last". Now this review and another that says "the battery lasts" share the same features, so a count of battery complaints will find both. Nothing else changed: "barely" and "day" were already in dictionary form.

If the review had said "did not last", you must keep "not", because it carries the negative meaning. A cleaning step that drops "not" would turn a complaint into a neutral or positive statement about lasting.

English · Natural Language Toolkit (nltk)

"The battery died fast, honestly!" → [ The · battery · died · fast · , · honestly · ! ]. Spaces and punctuation help identify token boundaries.

Chinese · jieba

续航很差，戴久了耳朵疼 → [续航 · 很差 · ， · 戴久 · 了 · 耳朵 · 疼] (battery life / very poor / punctuation / wear for a long time / grammatical particle / ears / hurt). A dictionary and probabilities determine token boundaries.

Because jieba depends on its dictionary, a word that is not in the dictionary gets cut into the wrong pieces. This matters most for brand terms, which companies invent all the time.

Worked example: a coined brand term, before and after

The illustrative sentence is 声阔小金标的调音偏暖 ("Soundcore's 'Little Gold Label' has a warm tuning"). The product term is 小金标, an invented name that the default dictionary does not know.

Before the fix, jieba gives [ 声阔 · 小金 · 标的 · 调音 · 偏暖 ]. The product name is broken in two, and its last character 标 is glued to the particle 的 to form 标的, which is a different word. So two errors happen at once: the product is never counted as itself, and 的 joins the wrong token.

After adding 小金标 to the dictionary, jieba gives [ 声阔 · 小金标 · 的 · 调音 · 偏暖 ]. The product term stays one token, and 的 is back in its normal role of marking possession. Add brand terms to the dictionary before counting.

Dictionaries require regular maintenance

Built-in terms

Common words: 入耳式 (in-ear) and 降噪 (noise cancellation) are segmented correctly without customization.

Added terms

Coined brand terms, product codes and campaign hashtags: one entry per line, as with 小金标.

Maintenance responsibility

New campaigns introduce terms. Outdated dictionaries can cause segmentation errors that affect later analysis.

Sixty years of Xinhua Dictionary editions illustrate the same point: a dictionary is something someone has to keep updating. In the SoundPeak lab, the same problem appears with 充电盒 (charging case), which the default dictionary splits into 充电 and 盒真 until one line is added. Stop lists are also language-specific: the English list contains 179 words; Chinese lists are separate, with particles such as 的 (linking particle), 了 (aspect particle) and 在 (at/in). The word 很 (very) is a judgement call: its relevance depends on the analytical task.

Trap

"Tokenization is a technicality; the model sees the same text either way" is wrong. The tokenizer decides what the words are before any model runs. If a product name is split, its mentions scatter across wrong tokens and are undercounted, and every later number inherits the error.

Stop words: what to remove and what to keep

On the exam

Stop words are tokens you exclude for a task, usually very common grammatical words that carry little topic information. Stop lists are language-specific files: you cannot apply the English list to Chinese text.

Stop words: remove versus retain

Terms removed

the · a · and · of · 的 · 了 · 在. Grammatical words; the English list contains 179. Meanings of the Chinese particles depend on context.

Negation terms retained

not · never · no · 不 · 没. Removing negation turns 不好 (not good) into 好 (good), reversing the meaning.

Inspect the stop list before applying it. Adapt it to the analytical question; removing negation can change sentiment interpretation. Even if you keep "not", a plain word count only records that a negator appeared somewhere in the review, not that it sat next to "good". Keeping negation is necessary, but word order is still lost, which is the problem the next section shows.

Bag of words: documents as numbers

On the exam

The simplest representation is the bag of words: each document becomes a row of token counts, with one column per word in the vocabulary and no information about word order. Imagine pouring the words of a review into a bag and shaking it. You know which words are inside and how many times, but not their order.

Reviewsoundgreatbatteryterrible
d1 · 'Great sound, terrible battery'1111
d2 · 'Terrible sound, great battery'1111

The first customer likes the sound and hates the battery. The second customer is the opposite. A product manager would send these two reviews to different teams. Yet their rows are identical, because both contain the same four words once each. This is the limitation: different meanings, identical rows, because word order is not preserved.

Why use counts at all, then? Because once text is numeric, you can feed it into the classifiers of Week 4, the regressions of Week 5 and the clustering methods of Week 6. Counts are cheap and easy to inspect. N-grams preserve local order; contextual embeddings can represent broader context. Those are the fixes for the problem in this table.

Exam tip

If a question shows two reviews with the same words in a different order and asks why a model treats them alike, the answer is that bag of words (and TF-IDF, which is built on it) ignores word order. The fix is n-grams for short phrases or embeddings for wider context.

TF-IDF: weighting words by frequency and rarity

On the exam

Raw counts reward words that appear everywhere. In earbud reviews, "earbuds" appears in nearly every review, so it tells you almost nothing about what makes one review different. TF-IDF fixes this by multiplying two factors.

TF-IDF = frequent here × rare elsewhere = informative

Term frequency (TF)

How often the word appears in this review.

Inverse document frequency (IDF)

How rare the word is across the whole collection, including this review.

'earbuds'

If it appears in nearly every review, it has low IDF and distinguishes reviews weakly.

'firmware'

A rarer term has higher IDF. Interpret its context before concluding that it indicates a business problem.

TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t),   with   IDF(t) = ln(N / df(t))

TF(t, d) is the number of times term t appears in document d. N is the number of documents in the collection. df(t), the document frequency, is the number of documents that contain t at least once. ln is the natural logarithm. A word in every document has df = N, so IDF = ln(1) = 0 and its weight is zero however often it appears. A word in only one document gets the largest IDF, ln(N).

A high weight tells you a word is distinctive, not that it is bad news. TF-IDF is a common baseline for document retrieval and text analysis.

Worked example: TF-IDF on three reviews

The collection has N = 3 reviews:

d1: "Great sound, perfect fit, lovely case". d2: "Terrible battery, battery died every hour". d3: "Great price, lovely case, perfect fit".

Step 1: document frequency. "fit" is in d1 and d3, so df = 2. "great" is in d1 and d3, so df = 2. "battery" is only in d2, so df = 1. "sound" is only in d1, so df = 1. "died" is only in d2, so df = 1.

Step 2: IDF with the natural log. For words in 2 of 3 reviews: ln(3/2) = ln(1.5) = 0.405, which rounds to 0.41. For words in 1 of 3 reviews: ln(3/1) = ln(3) = 1.099, which rounds to 1.10.

Step 3: weights in d2. "fit" and "great" do not appear in d2, so TF = 0 and their weight in d2 is 0 × 0.41 = 0. "battery" appears twice in d2, so its weight is 2 × 1.10 = 2.20. "died" appears once, so its weight is 1 × 1.10 = 1.10.

Step 4: weight of "sound" in d1. "sound" appears once in d1 and its IDF is ln(3) ≈ 1.10, so its weight in d1 is 1 × 1.10 = 1.10. Every other word in d1 ("great", "perfect", "fit", "lovely", "case") also appears in d3, so each has weight 1 × 0.41 = 0.41. "sound" is the highest weight in d1.

Business reading. In d2, "battery" has the highest weight, and reading the original review confirms that the battery is the subject of the complaint. In d1, the word that sets the review apart is "sound", because the rest of d1 repeats d3.

Trap

A high TF-IDF weight does not mean "a problem". It means "distinctive in this document". "firmware" could be distinctive because people praise a firmware update. Always read the reviews behind a high-weight word before you name it a complaint.

Exam tip

Use the natural log, ln, not log base 10. ln(3) = 1.10 and ln(1.5) = 0.41; with log base 10 you would get 0.48 and 0.18, which do not match the course numbers. If a word appears in every document, its IDF is ln(1) = 0, so its weight is zero.

N-grams: keeping local word order

On the exam

An n-gram is a sequence of n consecutive words (tokens) that keeps their local order.

N-grams by length

  1. Unigram'not'
  2. Bigram'not good'
  3. Trigram'not good value'

If you add bigrams as features, "not good" becomes its own column, separate from "good", and the bag of words no longer confuses the two. In the bag-of-words example, the bigrams "great sound" and "terrible battery" would finally separate d1 from d2.

Context changes probabilities. Among all letters an "h" might appear about 4% of the time, but right after a "t" about 20% of the time (both numbers illustrative). The preceding letter changes the probability of the next one, and words work the same way. N-grams record this local context.

The cost is size. Each extra word of length creates many more possible combinations, so longer n-grams give larger, sparser vocabularies: more columns, and most of them are zero for most reviews. Choose the length for the task; bigrams are often enough to capture negation. A Transformer uses a mechanism called attention to represent context, instead of a fixed table of n-gram counts, and language prediction is one application of it. You only need to know that Transformers handle context more flexibly than n-grams.

Embeddings and cosine similarity

On the exam

An embedding is a vector representation that can encode patterns of word usage. Each word gets a list of coordinates, and training adjusts these coordinates using the contexts in which words appear. Words used in similar contexts end up close together. The number of dimensions depends on the model.

The famous illustration is king − man + woman ≈ queen. The arrow from "man" to "king" is about the same as the arrow from "woman" to "queen": similar offsets encode a relation. Start at "king", subtract the "man" direction, add the "woman" direction, and you land near "queen". This is an analogy, not a rule. The relation is approximate, it depends on the training data, and the positions are only a sketch. For business use, review search still needs the original text and task-specific validation.

Once texts are vectors, you need a way to say how close two of them are. The course uses cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between two nonzero vectors.

cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

a and b are the two vectors (for example two reviews). a · b is the dot product: multiply matching coordinates and add them up. ‖a‖ and ‖b‖ are the lengths of the vectors. Dividing by the lengths removes the effect of size, so only direction matters. A value of 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular directions (unrelated), and −1 means opposite directions.

Three facts are easy exam material. First, smaller angle means larger cosine. Second, vector length does not affect cosine: a long review and a short review can point the same way and get a high score. Third, a zero vector has no direction, so its cosine is undefined. This happens, for example, when none of the query's words are in the vocabulary.

Worked example: the battery query

The query is "The battery died within an hour". Against "Power drains faster than advertised", the illustrative cosine is about 0.7. Against "The case scratches easily", it is about 0.4. The first candidate shares no words with the query, yet it ranks higher, because in an embedding space "battery died" and "power drains" point in similar directions. The second shares "The" but is about a different problem.

The reading: a keyword search for "battery" would miss the power-drain review completely. A meaning-based search finds it first. That ranking is the value of embeddings. Remember the values are illustrative and precomputed; you are expected to read them, not calculate them.

Worked example: cosine by hand (my own illustration)

Suppose two reviews have count vectors a = (2, 0, 1) and b = (4, 0, 2) over the words (battery, case, drain). Then a · b = 2×4 + 0×0 + 1×2 = 10. ‖a‖ = √(4 + 0 + 1) = √5, and ‖b‖ = √(16 + 0 + 4) = √20. So cos θ = 10 / (√5 × √20) = 10 / √100 = 10 / 10 = 1. Review b is just review a written twice as long, and cosine says they point the same way. That is what "length does not matter" means. If a third review has the vector (0, 3, 0), its dot product with a is 0, so the cosine is 0: no shared direction.

High cosine is similarity of wording or of vector direction, not proof of the same complaint, the same sentiment or the same cause. And a query with no known words gives a zero vector, so the cosine is undefined, not zero.

Topic models and latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)

On the exam

Counting words tells you which words are common. A topic model goes one step further and asks what the reviews are about, by finding groups of words that tend to appear together. The words are observed; the topics are hidden and must be estimated.

Start from a documents × words count table. Four invented reviews are R1 "Comfortable fit, but the battery fades", R2 "Light on my ears; charging takes hours", R3 "Battery lasts hours after each charge" and R4 "Light fit, easy on my ears". Over six words they give this table:

A fitted model estimates two tables: topic-word probabilities, with K × V = 2 × 6 numbers, and review mixtures, with D × K = 4 × 2 numbers. You can already guess the pattern: fit, ears and light go together, and battery, charge and hours go together.

The model used here is latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA). "Latent" means hidden. LDA is built on two mixtures.

LDA: two mixtures

A review is a mixture of topics

"Comfortable, but the battery fades." Illustrative mixture: Comfort 60%, Battery 40%. One review can contain both topics.

Each topic assigns probabilities to words

Comfort: fit · ears · light; P(battery | Comfort) = 0.06. Battery life: battery · charge · hours; P(battery | Battery life) = 0.35.

A word can have nonzero probability under several topics. This is more realistic than forcing each review into one group as clustering does.

P(w = v | θd, Φ) = Σk=1K θdk φkv

This is the probability that a word drawn from review d is the word v. d is the review, v is the word, k is the topic. θdk is the share of topic k in review d (the review mixture). φkv is the probability of word v inside topic k (the topic-word probability). You multiply "how much of this topic is in the review" by "how likely this word is in the topic", then add over all topics.

Worked example: the probability of "battery"

The review mixture is Comfort 60% and Battery life 40%, so θ = 0.60 and 0.40. The topic-word probabilities for "battery" are P(battery | Comfort) = 0.06 and P(battery | Battery life) = 0.35.

Comfort contributes 0.60 × 0.06 = 0.036. Battery life contributes 0.40 × 0.35 = 0.140. The total is 0.036 + 0.140 = 0.176, or 17.6%.

The reading: a randomly chosen word from this review has a 17.6% chance of being "battery". Most of that chance comes from the Battery life topic, even though that topic is the smaller share of the review, because "battery" is much more likely inside that topic. All values are illustrative.

Evaluate topics before choosing an action

K specifies the number of topics. α (alpha) controls prior expectations about review mixtures, roughly how many topics you expect one review to mix. Different K values, different preprocessing and even repeated fits of the same model can give different topics, so compare them. Lower perplexity need not produce clearer topics. Perplexity is a statistical fit score where lower means the model predicts the words better, but a better fit score can still give topics that humans find harder to interpret.

The biggest trap is that one topic can hold different meanings. The same battery topic can contain three kinds of comment:

"The battery lasts for hours. Excellent." A product strength.

"The battery dies after two hours." A report to investigate.

"How many hours should the battery last?" An information need.

All three use battery and hours, so the topic model puts them together. A topic tells you what people talk about, not how they feel or what they want. Inspect the original comments and their sentiment before choosing an action.

"The battery topic is large, so we have a battery problem" does not follow. A topic groups words, not attitudes. Praise, complaints and questions can share one topic. You need sentiment, and a look at the reviews, before you act.

Sentiment analysis at scale

On the exam

The second half of the week asks how customers feel. The business task is to estimate the sentiment expressed in each review, every day. The input is the review text plus a star rating where available. The output can be one of three forms: a class (positive, negative or neutral), a sentiment score, or sentiment per aspect. An aspect is an entity, attribute or component that the text evaluates, such as sound, battery or case.

The level of analysis matters. Document-level sentiment gives one label for the whole review. Aspect-level sentiment gives one per feature. 'Great sound, terrible battery' becomes sound + and battery −. A manager can act on that, because it points to the battery.

Method 1: lexicon scoring, and its failures

On the exam

A lexicon is a word list with sentiment scores or categories. Lexicon scoring uses a fixed list and adds up the hits.

score = positive hits − negative hits

Count the words in the review that are on the positive list and the words on the negative list, and subtract. A score above zero is classed positive, below zero negative, and zero neutral.

Lexicon scoring: method, advantages, limitations, use

Scoring method

A fixed word list supplies scores. Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER) adds rules for English social text. The lab uses its own English word list.

Advantages

Fast scoring, low computational cost and an inspectable word list.

Limitations

Slang: 'sick fit!' may be scored as negative. Negation: 'not bad at all' requires a negation rule. Domain-specific terms may be absent.

Potential use

Rapid sentiment summaries for standard language, subject to validation.

The word list and scoring rules can be inspected directly. For Chinese the lab uses SnowNLP, which returns a score between 0 and 1 (0.5 or more reads as positive).

Worked example: the lexicon, word by word

Row C000301 of the course dataset, rated 1 star out of 5, says: 'Amazing battery, I charge them twice a day.'

Go word by word. "amazing" is a positive word: +1. "battery" is not in the list: 0. "charge" is not in the list: 0. No other word matches. The total is +1, so the review is classified POSITIVE.

That is wrong. Charging earbuds twice a day means the battery is bad, and the 1-star rating agrees. The positive word is used sarcastically. Word-level scoring cannot see sarcasm, because the meaning lives in the situation the sentence describes, not in any single word.

Sentiment errors: three challenge families

Negation · 15 reviews

'Not bad at all for the price' needs a negation rule. An English scorer with a negation window handles this example correctly.

Sarcasm · 15 reviews

'Amazing battery, I charge them twice a day.' Positive wording expresses dissatisfaction.

Mixed language · 15 reviews

这个bass 真的顶 ("This bass is really excellent."). 顶 is positive slang. SnowNLP classifies the full example correctly.

The 4,000 synthetic reviews include 15 examples of each challenge family, and not all are misclassified. SnowNLP matches the provided labels on 243 of 355 Chinese reviews. Check it: 243 / 355 = 0.6845, or 68.45%. So about one in three Chinese reviews gets the wrong label. The lab reports that the English course lexicon matches on 254 of 319, which is 79.62%.

Validate before reporting sentiment

  1. Inspect 50 random casesa human sees sarcasm in seconds
  2. Examine positive wording beside a 1-star ratingthat disagreement is a red flag
  3. Evaluate rules and thresholds separatelyso you know which change fixed what

Trap

A sentiment score you have not checked is a number you cannot quote. A lexicon fails in predictable families (negation, sarcasm, slang, mixed language, missing domain words). Measure agreement with labels and read the errors before you report a dashboard figure.

Named entity recognition

Background

Sentiment analysis estimates the attitude a text expresses. Named entity recognition (NER) identifies the entities a text mentions, sorted into categories such as person, organization, place, product, money and date. It answers "who and what is named?", not "how does the writer feel?". Its uses are to route a complaint to the team responsible for the named product or service, and to extract competitor mentions and store relevant entities as structured fields. From "The BrightNote app failed during my Paris trip", NER gives product: BrightNote and place: Paris. A sentiment score or a word count would not give those fields. If a question says "NER tells you how positive a review is", that is false. This idea was tested on the mock exam.

The short version

Models need numbers, so text must be cleaned and represented first. Tokenizing splits text; lemmatizing maps forms to a dictionary word ("was, were, is" to "be"); stemming chops endings and may leave a non-word ("automat"). English uses spaces (nltk); Chinese uses a dictionary and probabilities (jieba), so brand terms must be added or they split wrongly. Remove stop words, but keep negation, or "not good" becomes "good". Bag of words loses order, so "great sound, terrible battery" and "terrible sound, great battery" get identical rows. TF-IDF is TF × ln(N/df): frequent here and rare elsewhere; in the three-review example battery scores 2 × 1.10 = 2.20 in d2, sound 1.10 in d1, while great and fit have IDF 0.41. N-grams keep local order at the cost of larger, sparser vocabularies. Embeddings put similar usage close together; king − man + woman ≈ queen is an analogy, not a rule. Cosine compares direction only (1 same, 0 perpendicular, −1 opposite), ignores length, is undefined for a zero vector, and a high cosine does not prove the same complaint. LDA treats each review as a mixture of topics and each topic as word probabilities (0.60 × 0.06 + 0.40 × 0.35 = 0.176); read the word bars and the reviews before naming a topic, and remember one topic can mix praise, complaints and questions. For sentiment, aspect level tells you what to fix. The lexicon (positive hits minus negative hits) is cheap and auditable but misses sarcasm (C000301 scores +1 at 1 star); SnowNLP agrees on only 243 of 355 Chinese reviews (68.45%), so validate before reporting. Supervised learning is Week 4 with TF-IDF columns and needs good labels, which star ratings are not. An LLM zero-shot with fixed output fields can catch sarcasm but costs more and varies. Route clean language to a lexicon, stable labeled tasks to a supervised model, and aspects, sarcasm or mixed language to LLM-assisted analysis.

Week 8 Social network analysis

Nodes, edges and components

Background

Most of the data in earlier weeks sits in tables: one row per customer, one row per review. A lot of business value lives between the rows, in who works with whom, who reposts whom, and who brought whom to the platform. Social network analysis (SNA) turns those relationships into a map. It is the general method of network analysis applied to people, and it adds social ideas such as brokers and weak ties.

A network has two parts. A node (also called a vertex) is the thing being connected: a person, a company, an account. An edge (also called a tie or a link) is a relationship between two nodes: collaborates with, reposts, mentions. An edge is undirected when the relationship is mutual by nature, such as a collaboration where both accounts appeared together. An edge is directed when it has a direction: account X reposted account Y, which does not mean Y reposted X. A component is an island of connected nodes: inside it, every node can reach every other node by following edges, and no edge leaves the island. The largest component is where most of the action is.

Reachability follows directly from the edges you are given. If two components have no edge between them, a message that moves only along edges cannot cross from one to the other, however close they look on a drawing. A picture of a network is just a layout; only the edge list is data. With directed edges you must also respect the arrow: an arrow from U to P lets a message go from U to P, never from P back to U.

Betweenness: counting the paths through you

On the exam

Betweenness asks how often an account lies on the shortest path between two other accounts. Take every pair of accounts. Find the shortest route between them. If that route passes through account v (and v is not one of the two ends), v gets a point. Add up the points over all pairs, and you have v's betweenness. An account with high betweenness is a broker: many conversations have to pass through it. In the corner every pair has exactly one shortest path, because the only loop is the left triangle, so you can count whole pairs. In bigger networks, when a pair has several equally short paths, each path carries a share of the point.

The first use is crossing communities. If you want a message to travel from one group into another, seed it at the broker, because the broker is where the groups touch. Think of a single bridge over a river: every crossing pays it a visit.

The second use is finding change agents. Battilana and Casciaro (2012) found that the people who drive organisational change tend to sit in high-betweenness positions. They bridge groups that do not otherwise talk, so they see more information and can carry ideas across.

The third point is the risk side. A broker can be a single point of failure. Where no second road exists, removing the broker splits the map. So high betweenness is good news when you want to spread something, and a warning when you depend on one account to hold two groups together. The contrast to remember: degree never saw the crossing; betweenness sees nothing else.

Closeness: how fast you reach everyone

On the exam

Closeness measures how near an account is to everyone else. For each account, find the shortest distance in hops (number of edges) to every other account, and add those distances up. A small distance sum means the account can get a message to everyone quickly. To turn the sum into a score where bigger means better, you divide the number of other accounts by the sum.

Closeness = (n − 1) / distance sum

Here n is the number of accounts in the network, so n − 1 is the number of other accounts (7 in the corner). The distance sum is the total of the shortest hop distances from this account to every other account. A lower sum gives a higher closeness. The highest possible score is 1, for an account tied directly to everyone.

A126 (handle city_pace_02) leads on betweenness, yet it is only tenth on degree, with 15 ties, less than half of A003's count. Two leaders means two definitions of mattering, and both rankings can be computed in full for every account. If you gave a retention bonus to "the most important account" by follower count, you would pick A003 and might miss the account that actually holds the scenes together.

Exam tip

When a question gives you two accounts, one with many ties and one with high betweenness, the expected answer is almost always "it depends on the job". Say what you lose if each one leaves: a hub's departure thins reach, a broker's departure can cut the short roads between groups.

What a community is

On the exam

The second half of the topic moves from single accounts to groups.

What a community is

The definition

What it buys

A community is a set of nodes that is denser inside than outside: the members have many ties to each other and few ties to the rest of the network. That is the whole definition. Picture two dense clusters of nodes with one thin crossing between them. The phrase "markets within the market" is the business meaning: a large network is often several smaller networks loosely joined.

What does a community buy you? Natural segments, meaning groups of customers or creators who actually interact. Regional pockets, such as a city or district where ties cluster. And fraud rings: accounts that trade only with each other. A fraud ring shows up as a community because its members are densely tied inside and barely tied to anyone outside, which is unusual for honest buyers and sellers. The key point is that nobody declares the groups: the algorithm finds them in the ties.

Modularity and the Louvain search

On the exam

To find communities, a computer needs two things: a search method that tries different splits, and a score that says how good each split is.

Start with the score. Modularity, written Q, gives every way of splitting the network into groups a number: the edges inside the groups, minus what chance alone would give. Higher is tighter. "What chance alone would give" matters because big, well-connected groups would hold many inside ties even if ties were placed at random. Modularity only rewards inside ties beyond that random expectation. A Q near zero means the split is no better than chance.

Q = (share of edges inside the groups) − (share expected inside the groups by chance)

The first part is the actual fraction of all edges that fall inside groups. The second part is the fraction you would expect inside those same groups if the ties were scattered at random, keeping each account's number of ties. A good split has many more inside edges than chance would predict, so Q is clearly positive. The full version is Q = Σ over groups of [ lc/m − (dc/2m)2 ], where m is the number of edges, lc the edges inside group c, and dc the sum of degrees in group c. For the exam you need to read Q, not compute it.

Now the search. The Louvain method (Blondel et al., 2008) works in three plain steps. Every account starts alone, in a group of one. Then the algorithm moves each account to the neighbouring group that raises Q the most. It repeats until nothing moves, because no single move can raise Q any further. (Behind the scenes, Louvain then merges each group into a single super-node and runs the moves again, but the three steps are what you need.) The key feature for the exam is that you never tell Louvain how many groups to find; the number of groups comes out of the search. Platforms run this nightly on millions of nodes. On the 240-account course network it finds seven communities at Q = 0.808.

A second method is label propagation: every account starts with its own label, and at each step an account takes the label most common among its neighbours. Labels spread through dense areas until they stabilise, and each final label is a community.

Then comes the honest limit. Modularity has a blind spot for size: small groups get merged into big ones, because the score cannot see a group below a certain size. If you are looking for a tiny fraud ring of four accounts inside a network of millions, a modularity search may swallow it into a larger community.

Trap

A high Q says the split is tight. It does not say the groups mean anything in business terms, and it does not promise that every small real group was found. To check meaning, you compare the groups with an outside variable, such as the accounts' handles or content categories.

How things travel: cascades and seeds

Background

Information, adoption and rumours move along ties, not through the air. A cascade is a spread that starts from one or more seeds and moves outward, round by round. The simplest rule: every newly active account activates all its neighbours in the next round. On the corner, seed A003 (round 0). Round 1: A005, A012 and A038. Round 2: A126 and A173. Round 3: A073 and A089, and the corner is covered. Seed A012 and the corner is also covered in three rounds. Seed A126 and it takes four, because the spread has to drain one hop per round down the left side. Same map, different clock: the seed sets the speed.

When you trace a round-by-round rule on a small directed network, follow the rules strictly. An account activates only once. A newly activated account sends in the next round, not immediately. If two arrows reach the same account in the same round, or a later arrow reaches an account that is already active, it does not count as extra reach: you count distinct accounts. And you can only move in the direction of the arrow, so an account whose only arrow points into the active set can never be activated. State whether your total includes the seed.

Trap

This was tested on the mock exam: a round-by-round spread on a small directed network. The usual lost marks come from counting an account twice when two arrows reach it, letting a message travel against an arrow, or forgetting to say whether the seed is in the total.

Real spread is random: a message passes along a tie only with some probability. The independent cascade model gives each newly active account one chance to activate each neighbour, here with probability 0.10. Because the outcome changes from run to run, one run proves nothing. A fair comparison of seed choices uses the same 240-account network, the same model and probability, equal budgets (one head account costs what three waist accounts cost), and 300 runs each, reporting the average reach. One head account, A003, reached 11.6 accounts on average. Three waist accounts with 12 ties each and barely overlapping neighbourhoods reached 24.2, which is 2.09 times the head. One bridge account, A126, reached 10.5, so it still lost to the head. Portfolios beat heroes; one bridge alone is not a strategy. Three more ideas belong here: thresholds (people often adopt only after two or more exposures), weak ties (Granovetter, 1973: acquaintances carry new information across groups) and the K-factor, K = invites sent × conversion, where K above 1 means the spread sustains itself.

Exam tip

For a "is seed X the best?" question, the marks are for fairness: keep the network, transmission rule, probability, budget and number of rounds the same; run each seed many times because transmissions are random; report the average number of distinct accounts reached (with the same convention about counting the seed), and ideally the spread of results too. One successful cascade, or a simulation where every transmission succeeds, cannot rank seeds.

Network effects: registrations versus active ties

Background

Two ideas sound alike but are different. Network analysis is the method: it reads the shape of the network. A network effect is the economics: each additional user makes the platform more valuable to every other user. It cares about size, not shape. Metcalfe's law prices a network at about n2; Odlyzko and Tilly's correction is n × log n, because most ties are weak or dormant. The manager's version is the part to remember: value tracks active, weighted ties, not registrations. A platform with a million registrations and ten thousand monthly transactions is worth the ten thousand.

Marketplaces show this clearly. Vinted kept listing free for sellers and charged buyers a protection fee; BlaBlaCar was free in France for about six years before charging; Xianyu launched into Taobao's existing graph and never had a cold start. The same idea works from the other side: if registrations double while transactions stay flat, value has not quadrupled. A good answer names an activity measure, such as completed monthly transactions or distinct buyer-seller pairs that traded this month, and adds that mapping those ties answers a structural question a user count cannot: are there disconnected components, is trade concentrated on a few hubs, are some groups cut off?

The short version

"Which account matters most" has three answers. Degree counts ties and measures reach, but it ignores position and can be inflated with bought followers. Betweenness counts the shortest paths that pass through an account; in the corner A003 and A012 score 12, A126 scores 11, A038 scores 6 and the rest score 0, and these brokers are exactly the accounts whose removal breaks the map, which makes them both change agents and single points of failure. Closeness is (n − 1) divided by the distance sum; A003 and A012 tie at 7/13 = 0.538 and A126 has 7/15 = 0.467. On the full 240-account map, A003 leads degree with 34 ties while A126 leads betweenness with only 15 ties, so pick the measure to match the job. A community is denser inside than outside; modularity Q scores a split as inside ties minus what chance would give, Louvain searches for a high Q without being told how many groups to find, and it can merge small groups. In the corner, the ties put A012 with the city side at Q = 0.367 even though its handle says food. Clustering groups by features with k chosen in advance; communities group by ties and find k themselves.

The pattern behind almost every mark

Background

Look at any Part B question in the mock marking guide and the 6 marks split the same way: two points of two marks each, plus a third point of two marks, and each two-mark point splits again into "state it" and "connect it to the scenario". When the question involves data, the points almost always come out as four moves.

  2. What it meansin plain words, tied to the denominator or units
  3. The decisionthe action or choice the number supports
  4. One honest limitspecific to this case, with its consequence

If your answer makes all four moves in three to five sentences, you have usually covered the marking scheme without seeing it. Take the review-queue example. The queue holds 16 + 20 = 36 messages, which is 12 more than the team can check (the number). Raising the lower boundary to 0.50 sends the 16 messages in that band straight to "keep", so the queue falls to 20 and fits the capacity (what it means and the decision). But some of those 16 may be spam, so missed spam could rise, and the table cannot say by how much because it has no true labels (the limit). That is four sentences and all 6 marks. Notice what the guide refuses to reward: calling all 16 messages spam. You only say what the evidence supports.

Part A: how to avoid losing easy marks

Background

Multiple-choice questions in this course rarely ask for obscure facts. They test whether you can apply a definition exactly to a tiny example.
